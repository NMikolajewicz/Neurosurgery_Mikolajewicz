# Cervical vs Thoracic vs Lumbar Vertebrae: Morphology

**Figure:** Schematic of the human spine showing the three mobile regions: cervical (C1–C7), thoracic (T1–T12), and lumbar (L1–L5). Each vertebra has a body (anterior) and posterior elements (pedicles, laminae, processes).

Cervical vertebrae have **small bodies** and **large foramina** (for cord and vertebral arteries).  Typical C3–C6 have bifid spinous processes, C7 has a long “vertebra prominens” spinous. Thoracic vertebrae are intermediate in size with **costal facets** on bodies and transverse processes for rib articulation; their spinous processes are long and angled downward.  Lumbar vertebrae have the **largest, kidney-shaped bodies** supporting body weight; their spinous processes are short and broad.  Facet joints show a caudal-to-rostral trend: cervical facets slope downward to allow flexion/extension; thoracic facets are vertical (favor rotation); lumbar facets are sagittal (favor flexion/extension).

## Biomechanical Properties and Function

* **Motion:** The cervical spine is _most mobile_ of the three regions, allowing ~80° of flexion/extension, ~65° rotation, and ~45° lateral flexion, due to its small bodies and nearly horizontal facets.  Thoracic motion is _most restricted_: flexion/extension is minimal (ribs and long spinous processes limit bending), lateral flexion is modest, but rotation is relatively better (especially T5–T8).  Lumbar motion is intermediate: large flexion/extension (~50° total) and some lateral flexion are possible, but axial rotation is very limited (because nearly sagittal facets).
* **Curvature and Load:** Cervical and lumbar spines exhibit **lordosis** (inward curve), while the thoracic spine shows **kyphosis** (outward curve). Weight-bearing increases caudally: cervical vertebrae bear the head (~4–5 kg), thoracic vertebrae bear ribcage and upper body, and lumbar vertebrae bear the entire torso.  Accordingly, **vertebral body size and disc thickness increase** from C to L.  Lumbar discs are the thickest (supporting maximal load), whereas thoracic disc height is smallest.  The cervical spine’s mobility (and lighter loading) predisposes it to flexibility but also to wear; conversely, the thoracic spine is stabilized by the rib cage and is less prone to degenerative “wear and tear”.
* **Load Distribution:** In neutral posture, the anterior column (vertebral bodies + discs) bears ~80% of compressive load.  The increased cross-sectional area of lumbar vertebrae distributes higher loads; stress is highest at the thoracolumbar junction (T12–L1), a common fracture zone.  Muscle and ligament demands also differ: the cervical region has many small muscles for head control; the thoracic region has rib-attached muscles (respiratory, stability); the lumbar region has large extensor muscles to support torso and pelvis.

## Neuroanatomical Context

* **Spinal Cord and Nerve Roots:** The spinal cord spans C1 to ~L1–L2 vertebral levels. There are 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, and 5 lumbar cord segments (plus sacral/coccygeal). The cord has enlargements at C5 (brachial plexus) and L3 (lumbosacral plexus).  Below ~L1–L2, nerve roots descend as the cauda equina.
* **Exit Points:** Cervical spinal nerves C1–C7 exit _above_ their corresponding vertebrae; C8 exits between C7 and T1.  All thoracic and lumbar nerves exit _below_ their like-numbered vertebra (e.g. T5 nerve below T5 vertebra).  Thus, a disc herniation or foramen stenosis at C5–6 impinges the C6 nerve, whereas at L4–5 it impinges the L5 nerve.
* **Dermatomes/Segments:** Cervical roots form the cervical and brachial plexuses (innervating arms); thoracic roots remain segmental as intercostal nerves; lumbar/sacral roots form the lumbosacral plexus (legs and perineum).
* **Clinical Syndromes:** Cervical pathology often produces **radiculopathy** (neck pain radiating to shoulders/arms, e.g. C6 root → forearm) or **myelopathy** (cord compression causing bilateral hand clumsiness, gait disturbance).  For example, C6–7 spondylosis typically causes C7 radicular pain into the triceps, plus possible hand weakness.  Thoracic nerve root compression is uncommon but can cause band-like chest pain. Thoracic cord lesions (e.g. tumor/stenosis) may cause **paraparesis or sensory level**.  Lumbar pathology causes **sciatica** (low back pain radiating to legs). Lumbar central stenosis or large herniation can produce **cauda equina syndrome** (saddle anesthesia, bowel/bladder loss).  Notably, cervical stenosis leads to cord (upper motor neuron) signs, whereas lumbar stenosis affects nerve roots (lower motor neuron signs).  Cadaveric and imaging data show the cord ends at L1–L2, so lumbar canal stenosis spares the cord but may injure cauda equina.

## Region-Specific Pathologies

* **Cervical Spine:**  Common conditions include _spondylotic degeneration_ with osteophytes and disc bulges (cervical spondylosis), often at C5–6/C6–7, causing radiculopathy or myelopathy.  _Disc herniations_ are frequent at C5–6 and C6–7 (nerve root compression to arms). _Stenosis_ of the canal (degenerative or congenital) causes **cervical myelopathy** (hand clumsiness, gait issues).  Traumatic fractures are serious: C1 “Jefferson” fractures (burst atlas), odontoid (C2 dens fractures), and C2 pedicle (“Hangman’s”) fractures are classic.  _Atlantoaxial instability_ (e.g. in rheumatoid arthritis) can compress the cord. Ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL) is a degenerative cause of cervical myelopathy.
* **Thoracic Spine:**  Degenerative disc disease and herniations are **rare** in the thoracic region (estimated ~1 per million per year), but when they occur often at T11–T12.  Thoracic _disc herniation_ can cause radicular back/abdomen pain or myelopathy if large.  _Spinal stenosis_ may result from ossified ligaments (e.g. ossified ligamentum flavum) or tumors (meningiomas often occur in thoracic canal).  _Compression fractures_ (osteoporotic wedge fractures) are common in thoracic vertebrae, especially T7–T8, leading to kyphotic deformity.  Scheuermann’s kyphosis (juvenile vertebral wedging) typically affects mid-thoracic levels, causing rigid kyphosis in adolescents.  Trauma (e.g. MVA) often causes burst fractures at the thoracolumbar junction (T12–L1).  Thoracic nerve root impingement causes mid‐back or chest wall pain in the corresponding dermatome.
* **Lumbar Spine:**  **Disc herniations** are very common, especially L4–L5 and L5–S1, producing sciatica (leg pain, foot drop).  **Degenerative spondylosis** and facet arthropathy are frequent, sometimes leading to spinal stenosis.  Lumbar **spinal stenosis** causes neurogenic claudication (leg pain on walking) and is often exacerbated by facet hypertrophy or ligamentum flavum thickening.  **Spondylolysis/spondylolisthesis**: a pars interarticularis defect (L5 most common) can slip (often L4–L5).  **Fractures:** osteoporotic compression fractures occur at L1–L2; high-energy trauma causes burst fractures at L1.  **Cauda Equina Syndrome:** large central herniations or stenosis can compress the cauda equina, causing saddle anesthesia and bladder/bowel loss (a neurosurgical emergency).  Degenerative lumbar scoliosis can also develop in elderly.  Lumbar radiculopathy causes numbness/weakness in leg dermatomes (e.g. L5 nerve → dorsum of foot).

## Surgical Considerations

* **Cervical Spine Surgery:**  Common anterior approaches (Smith–Robinson) allow discectomy or corpectomy (e.g. ACDF, ACCF) for radiculopathy or myelopathy. Posterior approaches (laminectomy, laminoplasty, lateral mass or pedicle fixation) address multilevel stenosis or instability.  Instrumentation includes anterior plating and cages or posterior lateral-mass/pedicle screws. Pedicle screws at C2 and C7 are used (C7 pedicle often larger than lateral mass).  Risks: the vertebral artery courses through C6–C1 transverse foramina, so lateral mass/pedicle screw placement carries risk of vertebral artery injury.  Spinal cord monitoring (SSEPs/MEPs) is standard to prevent neurological injury.  C1–2 fixation often uses posterior C1 lateral mass and C2 pedicle screws, requiring careful angles (avoid VA injury).  Atlantoaxial instrumentation must account for vertebral artery anomalies.
* **Thoracic Spine Surgery:**  Approaches depend on pathology. Posterior laminectomy (and fusion) is used for dorsal pathology (e.g. OLF, trauma). Ventral lesions (disc, tumor) often require transthoracic (open or thoracoscopic) or costotransversectomy approaches. Instrumentation is usually via pedicle screws and rods spanning multiple levels. Thoracic pedicles are **small** (especially T1–T4) and close to the aorta/pleura, so screw placement can be challenging.  Intraoperative navigation or neuromonitoring is often used due to the narrow canal and risk to the spinal cord.  Neuromonitoring (SSEPs/MEPs) is **highly recommended** whenever the cord is at risk. Rib resection may be needed to decompress or access anteriorly.
* **Lumbar Spine Surgery:**  Posterior approaches (laminotomy, TLIF, PLIF) are common for decompression and fusion. Anterior approaches (ALIF) or lateral (LLIF/XLIF) approaches avoid neural elements but require vascular mobilization.  Instrumentation uses large pedicle screws and interbody cages. At L5–S1, pelvic fixation or iliac bolts may be added for long constructs. Vascular injury is the major concern in anterior lumbar surgery: the left common iliac vein lies over L4–L5/L5–S1 and is the vessel most often injured in ALIF. In fact, significant venous injuries occur in ~2–4% of ALIF cases. Careful retraction of iliac vessels is mandatory.  The lumbar cord is absent, so true spinal cord monitoring is not needed; however, triggered EMG can help verify safe pedicle screw placement, and nerve-root SSEPs/EMG monitor nerve integrity. During deformity or intradural tumor surgery, SSEPs/MEPs are employed to protect the cord and cauda.
* **Instrumentation Challenges:**  In summary, cervical surgery is constrained by small bones and the proximity of the vertebral artery (risk ~0.1–1% per screw). Thoracic fixation must account for kyphosis alignment, small pedicles, and lung/vascular anatomy.  Lumbar constructs handle the greatest loads, so attention to lumbosacral alignment and robust screw fixation is key.  In all regions, intraoperative neuromonitoring (multimodal IONM) is recommended for high-risk cases to detect impending injury.
