> [!summary]  
> **Refeeding syndrome = metabolic and clinical deterioration after reintroducing calories (esp. carbs) to a significantly malnourished patient**, driven by insulin surge → cellular uptake of **phosphate, potassium, magnesium**, thiamine depletion, and sodium–water retention.  
> Onset: typically **within 5 days** of starting or substantially increasing nutrition.

---

## 1. Definition

- Constellation of:
    - **Hypophosphatemia ± hypokalemia ± hypomagnesemia**
    - **Thiamine deficiency**
    - **Sodium and fluid retention**
- Triggered by reintroduction or rapid escalation of **oral, enteral, or parenteral nutrition** after prolonged undernutrition or starvation.
- Leads to **organ dysfunction** – primarily **cardiac, respiratory, neurologic**.

---

## 2. Types & Classifications

### 2.1 ASPEN 2020 – _Severity of Actual RFS_ (biochemical ± clinical)

|Severity|Criteria (within 5 days of starting/increasing calories)|
|---|---|
|**Mild**|↓ in P/K/Mg by **10–20%** from baseline|
|**Moderate**|↓ in P/K/Mg by **20–30%**|
|**Severe**|↓ in P/K/Mg by **>30%** **and/or** new organ dysfunction attributable to these shifts and/or thiamine deficiency|

---

### 2.2 Clinical Status Categories

- **At-risk (no biochemical drop yet)**
    - Meets risk criteria (low BMI, weight loss, minimal intake, low baseline electrolytes, alcoholism, chemo, diuretics, ICU, elderly, etc.).
    - **No significant fall** in P/K/Mg yet after feeding.
- **Biochemical RFS**
    - Characteristic **P/K/Mg falls meeting ASPEN cut-offs**.
    - Few or no overt symptoms/organ failure.
- **Clinically overt RFS**
    - Biochemical changes **plus** organ dysfunction:
        - Arrhythmias, heart failure
        - Respiratory failure / weaning difficulty
        - Delirium, seizures, Wernicke's
        - Ileus, rhabdo, etc.

---

### 2.3 Historical / Lab-Based Terminology

- **Refeeding hypophosphatemia**
    - Older literature used isolated hypophos (e.g., P <0.65–0.8 mmol/L) as proxy for RFS.
    - Now understood as **one component**, not the full syndrome.

---

### 2.4 By Setting / Route of Feeding

- **Oral / Enteral RFS**
    - Medical wards, ED, stroke, post-op, oncology, eating-disorder units.
- **Parenteral-Nutrition–Associated RFS**
    - High incidence with aggressive TPN and inadequate electrolyte support.
    - Adult TPN cohorts: RFS ≈ **20–45%** in high-risk groups.
- **ICU / Critically Ill (adult & pediatric)**
    - Incidence commonly **7–36%+**, depending on definition and case mix.

---

## 3. Symptoms & Clinical Features

### 3.1 Metabolic Hallmarks

- **Electrolytes**
    - ↓ **Phosphate** (most characteristic)
    - ↓ **Potassium**
    - ↓ **Magnesium**
- **Other**
    - Thiamine deficiency
    - Hyperglycemia
    - Metabolic acidosis/alkalosis
    - Sodium and fluid retention (edema, CHF)

---

### 3.2 System-Specific Manifestations

**Neuromuscular**

- Weakness, myalgia, paresthesias
- Tremor, tetany, hyporeflexia, paralysis
- Rhabdomyolysis

**Respiratory**

- Diaphragmatic and respiratory muscle weakness
- ↓ Vital capacity
- **Acute respiratory failure**
- Difficulty weaning from ventilator

**Cardiac**

- Sinus tachycardia or bradycardia
- **Arrhythmias** (incl. torsades with low Mg/K)
- Cardiomyopathy, acute LV failure
- Hypotension, shock, fluid overload/CHF

**Neurologic / Psychiatric**

- Irritability, agitation, delirium
- Lethargy, confusion, coma
- Seizures
- Wernicke's encephalopathy (ataxia, ophthalmoplegia, confusion)

**GI / Hepatic**

- Nausea, vomiting
- Abdominal pain
- Ileus/constipation
- Transaminitis, hepatic steatosis

**Hematologic / Immune / Other**

- Hemolysis
- Impaired leukocyte function, ↑ infection risk
- Peripheral edema, anasarca
- Exacerbation of existing cardiac/renal disease

## 4. Epidemiology & High-Risk Groups

> [!note] Wide range due to varying definitions (hypophos vs ASPEN vs composite).  
> Reported incidences overall: **~1.5–>60%** in at-risk cohorts.

### 4.1 Rough Rank Order of Common Clinical Contexts

1. **Older malnourished general medical inpatients**
    - Tube-fed, high-risk elderly (BMI ~18.5):
        - RFS incidence **12.9%** (traditional hypophos) → **65.9%** (ASPEN-based) depending on criteria.
            
2. **Critically ill adults (incl. COVID-19 ICU)**
    - Up to **82% at risk**, **≈36% with RFS** using ASPEN definitions in some COVID-ICU cohorts.
        
3. **Patients on TPN**
    - Tertiary-centre adult TPN cohorts:
        - RFS ≈ **20–45%** when energy is advanced quickly and electrolytes are not optimized.
            
4. **Internal-medicine inpatients on enteral feeding**
    - Commonly **~14–34%** with hypophosor composite-based criteria.
        
5. **Children with severe acute malnutrition (SAM)**
    - Hospitalized SAM: RFS **~7–21%**, higher with HIV and aggressive feeding.
        
6. **Eating-disorder / anorexia nervosa inpatients**
    - Clinically significant RFS (ASPEN) in a **minority (~5–20%)**, but many meet "at-risk" criteria.
        
7. **Other high-risk groups**
    - Oncology (chemo, HSCT)
    - Chronic alcoholism
    - Uncontrolled diabetes
    - Post-bariatric or major GI surgery
    - Frail LTC residents

## 5. Diagnosis

### 5.1 Identify High-Risk Patients (NICE + ASPEN Core Elements)

**High risk if any ONE of:**

- BMI **<16 kg/m²**
- Unintentional weight loss **>15%** in 3–6 months
- Little/no intake for **>10 days**
- Low baseline **P/K/Mg**

**OR any TWO of:**

- BMI **<18.5 kg/m²**
- Unintentional weight loss **>10%** in 3–6 months
- Little/no intake for **>5 days**
- History of **alcohol misuse** or use of:
    - Insulin
    - Chemotherapy
    - Antacids
    - Diuretics

**ASPEN** adds:

- Clinical evidence of fat/muscle loss
- Comorbidities (oncology, geriatrics, ICU, anorexia, chronic GI disease, etc.)

---

### 5.2 Biochemical Criteria for "Actual" RFS (ASPEN 2020)

Within **5 days** of starting or substantially increasing calories:

- Fall in **serum phosphate, potassium, and/or magnesium**:
    - Mild: ↓ **10–20%**
    - Moderate: ↓ **20–30%**
    - Severe: ↓ **>30%**
- **AND/OR** new organ dysfunction attributable to:
    - These electrolyte shifts, and/or
    - Thiamine deficiency

---

### 5.3 Monitoring & Workup

- **Baseline + daily (or more frequent) labs** for at least 3–5 days:
    - P, K, Mg, Ca
    - Glucose
    - Renal & liver function
- **Clinical monitoring**
    - Vitals, strict I/O, daily weights
    - Cardiorespiratory status, neuro status
    - **ECG** for arrhythmias (especially if K/Mg low)
- **Differential for electrolyte changes**
    - Diuretics, diarrhea, renal losses
    - DKA treatment
    - Tumor lysis
    - Other causes of hypophos/K/Mg

---

## 6. Treatment & Prevention

> [!danger] Core principle  
> **Prevent, then treat**: start low, go slow, aggressively replace electrolytes, give thiamine, and watch closely.

### 6.1 Before / At Start of Feeding

- **Risk stratify** using NICE/ASPEN.
- **Thiamine**
    - Start **before and during** early feeding.
    - Common adult practice: **100–300 mg/day IV or PO** for ≥3–5 days (higher in suspected Wernicke's, locally variable).
- **Correct / cover electrolytes**
    - Check P, K, Mg pre-feeding and replete.
    - For significant deficits or ICU patients: **IV replacement** per local protocols.
- Consider **multivitamin** supplementation.

---

### 6.2 Caloric Strategy (Adults – adjust to local protocol)

- **Moderate risk**
    - Start ≈ **10–20 kcal/kg/day** or ~20–25% of goal.
    - Increase by **10–20% of goal/day** over 4–7 days if electrolytes stable.
- **Very high risk** (e.g., BMI ≤14, negligible intake ≥2 weeks)
    - Start as low as **~5–10 kcal/kg/day**.
    - Very slow escalation with close monitoring.
    - Continuous cardiac monitoring often recommended.
- **General**
    - Avoid large, rapid carbohydrate loads.
    - Limit initial glucose infusion rates.
    - Consider protein-forward, lower-carb distribution early on if feasible.

---

### 6.3 Electrolyte & Fluid Management

- **Monitoring**
    - P/K/Mg: **q6–24 h** early on, depending on risk/setting.
- **Replacement**
    - **IV phosphate** and **IV magnesium** commonly used in ICU.
    - Oral supplements where feasible and mild.
    - Aim to maintain:
        - K often **>4.0 mmol/L**
        - Mg **>0.8–1.0 mmol/L**
        - Phos within normal or low-normal range per local targets.
- **Fluids**
    - Avoid excessive sodium load.
    - Cautious IV fluids in patients with CHF/CKD.
- **If RFS develops**
    - Slow or pause **caloric escalation**.
    - Aggressively correct electrolytes.
    - Treat organ dysfunction (arrhythmias, HF, respiratory failure, etc.).

---

### 6.4 Management of Overt / Severe RFS

- **ICU-level care**
    - Continuous ECG, frequent labs, possible invasive monitoring.
- **Thiamine**
    - Continue, often at **higher doses** for suspected Wernicke's.
- **Multidisciplinary approach**
    - Nutrition support team
    - ICU / internal medicine
    - Pharmacy

---

## 7. Prognosis & Outcomes

### 7.1 General Themes

- RFS is associated with **higher shortand long-term mortality**, but:
    - Mortality is often driven by **underlying illness** plus the added burden of RFS.
- If **recognized early** and managed with:
    - Low initial calories
    - Close electrolyte & thiamine management  
        → most biochemical RFS episodes are **reversible** with good recovery.

---

### 7.2 Illustrative Outcome Data

- **Medical inpatients with malnutrition**
    - Confirmed RFS:
        - **~29.8% 180-day mortality** vs **~21.9%** without RFS.
- **Older high-risk tube-fed patients**
    - Overall mortality:
        - **~36.5% at 3 months**, **~56.5% at 1 year**.
    - Patients meeting stricter RFS criteria:
        - 1-year mortality up to **~73%** vs ~54% without RFS  
            (not always statistically significant, but clinically concerning).
            
- **Critically ill COVID-19 ICU cohorts**
    - RFS incidence ≈ **36%**.
    - Hospital mortality ≈ **48%**, highest in the RFS subgroup.
- **RFS risk categories (critically ill)**
    - 30-day mortality rises with risk:
        - No risk ~**5%**
        - Low risk ~**7%**
        - High risk ~**16%**
        - Very high risk ~**27%**
- **Systematic reviews**
    - RFS prevalence **1.5–88%** in various cohorts.
    - RFS typically confers **~1.2–1.7× increased mortality risk.**

> [!tip] Practical takeaway
> 
> - **Screen** all malnourished / high-risk patients before feeding.
> 
> - **Start low, go slow** with calories.
> 
> - **Daily (or more frequent) P/K/Mg** for the first 3–5 days.
> 
> - **Thiamine early** and **electrolytes aggressively**.
> 
> - Escalate level of care quickly if organ dysfunction emerges.
