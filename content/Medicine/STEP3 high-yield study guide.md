## Cardiology
#cardiology 
#### ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction (STEMI) 
#cardiology/STEMI 
- Presents with crushing substernal chest pain (often radiating to left arm or jaw), diaphoresis, and dyspnea. ECG shows ST elevations in contiguous leads (or new LBBB); troponin is elevated. 
- Management: Give aspirin immediately (chew 162–325 mg) [aafp.org](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2009/0615/p1080.html#:~:text=Clinical%20recommendation%20ACC%2FAHA%20LOE,if%20there%20are%20no%20contraindications), oxygen if low O₂ sat, sublingual nitroglycerin, and morphine for pain. Perform urgent reperfusion therapy (percutaneous coronary intervention within 90 minutes is preferred) [aafp.org](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2009/0615/p1080.html#:~:text=STEMI%20is%20defined%20as%20ST,in%20less%20than%2090%20minutes); use thrombolytics if PCI not available within 120 minutes and no contraindications.
#### Non-ST-Elevation ACS (NSTEMI/Unstable Angina)
#cardiology/NSTEMI
- Chest pain without ST elevations on ECG; NSTEMI has elevated troponin, UA does not. 
- Management: Admit for monitoring, anti-ischemic therapy (nitroglycerin, β-blocker), and anticoagulation (heparin). Start dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin + P2Y12 inhibitor). No immediate thrombolytics; proceed to early cardiac catheterization for NSTEMI based on risk.
#### Heart Failure (HFrEF)
#cardiology/HFrEF
- Systolic dysfunction (EF <40%) causing exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, PND, and edema. Exam may show S₃ gallop and pulmonary crackles. 
- Management: ACE inhibitors (or ARNI) and β-blockers improve mortality (first-line); add loop diuretics for symptom relief
#### Atrial Fibrillation (AF)
#cardiology/afib
- Irregularly irregular rhythm, absent P waves on ECG; may cause palpitations or stroke. Management:Rate control with β-blocker or diltiazem (first-line for stable AF)
	[aafp.org](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2011/0101/p61.html#:~:text=Beta%20blockers%20%28e,line%20agents). Assess stroke risk (CHA₂DS₂-VASc score) – if ≥2 in men or ≥3 in women, anticoagulate with a DOAC or warfarin to prevent stroke. Unstable AF with rapid ventricular response requires immediate synchronized cardioversion.
#### Infective Endocarditis
#cardiology/IE
- Fever, new murmur, and positive blood cultures in a patient with risk factors (IV drug use, valvular disease). Signs can include Janeway lesions, Osler nodes, Roth spots. Diagnosis:Transesophageal echocardiogram to visualize vegetations. 
- Treatment: IV antibiotics tailored to organism (empiric vancomycinto cover Staph aureus while awaiting cultures) [aafp.org](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2009/0615/p1080.html#:~:text=elevation%20myocardial%20infarction,Secondary%20prevention%20interventions%20are%20critically)	[aafp.org](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2009/0615/p1080.html#:~:text=they%20may%20increase%20the%20risk,events%20in%20patients%20who%20survive). Prosthetic valve endocarditis or complications (abscess, heart failure) may require surgery.
#### Acute Pericarditis
#cardiology/pericarditis
- Sharp pleuritic chest pain that improves when sitting up and leaning forward; often post-viral. ECG shows diffuse ST elevations and PR depressions. 
- Treatment: NSAIDs (e.g. high-dose ibuprofen) are first-line; add colchicine to reduce recurrence. Avoid corticosteroids unless refractory. Monitor for complications like pericardial effusion.
#### Cardiac Tamponade
#cardiology/cardiac_tamponade
- Beck’s triad: hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds (often from trauma or pericarditis). Pulsus paradoxus may be present (SBP drops >10 mmHg on inspiration). 
- Diagnosis: Echocardiogram shows pericardial effusion with diastolic collapse of chambers. Management:Urgent pericardiocentesis to relieve pressure. IV fluids can temporize while preparing for drainage.
#### Aortic Stenosis
#cardiology/aortic_stenosis
- Usually in older patients (often calcific); triad of syncope, angina, dyspnea. Harsh systolic ejection murmur at right 2nd ICS, radiating to carotids; pulsus parvus et tardus (weak, delayed carotid upstroke). 
- Management: Surgical aortic valve replacement is definitive for symptomatic severe AS. 
#### Mitral Regurgitation
#cardiology/mitral_regurgitation
- Holosystolic murmur radiating to axilla; treat underlying cause (repair if severe). 
#### Mitral Stenosis
#cardiology/mitral_stenosis
- Often rheumatic; diastolic rumbling murmur with opening snap; treat with balloon valvuloplasty or commissurotomy if severe.
#### Hypertrophic Obstructive Cardiomyopathy (HOCM)
#cardiology/HOCM
- Young patient with exertional syncope or sudden death risk. Harsh crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur (louder with Valsalva, quieter with squatting). 
- Management: β-blockers are first-line (improve filling time and reduce obstruction); advise avoidance of dehydration and extreme exertion. Septal myectomy or alcohol ablation is considered for refractory cases.
#### Hypertension
#cardiology/hypertension
- Usually asymptomatic; leads to risks of stroke, CAD, CKD. 
- Initial management: lifestyle modifications (diet, exercise, weight loss, low salt). 
- First-line medications include thiazide diuretics, ACE inhibitors (especially if diabetic or CKD), or calcium channel blockers (for black patients, CCB or thiazide preferred). Goal BP < 130/80 mmHg in most patients. Hypertensive emergency (BP >180/120 with end-organ damage) requires IV nitroprusside, labetalol, or other appropriate IV meds; hypertensive urgency can be managed with oral meds.
## Pulmonology
#respiratory 
#### Asthma
#respiratory/asthma
- Reversible airway hyperreactivity causing episodes of wheezing, cough, and dyspnea (often worse at night or with triggers). 
- Diagnosis: PFTs show reversible obstruction (FEV₁ improves >12% with bronchodilator). 
- Acute exacerbation: Treat with high-flow oxygen, short-acting β₂ agonist nebulizers (albuterol), anticholinergic nebulizer (ipratropium), and systemic corticosteroids. 
- Chronic management: Inhaled corticosteroids for persistent asthma, plus as-needed SABA; add LABA or montelukast for moderate-to-severe asthma.
#### Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD)
#respiratory/COPD 
- Usually from smoking (emphysema and chronic bronchitis). Presents with chronic cough, sputum, and dyspnea; barrel chest and prolonged exhalation on exam. 
- Diagnosis: PFTs show poorly reversible airflow limitation (FEV₁/FVC <70% after bronchodilator). 
- Management: Smoking cessation is most important. Inhaled bronchodilators (ipatropium, tiotropium ± albuterol) are first-line for symptom control; add inhaled steroids for frequent exacerbations. Provide home O₂ therapy if chronic hypoxemia (SaO₂ ≤88%). Acute exacerbation: give supplemental oxygen (target 88–92% O₂ sat), bronchodilators, systemic steroids, and antibiotics if infection suspected.
#### Pulmonary Embolism (PE)
#respiratory/PE #hematology/PE
- Presents with acute pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea, tachycardia; may have hemoptysis or leg DVT signs. 
- Diagnosis: If low probability, get D-dimer to rule out; if high suspicion or D-dimer positive, do CT pulmonary angiography (V/Q scan if CT contraindicated). 
- Treatment: Immediate anticoagulation (IV heparin or LMWH, then transition to oral anticoagulant like apixaban or warfarin) – this reduces mortality [emedicine.medscape.com](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/300901-medication#:~:text=Pulmonary%20Embolism%20%28PE%29%20Medication%20,essential%2C%20but%20anticoagulation%20alone). Massive PE with hemodynamic collapse warrants thrombolysis. Place an IVC filter if anticoagulation is contraindicated.
#### Pneumonia
#respiratory/pneumonia #infectious-disease/pneumonia #CAP #HAP
- Community-acquired: Fever, productive cough, pleuritic pain, and lobar consolidation on CXR (classically S. pneumoniae). 
	- Management: Outpatient – azithromycin or doxycycline (if healthy); if comorbid, use respiratory fluoroquinolone or β-lactam + macrolide. Inpatient (non-ICU) – IV ceftriaxone + azithromycin, or IV fluoroquinolone. 
- Hospital-acquired:Cover Pseudomonas/MRSA (e.g. pip-tazo + vancomycin). 
- Atypical pneumonia: Dry cough, patchy interstitial infiltrates (Mycoplasma, Chlamydia) – treat with macrolides. Ensure pneumococcal vaccination for prevention in elderly or at-risk patients.
#### Tuberculosis (TB)
#infectious-disease/TB #respiratory/TB
- Risk factors: endemic area, immunosuppression. Chronic cough, night sweats, weight loss, hemoptysis in active TB. 
- Diagnosis: Screen with TST or IGRA; confirm active TB with sputum AFB cultures/NAAT and chest X-ray (upper lobe cavitary lesions classic). 
- Treatment: RIPE therapy for active TB – Rifampin, Isoniazid (with B6), Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol for 2 months, then RI for 4 more months (total 6 months). Latent TB (positive test, no symptoms/clear CXR) is treated with isoniazid for 9 months or rifampin for 4 months to prevent reactivation.'
#### Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)
#respiratory/ARDS
- Acute onset respiratory failure with refractory hypoxemia, bilateral infiltrates on CXR, and PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio ≤300 (despite PEEP). Often due to sepsis, pancreatitis, severe pneumonia, or trauma. 
- Management: Treat underlying cause. Use low tidal volume mechanical ventilation (6 mL/kg) to reduce barotrauma and apply adequate PEEP to improve oxygenation. Goal is to maintain low plateau pressures and oxygenate while avoiding oxygen toxicity. Prone positioning can improve oxygenation in severe ARDS.
#### Pneumothorax
#respiratory/pneumothorax
- Spontaneous PTX: Sudden unilateral pleuritic chest pain and dyspnea in a young thin male (primary) or COPD patient (secondary); exam shows absent breath sounds on one side, hyperresonance. 
	- Management: Small PTX (<15% lung) can be observed with oxygen; larger requires chest tube. 
- Tension PTX: Life-threatening – tracheal deviation away, hypotension, distended neck veins
	- Management: Immediate needle decompression (2nd ICS) followed by chest tube placement (do not wait for imaging)
#### Sarcoidosis
#respiratory/sarcoidosis
- Young African American with cough, dyspnea, and bilateral hilar lymphadenopathyon CXR. Noncaseating granulomas in multiple organs; may have erythema nodosum, uveitis, and elevated ACE levels. 
- Diagnosis: Biopsy of affected lymph node or lung tissue confirms noncaseating granulomas. 
- Management:Many cases asymptomatic and resolve; if symptomatic (lungs or hypercalcemia), treat with corticosteroids.
## Gastroenterology
#gastroenterology 
#### Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)
#gastroenterology/PUD
- Epigastric burning pain related to meals (duodenal ulcers improve with eating, gastric ulcers worsen). Most commonly caused by H. pylori infection or NSAIDs. 
- Diagnosis: Endoscopy with biopsy for gastric ulcers (to rule out malignancy); noninvasive H. pylori tests (urea breath or stool antigen). 
- Treatment: If H. pylori positive, eradicate with triple therapy (PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin for 14 days) or quadruple therapy. All PUD patients get acid suppression (PPI) [aafp.org](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2009/0615/p1080.html#:~:text=elevation%20myocardial%20infarction,Secondary%20prevention%20interventions%20are%20critically) and should avoid NSAIDs.
#### Upper GI Bleed
#gastroenterology/UGIB
- Hematemesis or melena usually from peptic ulcers, varices, or Mallory–Weiss tear. 
- Management: Stabilize first – ABCs, IV fluids, IV proton pump inhibitor, and type & cross for possible transfusion. If variceal bleed suspected (cirrhotic with hematemesis), give IV octreotide and antibiotics. Perform urgent endoscopy to locate bleed and treat (epinephrine injection, banding of varices, or cautery). Monitor hemoglobin; transfuse packed RBCs for significant anemia (Hb <7 in most, <9 if cardiac disease).
#### Acute Pancreatitis
#gastroenterology/pancreatitis
- Severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, nausea/vomiting; often due to gallstones or chronic alcohol use. Serum amylase and lipase are elevated (>3× normal).
- Management: Supportive care – aggressive IV fluids is the initial priority [aafp.org](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/1999/0801/p455.html#:~:text=The%20initial%20priority%20in%20the,volume%2C%20decrease%20counterregulatory%20hormones%20and), NPO bowel rest, and pain control (IV opioids). Most cases are mild and self-limited. Monitor for complications (necrosis, pseudocyst). If due to gallstones, perform cholecystectomy after recovery.
#### Acute Cholecystitis
#gastroenterology/acute_cholecystitis
- RUQ pain (often postprandial), fever, positive Murphy’s sign (inspiratory pause on RUQ palpation). Usually from a gallstone obstructing the cystic duct with secondary infection. 
- Diagnosis: RUQ ultrasound (gallstones, wall thickening, pericholecystic fluid); HIDA scan if uncertain. 
- Treatment: Admit for IV antibiotics (coverage for Gram-negatives), IV fluids, analgesia. Perform cholecystectomy during same hospitalization (usually within 48 hours once stabilized).
#### Ascending Cholangitis
#gastroenterology/ascending_cholangitis
- Bacterial infection due to common bile duct obstruction (typically stone). Charcot’s triad: fever, RUQ pain, jaundice (Reynolds pentad adds hypotension and confusion). 
- Management: This is an emergency – start IV broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g. ciprofloxacin + metronidazole, or pip-tazo), and perform urgent ERCP to decompress and remove obstruction. After resolution, do elective cholecystectomy to prevent recurrence.
#### Diverticulitis
#gastroenterology/diverticulitis
- LLQ abdominal pain, fever, and leukocytosis in an older adult; can have bowel habit changes or occult blood in stool. Caused by inflammation or microperforation of colonic diverticulum (usually sigmoid). 
- Diagnosis: Abdominal CT scan (wall thickening, fat stranding, ± abscess). 
- Treatment: Bowel rest (clear liquids or NPO) and antibiotics that cover Gram-negatives/anaerobes (e.g. ciprofloxacin + metronidazole). Complications like abscess, perforation, or peritonitis may require percutaneous drainage or surgery.
#### Appendicitis
#gastroenterology/appendicitis
- Periumbilical pain that localizes to RLQ (McBurney’s point), with fever, nausea, and leukocytosis. Often due to a fecalith obstruction. 
- Diagnosis: Primarily clinical; abdominal ultrasound is useful in children, CT scan confirms in adults. 
- Management: Prompt surgical appendectomy (to prevent rupture); give IV fluids and broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g. ertapenem or cefoxitin) pre-op.
#### Ulcerative Colitis (UC)
#gastroenterology/IBD/ulcerative_colitis
- Chronic inflammation of colon starting in rectum and continuously spreading proximally. Presents with bloody diarrhea and abdominal pain. Associated with toxic megacolon and colorectal cancer. 
- Diagnosis: Colonoscopy with continuous colitis (pseudo-polyps) and biopsy (crypt abscesses). 
- Treatment: 5-ASA agents (mesalamine) for mild disease; steroids for flares; immunomodulators or biologics for refractory disease. Colectomy is curative and indicated for dysplasia or severe disease.
#### Crohn Disease
#gastroenterology/IBD/crohn_disease
- Transmural inflammation anywhere in GI tract (skip lesions; commonly ileum), causing diarrhea (often non-bloody), abdominal pain, weight loss, fistulas, and strictures. Endoscopy shows cobblestoning and skip lesions; biopsy with granulomas. 
- Treatment: Corticosteroids for acute flares; immunosuppressants (azathioprine, TNF-inhibitors like infliximab) for maintenance in moderate-severe disease. Unlike UC, surgery is not curative (reserved for complications).
#### Cirrhosis
#gastroenterology/cirrhosis #hepatic/cirrhosis
- End-stage chronic liver disease (e.g. from chronic hepatitis or alcoholism). Clinical features include ascites(abdominal fluid wave, shifting dullness), portal hypertension(esophageal varices, caput medusae, splenomegaly), hepatic encephalopathy(confusion, asterixis from high ammonia), and coagulopathy. 
- Management: Treat complications – ascites with salt restriction and diuretics (spironolactone ± furosemide), large volume paracentesis if tense ascites; nonselective β-blockers (propranolol) to prevent variceal bleeding; variceal hemorrhage treated with banding and octreotide. 
- Encephalopathy: give lactulose (traps ammonia in colon) ± rifaximin. All cirrhotics need surveillance for hepatocellular carcinoma (ultrasound every 6 months) and endoscopy for varices. Ultimately, liver transplant is definitive for decompensated cirrhosis.
#### Hepatitis
#gastroenterology/hepatitis #hepatic/hepatitis
- Acute Hepatitis
	- Hep A: fecal-oral, self-limited; 
	- Hep B: sexually or via blood, can become chronic; 
	- Hep C: primarily blood (IV drugs), high rate chronicity. Acute viral hepatitis presents with fatigue, anorexia, RUQ discomfort, jaundice, and elevated ALT/AST (often >1000). 
	- Management: Mostly supportive in acute hepatitis. 
- Chronic Hepatitis 
	- Hep B with active replication is treated with antivirals (e.g. tenofovir or entecavir). 
	- Hep C: treat all chronic infections with direct-acting antivirals (e.g. sofosbuvir-ledipasvir) which achieve >95% cure rates. 
	- Prevent hepatitis with vaccination (A and B vaccines available; no C vaccine).
#### Hepatic Encephalopathy
#gastroenterology/hepatic_encephalopathy #hepatic/hepatic_encephalopathy
- Reversible neuropsychiatric syndrome in advanced liver disease (triggered by high ammonia, often from GI bleed or dehydration). Patient has confusion, asterixis (flapping tremor). 
- Treatment: Lactulose is first-line (metabolized by gut flora into acids that trap ammonia in stool) and rifaximin (antibiotic that reduces ammonia-producing bacteria) for recurrent cases. Also correct precipitating factors (e.g. GI bleed, electrolyte imbalance).
## Neurology
#neurology 
#### Ischemic Stroke (CVA)
#neurology/ischemic_stroke  #neurology/TPA #neurology/ETV
- Sudden onset focal neurologic deficit (weakness, aphasia, vision loss, etc.) from arterial blockage. 
- Initial management: Get non-contrast CT head immediately_to rule out hemorrhage. 
	- If ischemic and within 3–4.5 hour window, administer IV tPA (alteplase) provided no contraindications [jamanetwork.com](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2806532#:~:text=,5%20hours%20of%20symptom%20onset). 
	- If outside tPA window or contraindicated, give aspirin early (within 24-48h) [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3278065/#:~:text=Stroke%20pmc,325%20mg%20over%20therapeutic). 
	- For large vessel occlusions (e.g. proximal MCA) up to 24h, consider mechanical thrombectomy. 
- Post-acute: Start high-intensity statin, antiplatelet (aspirin ± clopidogrel), and control risk factors (BP, diabetes, smoking) to prevent recurrence.
#### Hemorrhagic Stroke
#ICH #neurology/hemorrhagic_stroke #charchot-bouchard-aneurysms
- Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), often due to hypertension (Charcot-Bouchard aneurysms), presents with focal deficit plus headache, vomiting, and higher_blood pressure. 
- Management:Blood pressure control (target SBP ~140), reverse any coagulopathy (e.g. give vitamin K and PCC for warfarin), and neurosurgery consult for possible evacuation (especially cerebellar hemorrhages or large lobar hemorrhages with mass effect). 
#### Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH)
#SAH
- “Worst headache of life” (thunderclap headache), often from ruptured berry aneurysm; may have nuchal rigidity. Confirm by CT head (or LP showing xanthochromia if CT neg). 
- Management: neurosurgical evaluation for aneurysm repair (coiling or clipping) and give nimodipine(to prevent cerebral vasospasm).
#### Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA)
#neurology/TIA
Transient stroke symptoms that resolve fully within minutes to hours (<24h). Signifies high risk of future stroke. 
Workup: carotid ultrasound for stenosis, cardiac eval for embolic source (echo, ECG for afib). 
Management: Start aspirin (or aspirin + dipyridamole) for all non-cardioembolic TIAs; add high-intensity statin. If carotid stenosis ≥70%, refer for carotid endarterectomy. Control risk factors (BP, glucose, smoking, atrial fibrillation anticoagulation if cardioembolic source).
#### Seizures and Epilepsy
#neurology/seizure #neurology/epilepsy #keppra #phenytoin #valproate #abscence-seizure #ethosuximide #status-epilepticus
- Generalized tonic-clonic seizure:loss of consciousness with diffuse muscle contractions then jerking; postictal confusion. First unprovoked seizure warrants workup (electrolytes, glucose, toxicology, EEG, and brain imaging) but not always long-term therapy. 
- Epilepsy management: Levetiracetam, phenytoin, valproate, or lamotrigine are common first-line anti-epileptics for generalized seizures (avoid valproate in pregnancy; use levetiracetam or lamotrigine instead). 
- Absence seizure: child with brief staring spells, 3-Hz spike-and-wave EEG 
	- treat with ethosuximide(or valproate). 
- Status Epilepticus: ≥5 minutes of continuous seizure or recurrent seizures without regaining consciousness 
	- management:ABCs and IV benzodiazepines (lorazepam) first, then IV fosphenytoin. If persistent, consider IV phenobarbital or intubation with propofol.
#### Headache
#neurology/headache/migraine #neurology/headache/cluster-headache #neurology/headache/tension-headache #triptans 
- **Migraine**: Unilateral, pulsating headache often with nausea, photophobia; ± aura (visual scotomas). 
	- Acute treatment: NSAIDs or triptans (sumatriptan contraindicated in CAD due to vasospasm). 
	- Prophylaxis: Indicated for frequent migraines – e.g. β-blockers (propranolol), topiramate, or tricyclic antidepressants.
- **Cluster Headache:** Severe unilateral periorbital pain (clusters of attacks over weeks), with lacrimation and nasal congestion. Often occurs at the same time (night). 
	- Acute treatment:100% O₂ by nonrebreather mask (high-flow) and sumatriptan injection. 
	- Prophylaxis: Verapamilis first-line to prevent clusters.
- **Tension Headache**: Bilateral band-like pain, often related to stress, no focal neuro signs. 
	- Treatment: analgesics (NSAIDs, acetaminophen) and stress reduction; consider amitriptyline for chronic tension headache prophylaxis.
#### Parkinson Disease
#neurology/parkinson-disease
- Neurodegenerative loss of dopaminergic neurons in substantia nigra. Classic triad: resting tremor (“pill-rolling”), rigidity (cogwheel), and bradykinesia (shuffling gait, masked facies). Postural instability occurs later. 
- Treatment: For functional impairment, start levodopa/carbidopa(most effective); dopamine agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole) can be used in younger patients to delay levodopa use. Add COMT inhibitors or MAO-B inhibitors to enhance levodopa in advanced disease. Treat tremor-predominant with trihexyphenidyl (anticholinergic) if patient is young.
#### Myasthenia Gravis
#neurology/myasthenia-gravis
- Autoimmune antibodies against acetylcholine receptors at NMJ, causing fluctuating muscle weakness (classically ptosis and diplopia, weakness worse at end of day or with repeated use). 
- Diagnosis: Edrophonium (Tensilon) test transiently improves weakness, or better: autoantibody testing (AChR-Ab) and EMG. Associated with thymoma (get chest CT).
- Treatment: Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (pyridostigmine) provide symptomatic relief; add immunosuppression (steroids, azathioprine) for moderate to severe disease. Thymectomy can be curative, especially in younger patients or if thymoma present. For myasthenic crisis (respiratory failure), secure airway and give plasmapheresis or IVIG.
#### Guillain–Barré Syndrome (GBS)
#neurology/guillain-barre-syndrome 
- Acute autoimmune demyelinating polyneuropathy, often after a GI or respiratory infection (Campylobacter jejuni). Presents with ascending paralysis and areflexia; may progress to respiratory failure. 
- Diagnosis: Clinical; supported by LP showing elevated protein with normal WBC (albuminocytologic dissociation). 
- Management: Monitor respiratory function (spirometry for FVC); treat with IVIG or plasmapheresis(speeds recovery) – do _not_give steroids (not effective in GBS). Most patients recover over weeks to months.
#### Multiple Sclerosis (MS)
#neurology/multiple-sclerosis
- Autoimmune demyelination in CNS, classically young women with episodes of neurologic deficits separated in time and space (e.g. optic neuritis causing transient vision loss, internuclear ophthalmoplegia, sensory or motor symptoms). Symptoms may worsen transiently with heat (Uhthoff’s phenomenon). 
- Diagnosis: MRI of brain/spine shows multifocal white matter lesions (periventricular plaques); CSF often has oligoclonal bands. 
- Treatment: Acute relapses treated with high-dose IV corticosteroids. Disease-modifying therapy (interferon-β, glatiramer, or newer agents like natalizumab, ocrelizumab) reduces relapse frequency. Symptomatic management for spasticity (baclofen) or fatigue (amantadine) as needed.
#### Meningitis
#neurology/meningitis #infectious-disease/meningitis
- Classic triad: fever, headache, neck stiffness. May have photophobia, vomiting, altered mental status. 
- Workup: Perform LP for CSF analysis unless_signs of increased ICP (papilledema, focal neuro deficits) – if present, do CT head first. CSF:Bacterial meningitis: high opening pressure, neutrophils, low glucose, high protein; viral: lymphocytes, normal glucose. 
- Empiric Treatment: Start IV antibiotics ASAP– e.g. ceftriaxone + vancomycin (covers pneumococcus and meningococcus); add ampicillin if Listeria risk (age >50 or immunocompromised). Dexamethasone is given before or with first antibiotic dose for suspected pneumococcal meningitis (reduces neurologic complications). Close contacts of Neisseria meningitidis cases need prophylaxis (rifampin or ciprofloxacin).
#### Spinal Cord Compression
#spinal-cord-compression #cauda-equina-syndrome
- Consider in any patient with back pain plus neurological deficits(motor weakness, sensory level, bowel/bladder dysfunction). Causes include metastases (e.g. prostate or breast cancer to vertebrae), epidural abscess (fever, elevated ESR, IV drug use), or large disc herniation. 
- Management: This is an emergency – give high-dose corticosteroids immediately if cord compression by tumor is suspected, and obtain MRI of the spine. Neurosurgical evaluation for decompression is urgent. Epidural abscess requires antibiotics and often surgical drainage. 
- Cauda Equina Syndrome: Compression of lumbosacral nerve roots (e.g. massive disc herniation) causing saddle anesthesia, incontinence, and leg weakness – requires prompt surgical decompression for best recovery.
## Endocrinology
#endocrinology 
#### Diabetes Mellitus (Type 1 & 2) 
#endocrinology/diabetes 
- Symptoms: polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss (especially type 1), recurrent infections. 
- Type 1: autoimmune β-cell destruction – usually in youth, requires insulin for survival; prone to ketoacidosis. 
- Type 2: insulin resistance – associated with obesity; managed with diet/exercise and oral agents initially. 
	- Management of Type 2: First-line is metformin (unless contraindicated by renal insufficiency), then add sulfonylureas, GLP-1 agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, or insulin as needed. Target HbA1c ~7%. All diabetics get annual retinal exam, foot exam, and nephropathy screening (microalbuminuria); manage BP and lipids aggressively (statin for ≥40 years old).
#### Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)
#endocrinology/DKA #endocrinology/diabetes 
- Acute, life-threatening complication of type 1 DM (often the initial presentation or due to insulin noncompliance). 
- Features: abdominal pain, nausea/vomiting, Kussmaul respirations, fruity (acetone) breath. Labs: very high glucose (usually 300–700), high anion-gap metabolic acidosis, ketones in blood/urine, total body potassium deficit (despite normal/high serum K⁺). 
- Management:Aggressive IV fluid resuscitationis first priority. Begin IV insulin drip after_confirming K⁺ ≥3.3 (insulin drives K⁺ into cells). Monitor and replete potassium as needed. Add dextrose to IV fluids once glucose ~200 to prevent hypoglycemia while continuing insulin until anion gap closes. Identify and treat precipitant (infection, infarction, etc.).
#### Hyperosmolar Hyperglycemic State (HHS)
#endocrinology/HHS #endocrinology/diabetes 
- Seen in type 2 DM with some insulin present (prevents ketosis). Extremely high glucose (often >600) leads to dehydration and altered mental status, without significant ketosis or acidosis. 
- Treatment:Aggressive IV fluids and IV insulin (usually at lower dose than DKA); careful monitoring of electrolytes. HHS can cause seizures and osmotic demyelination if corrected too rapidly – go slow with fluid correction.
#### Hypoglycemia
#endocrinology/hyperglycemia
- Blood glucose <70 mg/dL with symptoms like sweating, tremors, palpitations, confusion, seizures. Common in diabetics from insulin/oral hypoglycemics overshooting. 
- Management: If conscious, give fast-acting carbs (glucose tabs or juice). If severe or unconscious, give IM glucagon(outpatient) or IV dextrose(50% D₅W amp) in hospital. Octreotide can be used for sulfonylurea overdose-induced hypoglycemia. Always re-check fingerstick glucose.
#### Hyperthyroidism (Thyrotoxicosis)
#endocrinology/thyroid #endocrinology/hyperthyroidism #thyroid-storm #graves-disease
- Symptoms: weight loss, heat intolerance, sweating, tremor, tachycardia or atrial fibrillation, hypertension, and anxiety. Graves disease is most common cause (autoimmune; features exophthalmos, pretibial myxedema). 
- Diagnosis: Low TSH, high T₃/T₄; radioactive iodine uptake (RAIU) diffuse in Graves, patchy in toxic nodules. 
- Treatment: β-blockers (e.g. propranolol) for symptom control. Antithyroid drugs – methimazoleis first-line (except in first trimester pregnancy, use PTU); PTU also preferred in thyroid storm. Definitive therapy is radioactive iodine ablation (Graves) or surgery (large goiters or cancer suspicion). 
- Thyroid storm: life-threatening hyperthyroid crisis (fever, delirium, arrhythmia) – treat with high-dose PTU, iodine solution (given after PTU), β-blocker, and steroids.
#### Hypothyroidism
#endocrinology/thyroid #endocrinology/hypothyroidism #levothyroxine
- Fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, constipation, bradycardia, dry skin. Most common cause in US is Hashimoto thyroiditis (autoimmune; anti-TPO antibodies). Labs: high TSH, low free T₄. 
- Treatment: Levothyroxine(synthetic T₄) with dose adjusted to normalize TSH. Myxedema coma (severe hypothyroid with AMS, hypothermia) is an emergency – treat with IV levothyroxine and IV hydrocortisone (if adrenal insufficiency not ruled out).
#### Adrenal Insufficiency (Addison Disease)
#endocrinology/adrenal-insufficiency #addison-disease  #adrenal-crisis #glucocorticoid #mineralcorticoid 
- Primary adrenal insufficiency usually autoimmune (can be due to TB worldwide). 
- Symptoms: weakness, fatigue, anorexia/weight loss, hyperpigmentation (in primary), hypotension, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia (lack of aldosterone). 
- Diagnosis: Low 8 AM cortisol and high ACTH in primary; confirm with cosyntropin stimulation test (little or no rise in cortisol). 
- Treatment: Daily glucocorticoid (e.g. hydrocortisone) and mineralocorticoid (fludrocortisone) replacement for primary. For secondary AI (pituitary cause), only glucocorticoids are needed. 
- Adrenal crisis (acute insufficiency):treat with IV fluids and IV high-dose hydrocortisone immediately.
#### Cushing Syndrome
#endocrinology/cushing-syndrome #pituitary-adenoma
- Signs of cortisol excess: central obesity, moon facies, buffalo hump, purple striae, skin bruising, muscle weakness, osteoporosis, hypertension, diabetes. 
- Etiologies: Exogenous steroids (most common), Cushing disease (ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma), adrenal tumor, or ectopic ACTH (e.g. small cell lung CA). 
- Diagnosis: Screening with low-dose dexamethasone suppression test (cortisol remains high in Cushing’s) or 24h urine free cortisol. Then check ACTH level to differentiate ACTH-dependent vs independent causes, and MRI/CT for source. 
- Treatment: Surgical resection of the tumor (transsphenoidal surgery for pituitary adenoma, adrenalectomy for adrenal tumor). Taper exogenous steroids if that’s the cause.
#### Pheochromocytoma
#endocrinology/pheochromocytoma #metanephrines #phenoxybenzamine
- Catecholamine-secreting tumor of adrenal medulla. Presents with episodic 5 P’s: Pressure (hypertension), Pain (headaches), Perspiration, Palpitations (tachycardia), Pallor. Episodes are episodic and can be precipitated by pressure or certain foods/drugs. 
- Diagnosis: Elevated plasma free metanephrines or 24-hour urine metanephrines/catecholamines. Localize with adrenal imaging (CT/MRI). 
- Management: Alpha blockade first(e.g. phenoxybenzamine) for 10–14 days, then beta blockade, then surgical resection of tumor. (Never give β-blocker first, to avoid unopposed alpha and hypertensive crisis.)
#### Calcium Disorders
#endocrinology/primary-hyperparathyroidism #endocrinology/hypocalcemia #parathyroid-adenoma #hypercalcemia-crisis 
- **Primary Hyperparathyroidism**: Causes hypercalcemia (stones, bones, abdominal groans, psychiatric overtones). Most often due to a parathyroid adenoma. Labs: high calcium, high PTH. 
	- Treatment: Parathyroidectomy if symptomatic or calcium significantly elevated; if mild, keep hydrated and avoid thiazides. Hypercalcemic crisis (>14 mg/dL) requires IV fluids, IV calcitonin acutely, and IV bisphosphonates for longer-term control.
- **Hypocalcemia**: Presents with perioral tingling, muscle cramps, tetany (Chvostek and Trousseau signs positive). Common causes: post-thyroidectomy (hypoparathyroid), or chronic kidney disease (↓vitamin D activation). 
	- Treatment: IV calcium gluconate if severe (tetany, arrhythmias); oral calcium and vitamin D for chronic management.
#### Diabetes Insipidus (DI)
#endocrinology/DI #central-DI #nephrogenic-DI #DDAVP 
- Polyuria and polydipsia with inability to concentrate urine (low urine osmolality, high serum osmolality). 
- Subtypes
	- Central DI: lack of ADH (from pituitary) – causes include idiopathic, head trauma, tumors. 
	- Nephrogenic DI: kidneys don’t respond to ADH (e.g. due to lithium, hypercalcemia). 
- Diagnosis: Water deprivation test – DI will have continued dilute urine; then desmopressin (ADH analog) test – central DI will concentrate urine (↓ output) after desmopressin, whereas nephrogenic will not. 
- Treatment:
	- Central DI – desmopressin (DDAVP)intranasal or oral. 
	- Nephrogenic DI – treat cause (stop lithium), low-solute diet, and thiazide diuretics or NSAIDs to reduce urine output.
#### Pituitary Adenomas 
#pituitary-adenoma #prolactinoma #acromegaly #cabergoline #bromocriptine
- Prolactinoma is most common: presents with hypogonadism (amenorrhea, infertility), galactorrhea, and headaches or visual field defects if large. 
- Treatment: Dopamine agonists (cabergoline, bromocriptine) to shrink tumor; transsphenoidal surgery if unresponsive or very large. 
- Acromegaly: GH-secreting adenoma – enlarged jaw/hands, diabetes, hypertension; treat with transsphenoidal surgery or octreotide. 
- Non-functioning adenomas: can cause panhypopituitarism and need hormone replacement and surgery if mass effect.
## Nephrology
#nephrology
#### Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)
#nephrology/AKI #intrinsic-AKI #prerenal-AKI #postrenal-AKI
- Rapid decline in GFR with rising BUN/Cr. 
- Pre-renal AKI:due to renal hypoperfusion (e.g. dehydration, heart failure) – BUN:Cr >20:1, low FeNa%. Treat with IV fluids or fix perfusion. 
- Intrinsic AKI: e.g. Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN from ischemia or toxins) – BUN:Cr ~10-15:1, FeNa% >2%, muddy brown casts; or glomerulonephritis – RBC casts, proteinuria (treat cause, often immunosuppressives). 
- Post-renal AKI: due to obstruction (enlarged prostate, stones) – diagnose with renal ultrasound showing hydronephrosis; relieve obstruction (catheter, surgery). 
- General management:remove nephrotoxins, ensure volume status, and dose-adjust medications.
#### Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)
#nephrology/CKD #dialysis #EPO 
- Progressive loss of kidney function (common causes: diabetes, hypertension). Often asymptomatic until advanced; labs show sustained low GFR, high Cr, and complications: anemia (↓EPO), metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia, mineral bone disease (secondary hyperparathyroidism). 
- Management: ACE inhibitors or ARBs to slow progression (especially in diabetic nephropathy with proteinuria). Manage complications: erythropoiesis-stimulating agents for anemia (target Hb ~10-11), phosphate binders and calcitriol for renal osteodystrophy, diuretics for edema and BP control. 
- Dialysis is indicated for uremic symptoms or severe metabolic issues (AEIOU: Acidosis, Electrolytes, Ingestion, Overload, Uremia) typically when GFR ~<10. Ultimate treatment is kidney transplant.
#### Nephrotic Syndrome
#nephrology/nephrotic-syndrome
- Heavy proteinuria >3.5 g/day, hypoalbuminemia, edema, hyperlipidemia. Causes: minimal change (kids), focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS in adults, HIV, heroin), membranous nephropathy (HBV, malignancy), diabetes, etc. 
- Presentation: Edema (peri-orbital, leg edema), foamy urine. Management:Treat underlying cause. Corticosteroids are first-line for minimal change (children) and often for FSGS. ACE inhibitors/ARBs help reduce proteinuria in all types. Manage edema with salt restriction and diuretics; hyperlipidemia with statins. Watch for hypercoagulability (especially in membranous nephropathy – can cause renal vein thrombosis).
#### Glomerulonephritis (Nephritic Syndrome)
#nephrology/nephritic-syndrome
- Glomerular inflammation leading to hematuria (RBC casts, cola-colored urine), proteinuria (usually <3.5 g), HTN, and edema. 
- Examples: _Post-streptococcal GN_ (2-3 weeks after strep throat, low C3, treat supportively), _IgA nephropathy_ (synpharyngitic hematuria, IgA deposits, treat with ACEi ± steroids), _Rapidly progressive GN_ (crescentic, as in Goodpasture or ANCA vasculitis – requires aggressive immunosuppressants like high-dose steroids + cyclophosphamide or plasmapheresis for Goodpasture). 
- Management: depends on cause, often immunosuppressive therapy for primary GN, plus supportive care (ACEi for proteinuria, BP control).
#### Nephrolithiasis (Kidney Stones)
#nephrology/nephrolithiasis
- Flank pain radiating to groin, hematuria, unilateral CVA tenderness. Types: calcium oxalate (most common), struvite (infection), uric acid, cystine. 
- Diagnosis: Non-contrast CT abdomen/pelvis is gold standard; ultrasound for pregnant patients. 
- Management: Hydration and analgesics for all. Stones <5 mm likely pass spontaneously; α-blockers (tamsulosin) can help pass distal ureteral stones. Larger stones or those causing obstruction/infection may need intervention: lithotripsy or ureteroscopic removal. Prevent recurrence by addressing underlying cause: thiazides for calcium stones, allopurinol or urine alkalinization for uric acid stones, etc.
#### Urinary Tract Infection (UTI) & Pyelonephritis
#nephrology/UTI #nephrology/pyelonephritis
- **Cystitis**: dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain; usually E. coli. 
	- Diagnosis:Urinalysis (nitrites, leukocyte esterase) and urine culture. 
	- Treatment:Nitrofurantoin or TMP-SMX for uncomplicated UTI (3–5 days); fosfomycin is another option. 
- **Pyelonephritis**: fever, chills, flank pain, CVA tenderness, often with UTI symptoms; UA with WBC casts. 
	- Treat with oral fluoroquinolone(e.g. ciprofloxacin for 7–10 days) if outpatient, or IV ceftriaxone/fluoroquinolone if inpatient. In pregnancy, use IV ceftriaxone for pyelonephritis. Always obtain culture to guide therapy, and adjust antibiotics per sensitivities.
#### Acid-Base Disorders
#metabolic-acidosis #metabolic-alkalosis #respiratory-acidosis #respiratory-alkalosis #MUDPILES
- Metabolic acidosis: 
	- calculate anion gap = Na – (Cl + HCO₃). 
	- High gap causes: MUDPILES (e.g. DKA, uremia, lactic acidosis); 
	- normal gap: diarrhea, RTA. 
- Metabolic alkalosis: check volume status and urine Cl⁻ (saline-responsive like vomiting vs saline-resistant like hyperaldosteronism). 
- Respiratory acidosis: hypoventilation (COPD, drug overdose); 
- Resp alkalosis: hyperventilation (anxiety, PE). 
- Management: Treat underlying cause; severe metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.1) may need IV bicarbonate; respiratory failures need ventilation support. Properly interpret ABG and use Winter’s formula for compensation in mixed disorders.\
## Hematology & Oncology
#hematology 
#### Iron Deficiency Anemia
#hematology/iron-deficiency-anemia #microcytic-anemia
- Microcytic anemia, usually from chronic blood loss (e.g. menstruation or GI bleed) or increased demand. Labs: low iron, low ferritin, high TIBC. Symptoms: fatigue, pallor, pica (craving ice/dirt). Workup:in men or postmenopausal women, assume GI bleed until proven otherwise (do colonoscopy/endoscopy). 
- Treatment: Oral iron supplementation (ferrous sulfate) – anticipate GI side effects; IV iron if intolerant or severe. Treat the underlying cause (e.g. heavy menstrual bleeding, GI lesion).
#### Megaloblastic Anemia (B₁₂ vs Folate)
#hematology/megaloblastic-anemia #folate #vitamin-b12
- Macrocytic anemia with hypersegmented neutrophils. 
	- Vitamin B₁₂ deficiency:from pernicious anemia, chronic malabsorption, or vegan diet. Presents with anemia and neurologic symptoms (peripheral neuropathy, posterior column damage causing loss of vibration/position sense). 
	- Folate deficiency: from poor diet or alcoholism; similar anemia without neuro deficits. 
- Labs: B₁₂ deficiency: high methylmalonic acid and homocysteine; Folate def: normal methylmalonic, high homocysteine. 
- Treatment: Replace the deficient vitamin (B₁₂ injections for pernicious anemia; oral folate for folate def.). Correct B₁₂ before folate to avoid precipitating neuro symptoms.
#### Anemia of Chronic Disease
#hematology/anemia-of-chronic-disease
- Mild/moderate anemia (usually normocytic or mildly microcytic) in patients with chronic inflammation (e.g. RA, chronic infection, malignancy). Iron is sequestered (↑ferritin, low serum iron, low TIBC). 
- Treatment: Manage the underlying disease; if severe anemia and inflammatory cause is not easily reversed, erythropoietin (epoetin alfa) can be considered especially in CKD.
#### Hemolytic Anemias
#hematology/hemolytic-anemia #g6pd-deficiency #sickle-cell-anemia #thalassemias #DIC #HIT #ITP #TTP
- Elevated reticulocyte count, LDH, indirect bilirubin; low haptoglobin. 
- **Sickle Cell Disease**: autosomal recessive (HbS); chronic hemolysis and vaso-occlusive crises (pain crises, acute chest syndrome, stroke, dactylitis in infants). Functional asplenia by childhood (risk of encapsulated infections). 
	- Management: Hydroxyurea increases HbF and reduces crises. Pain crises: IV fluids, oxygen, analgesia. Acute chest or stroke: exchange transfusion. Give pneumococcal vaccination and penicillin prophylaxis in childhood. 
- **G6PD deficiency:** X-linked; hemolysis after oxidative stress (fava beans, sulfa drugs); see bite cells and Heinz bodies; manage by avoiding triggers. 
- **Autoimmune hemolytic anemia:** positive Coombs test; treat with steroids or immunosuppression.
* **Sickle Cell Trait** – Heterozygous HbAS – generally asymptomatic, normal life expectancy. May have renal concentrating defect or hematuria, but no pain crises. Important to counsel on genetics; no specific treatment needed.
* **Thalassemias**– Inherited microcytic anemias with normal iron studies. 
	* Alpha thalassemia: 1-2 gene deletions cause mild anemia; 3 deletions (HbH disease) moderate hemolysis; 4 deletions is hydrops fetalis. 
	* Beta thalassemia: Minor (heterozygote) causes mild microcytosis; Major (Cooley anemia)– severe anemia in first year of life, “crewcut” skull on X-ray from marrow expansion, needs lifelong transfusions (leading to iron overload managed with chelation). Diagnose via hemoglobin electrophoresis.
* **Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)**– Pathologic clotting cascade activation leading to consumptive coagulopathy. Causes: sepsis, trauma, obstetric emergencies, malignancy (e.g. APML), snake bites. Labs: prolonged PT/PTT, low fibrinogen, elevated D-dimer, schistocytes on smear, low platelets. 
	* Management: Treat the underlying cause; supportive care with blood products as needed (cryoprecipitate for fibrinogen, platelets, FFP). In some chronic DIC cases, heparin can be used, but generally avoid in active bleeding.
* **Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT)**– Suspect if platelets drop >50% ~5–10 days after heparin start, with new thrombosis. An immune complex (IgG to PF4-heparin) activates platelets causing clotting and low platelet count. 
	* Management: Stop all heparin immediately. Start a non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban or bivalirudin). Do not give platelets. Switch to warfarin only after platelet count recovers (warfarin is contraindicated in acute HIT due to risk of skin necrosis).
* I**mmune Thrombocytopenic Purpura (ITP)**– Autoantibodies (IgG) against platelets (GpIIb/IIIa), often post-viral in kids or associated with SLE/HIV/HCV in adults. Platelet count is low with petechiae, mucosal bleeding, but coag tests normal. 
	* Management: If platelets <30k or significant bleeding, treat with corticosteroids first-line. IVIG is used for severe bleeding or need rapid increase (e.g. pre-op). Refractory cases: splenectomy or thrombopoietin receptor agonists. Children often recover spontaneously.
* **Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP)**– Deficiency of ADAMTS13 causing large vWF multimers and platelet thrombi in small vessels. Pentad: MAHA (microangiopathic hemolysis with schistocytes), thrombocytopenia, neurologic symptoms (confusion, stroke), renal impairment, and fever. 
	* Management: This is an emergency – start plasmapheresis (plasma exchange)immediately. Do not give platelets (worsens thrombosis). Can add steroids; rituximab for refractory cases.
#### Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)
#hematology/DVT 
- Unilateral leg swelling, pain, redness (commonly calf); risk factors by Virchow’s triad (stasis, endothelial injury, hypercoagulability – e.g. surgery, cancer, Factor V Leiden). 
- Diagnosis: Doppler ultrasound of the leg veins. 
- Treatment: Anticoagulation (e.g. subcutaneous LMWH or IV heparin bridge to warfarin, or start a DOAC like rivaroxaban). Typical duration is 3 months for provoked DVT, longer or indefinite if unprovoked or recurrent or underlying thrombophilia.
- Prevention: prophylactic heparin or enoxaparin in hospitalized, postoperative, or immobile patients. If contraindication to anticoagulation (recent hemorrhagic stroke, active bleeding), use an IVC filter to prevent PE.
#### Pulmonary Embolism– (See Pulmonology) 
#hematology/PE 
- treat as above with anticoagulation. Massive PE causing shock may need thrombolytics.
#### Anticoagulant Therapies
#anticoagulation #warfarin #DOAC #heparin
- **Heparin** (unfractionated): IV, monitored by PTT, reversed with protamine; can cause HIT. Low-molecular-weight heparin:SC, more predictable, no routine monitoring, renally cleared. 
- **Warfarin**: oral vitamin K antagonist (monitor PT/INR, goal 2–3 for most indications); reversed with vitamin K and PCC; teratogenic. 
- **Direct Oral Anticoagulants** (DOACs): factor Xa inhibitors (rivaroxaban, apixaban) and direct thrombin inhibitor (dabigatran) – fixed doses, no routine INR checks; dabigatran reversed with idarucizumab, factor Xa inh partially reversed with andexanet alfa. Use DOACs in non-valvular afib and VTE as first-line nowadays (easier than warfarin). Mechanical heart valves still require warfarin (DOACs not indicated).
#### Leukemia
#hematology/leukemia #AML #ALL #CML #CLL
- Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML): adult, myeloblasts on smear (Auer rods in M3 subtype); present with anemia, thrombocytopenia, infections. 
	- Treat with combination chemotherapy (e.g. cytarabine + anthracycline); M3 (APL) treated with ATRA (all-trans retinoic acid) which can induce remission. 
- Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL): children, lymphoblasts on smear, associated with Down syndrome. 
	- Treat with combination chemo; prophylactic chemo to CNS (intrathecal methotrexate) is needed.
- Chronic Myeloid Leukemia (CML): adults, marked leukocytosis (often >100k) with left shift, Philadelphia chromosomet(9;22) BCR-ABL. 
	- Treat with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (imatinib).
- Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (CLL): older adults, often asymptomatic lymphocytosis with smudge cells on smear; can cause lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly. Early stage may observe
	- Treat symptomatic or advanced with targeted therapies (e.g. BTK inhibitor ibrutinib).
#### Lymphoma
#hematology/lymphoma #hodgkin-lymphoma #non-hodgkin-lymphoma #ABVD
- Hodgkin Lymphoma: Bimodal age distribution, Reed-Sternberg cells (owl-eyes) on biopsy, typically presents with painless lymphadenopathy (often cervical) and “B” symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss). Very responsive to therapy (ABVD chemo regimen). 
- Non-Hodgkin Lymphomas: diverse group (e.g. diffuse large B-cell is most common aggressive type; follicular is indolent; Burkitt is high-grade with starry-sky histology). Diagnosis by excisional lymph node biopsy. Treatment varies by type/stage (chemo, rituximab for CD20+ B-cell lymphomas, etc.).
#### Multiple Myeloma
#hematology/multiple-myeloma
- Malignancy of plasma cells in bone marrow, common in older adults. Presents with CRAB: hyperCalcemia, Renal failure, Anemia, Bone pain (lytic lesions, fractures). Lab findings: monoclonal protein (IgG or IgA) spike on serum protein electrophoresis, Bence-Jones protein (light chains) in urine, rouleaux on smear. 
- Diagnosis: Bone marrow biopsy showing >10% clonal plasma cells. 
- Management: Immunomodulators (lenalidomide), proteasome inhibitors (bortezomib), and dexamethasone; autologous stem cell transplant in eligible patients. Bisphosphonates for bone lesions
#### Breast Cancer
#oncology/breast-cancer #tamoxifen #trastuzumab
- Most common cancer in women. Suspect in a woman >30 with a new breast mass. Workup:Mammography (if age >30) or ultrasound (if <30 or pregnant) for evaluation, then core needle biopsy of suspicious lesion. 
- Common types: Infiltrating ductal carcinoma is most common. 
- Treatment: Lumpectomy plus radiation or mastectomy for local disease; sentinel lymph node biopsy for staging. Adjuvant therapy based on subtype – e.g. ER/PR+ cancers get endocrine therapy (tamoxifen if premenopausal, aromatase inhibitor if postmenopausal), HER2+ cancers get trastuzumab. Chemotherapy for high-risk or advanced disease. 
- Screening: Mammograms every 2 years for women 40–74 (USPSTF) to reduce mortality.
#### Colon Cancer
#oncology/colon-cancer #FOLFOX
- Usually adenocarcinoma arising from polyps; risk factors include adenomatous polyps, long-standing IBD, familial syndromes (FAP, HNPCC). Symptoms: occult GI bleeding (iron deficiency anemia in older adult is colon cancer until proven otherwise), change in bowel habits, weight loss. 
- Diagnosis: Colonoscopy with biopsy is gold standard. 
- Treatment: Surgical resection for localized disease; add chemotherapy for stage III or higher (e.g. FOLFOX regimen). 
- Screening: Colonoscopy starting at age 45, then every 10 years if normal (or alternatives like annual FIT testing, flex sig every 5 yrs + FIT, etc., up to age 75).
#### Prostate Cancer
#oncology/prostate-cancer
- Common in older men, usually adenocarcinoma in peripheral zone of prostate. Often asymptomatic until advanced (may present with urinary retention or back pain from bone metastases). 
- Screening: Controversial – USPSTF recommends individualized decision for PSA screening age 55–69, not routine. 
- Diagnosis: Elevated PSA and abnormal DRE (hard nodule) raise suspicion; confirmed by transrectal ultrasound-guided biopsy. 
- Treatment: Localized disease can be managed with surgery (prostatectomy) or radiation; metastatic disease treated with androgen deprivation (GnRH agonists like leuprolide, or orchiectomy) and newer hormonal agents; bone metastases get androgen blockade and possibly radiation.
#### Lung Cancer
#oncology/lung-cancer #NSCLC #SCLC
- Leading cause of cancer death. 
- **Non–Small Cell (NSCLC)**: includes squamous cell (central, cavitation, hyperCa²⁺), adenocarcinoma (peripheral, most common in nonsmokers), large cell. Surgical resection is option in early stage; add chemo or radiation based on stage. 
- **Small Cell Lung Cancer**: central, aggressive, often metastatic at presentation; associated with paraneoplastic syndromes (ACTH, SIADH). Treatment is usually chemotherapy + radiation (surgery not usually feasible). 
- Screening: Annual low-dose CT for adults 50–80 who have ≥20 pack-year smoking history and currently smoke or quit within 15 years - reduces mortality by catching early. Encourage smoking cessation.
#### Pancreatic Cancer
#oncology/pancreatic-cancer 
- Usually adenocarcinoma in pancreatic head. Risk factors: smoking, chronic pancreatitis. Presents with weight loss, abdominal pain radiating to back, and painless obstructive jaundice (Courvoisier’s sign: nontender distended gallbladder). 
- Diagnosis: Abdominal imaging (CT or MRCP) to identify mass; endoscopic ultrasound with biopsy confirms. 
- Treatment: Surgical resection (Whipple procedure) if localized to head and no major vessel involvement; often chemotherapy even if resected. Prognosis is generally poor.
## Pediatrics
#pediatrics

#### Neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS)
#pediatrics/neonatal-respiratory-distress-syndrome
- Premature infant with grunting, tachypnea, and cyanosis shortly after birth due to surfactant deficiency (maternal diabetes is a risk). 
- CXR: ground-glass reticulogranular pattern with air bronchograms. 
- Prevention: Antenatal corticosteroids to mother if preterm labor <34 weeks (accelerates lung maturity). 
- Management: Oxygen (with CPAP) and exogenous surfactant **v**ia endotracheal tube. Avoid high oxygen to prevent retinopathy.
#### Transient Tachypnea of Newborn (TTN)
#pediatrics/transient-tachypnea-of-newborn 
- Term or near-term infant with tachypnea shortly after birth (especially if C-section, due to retained fluid). 
- CXR: perihilar streaking and fluid in fissures. 
- Management: Supportive (O₂, resolves in 1-2 days as fluid clears). Contrast with RDS and persistent pulmonary hypertension of newborn in different scenarios.
#### Croup (Laryngotracheitis)
#pediatrics/croup #steeple-sign #parainfluenza
- Parainfluenza viral infection in young children (ages 6 mo–3 yr) causing “barking” cough, hoarse voice, and inspiratory stridor. Often a mild fever, symptoms worse at night. 
- Diagnosis: Clinical; AP neck x-ray shows subglottic narrowing (“steeple sign”). Treatment: Cool mist and a single dose of dexamethasone for mild cases. If stridor at rest or respiratory distress, give nebulized racemic epinephrine (causes vasoconstriction and reduces airway edema) – observe for rebound symptoms.
#### Epiglottitis
#pediatrics/epiglottits #h-influenzae
- Emergency! Classically due to Hib (H. influenzae type b) in unvaccinated children. Rapid onset high fever, sore throat, dysphagia, drooling, and tripod positioning (sitting up, leaning forward). Stridor and muffled “hot potato” voice. 
- Management: Secure the airway _immediately_– prepare for intubation in the OR (potential for sudden airway closure). Do not_attempt to visualize the throat if not in controlled setting. Once airway secure, IV antibiotics (e.g. ceftriaxone) are given. Rifampin prophylaxis to household contacts if Hib.
#### Bronchiolitis
#pediatrics/bronchiolitis #respiratory-syncytial-virus
- Usually caused by Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) in infants in winter. Presents with wheezing, cough, and difficulty breathing in a child <2. 
- Management: Supportive care (hydration, nasal suctioning, oxygen if needed). No role for antibiotics; bronchodilators/steroids generally not helpful (trial of albuterol may be done, but discontinue if no benefit). High-risk infants (premature or chronic lung disease) get pavilizumab (monoclonal antibody) for RSV prophylaxis.
#### Otitis Media
#pediatrics/otitis-media #SHiM
- Common in infants/children. Ear pain, pulling at ear, often after URI; tympanic membrane is bulging and erythematous with decreased mobility on insufflation. Most often Strep pneumo, H. influenzae, Moraxella. 
- Treatment: High-dose amoxicillinfor 7-10 days is first-line. If recurrent or persistent, use amoxicillin-clavulanate. Tympanostomy tubes for chronic effusions or recurrent infections. 
#### Otitis externa (“swimmer’s ear”) 
#pediatrics/otitis-externa #pseudomonas
- outer ear canal infection (Pseudomonas common) – pain with tragus push; treat with topical fluoroquinolone ear drops.
#### Pertussis (Whooping Cough)
#pediatrics/pertussis #bordetella-pertussis #DTaP-vaccine #Tdap
- Caused by Bordetella pertussis in under-vaccinated or waning immunity. 
- Stages: catarrhal (mild cough, rhinorrhea), then paroxysmal (severe coughing fits with inspiratory “whoop” and post-tussive vomiting), then convalescent. 
- Diagnosis: PCR or culture of nasopharyngeal swab. 
- Treatment: Macrolide antibiotic (azithromycin) – helps if given early, and to reduce spread. Close contacts also get macrolide prophylaxis. Prevention with DTaP vaccine in childhood and Tdap boosters (adolescents, adults, and pregnant women each pregnancy).
#### Measles (Rubeola)
#pediatrics/measles #paramyxovirus #koplik-spots #MMR-vaccine
- Highly contagious paramyxovirus. Presentation: prodrome of cough, coryza, conjunctivitis, and high fever, with Koplik spots(white spots on buccal mucosa). Then a maculopapular rash starts on face (hairline) and spreads downward. Rash is confluent, and fever often continues during rash. 
- Management: Supportive care; vitamin A supplementation reduces morbidity. Complications include otitis media, pneumonia, and encephalitis. 
- Prevention: Live attenuated MMR vaccine (given at age 12-15 mo and 4-6 yrs).
#### Rubella (German Measles)
#pediatrics/rubella #MMR-vaccine
- Generally milder than measles. Low-grade fever, tender lymphadenopathy (posterior auricular, suboccipital), and a faster-spreading maculopapular rash that starts on face and spreads down, usually sparing palms/soles. Forchheimer spots (petechiae on soft palate) may appear. Arthralgias are common, especially in adult women. 
- Management: Supportive. Prevent with MMR vaccine. _Congenital rubella_(from maternal infection in pregnancy) causes PDA, cataracts, deafness.
#### Mumps
#pediatrics/mumps #MMR-vaccine #paramyxovirus #parotitis
- Paramyxovirus causing parotitis (painful swollen parotid glands, chipmunk cheeks), fever, and malaise. Can lead to orchitis in post-pubertal males (risk of infertility) and aseptic meningitis. 
- Management: Supportive (hydration, pain control). Prevent with MMR vaccine.
#### Varicella (Chickenpox)
#pediatrics/varicella #varicella-vaccine
- Varicella-zoster virus. Fever and successive crops of pruritic vesicles on an erythematous base (“dew drop on a rose petal”) in various stages (papules, vesicles, crusts simultaneously). Contagious until lesions crusted. 
- Management: Supportive care for most children; acyclovir for adolescents, adults, or immunocompromised to reduce severity. 
- Prevention: Varicella vaccine (live attenuated) at 12-15 mo and 4-6 yr.
#### Scarlet Fever
#pediatrics/scarlet-fever 
- Caused by Group A Strep (streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins) in the setting of strep pharyngitis. Symptoms: fever, pharyngitis, sandpaper rash(fine, diffuse erythematous rash starting on trunk, feels like sandpaper), strawberry tongue, circumoral pallor. Pastia’s lines (rash in skin folds). 
- Treatment: Penicillin (or amoxicillin) – prevents rheumatic fever.
#### Kawasaki Disease
#pediatrics/kawasaki-disease #vasculitis
- Vasculitis in young children (usually <5 years). 
- Presentation: Fever ≥5 days plus 4 of CRASH: _Conjunctivitis_ (bilateral, nonexudative), _Rash_ (polymorphous), _Adenopathy_ (cervical lymph node, usually unilateral), _Strawberry tongue_ (and other mucous membrane changes like red cracked lips), _Hand/foot changes_ (swelling, erythema, desquamation). 
- Complication: Coronary artery aneurysms (risk of thrombosis or rupture). 
- Treatment: High-dose IVIG and aspirin (one of the few times aspirin used in kids) to reduce aneurysm risk. Obtain an echo initially and at 6-8 weeks to monitor coronary arteries.
#### Henoch–Schönlein Purpura (HSP)
#pediatrics/henoch-schonlein-purpura  #vasculitis
- IgA vasculitis in children often after URI. Features: palpable purpuric rash on buttocks/legs, arthralgias, abdominal pain, and renal involvement (hematuria from IgA nephropathy). 
- Treatment: Supportive (hydration, NSAIDs for pain); severe cases may use steroids. Generally self-limited.
#### Pyloric Stenosis
#pediatrics/pyloric-stenosis
- Hypertrophy of pylorus in infants (especially first-born males) ~3-6 weeks old. Projectile non-bilious vomiting after feeds; infant is hungry after vomiting. Physical exam may show an “olive-like” palpable mass in RUQ. 
- Diagnosis: Ultrasound of abdomen (thickened pylorus). 
- Treatment: First correct dehydration and electrolyte imbalances (often hypokalemic hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis). Then surgical pyloromyotomy (Ramstedt procedure) is curative.=
#### Intussusception
#pediatrics/intussusception
- Telescoping of bowel (usually ileocolic, in children 6-36 months). Episodic inconsolable crying with knees drawn up, vomit, and “currant jelly” stools(bloody mucus). May palpate a sausage-shaped mass in right abdomen.
- Diagnosis/Treatment: Ultrasound is diagnostic (target sign) and air enema is both diagnostic and therapeutic (reduces the intussusception). If enema fails or intestine is perforated, surgical reduction is needed.
#### Congenital Intestinal Obstructions
#pediatrics/congenital-intestinal-obstructions #duodenal-atresia #hirschsprung-disease #malrotation-with-volvulus 
- **Duodenal atresia**: bilious vomiting in neonate, abdominal X-ray shows “double bubble” (associated with Down syndrome); treat with surgical duodenostomy. 
- **Hirschsprung disease**: neonate fails to pass meconium in first 48h, abdominal distension; due to lack of ganglion cells in distal colon (diagnose with rectal biopsy showing aganglionosis); treat by surgical resection of aganglionic segment. 
- **Malrotation with volvulus**: bilious vomiting in neonate, surgical emergency to untwist bowel (can compromise blood supply).
#### Developmental Milestones
#pediatrics/developmental-milestones
- 6 months – sits unsupported, babbles, stranger anxiety
- 9 months – crawls, pulls to stand, says “Mama/Dada” nonspecific, waves bye
- 12 months – walks, says 1-3 words, one-step commands
- 2 years – runs, 2-word phrases, 50+ words, parallel play
- 3 years – rides tricycle, 3-word sentences, knows age/gender
- 4 years – hops, cooperative play, knows some colors and numbers. 
#### Vaccine Schedule
#pediatrics/vaccines #Tdap  #MMR-vaccine  #varicella-vaccine  #DTaP-vaccine 
- By age 2, children should receive multiple doses of: Hep B, DTaP, Hib, Polio, PCV13, Rotavirus, and annual flu starting at 6 months. 
- MMR and Varicella start at 12 months. 
- HepA at 1 year (2 doses). 
- Tdap booster at 11-12 years, HPV series at 11-12 years, meningococcal at 11-12 and 16. 

## Surgery

#### Trauma Primary Survey (ABCs)
#trauma/primary-survery #ABCDE
- A: Airway with C-spine protection (assume cervical injury in trauma – immobilize neck; intubate early if airway compromise).  
- B: Breathing– ensure adequate ventilation (listen to breath sounds, chest tube for pneumothorax, needle decompression for tension pneumothorax). 
- C: Circulation– check pulses, control hemorrhage with direct pressure or tourniquets, obtain large-bore IV access for fluids/blood. 
- D: Disability– rapid neuro status (GCS, pupil response). 
- E: Exposure– fully undress to look for injuries, and prevent hypothermia. Follow with secondary survey (head-to-toe exam, imaging).
#### Tension Pneumothorax
#tension-pneumothorax
- Trauma (or barotrauma) causes one-way air leak into pleural space. Signs: hypotension, distended neck veins, tracheal deviation away from affected side, absent breath sounds and hyperresonance on affected side. 
- Management: Do not wait for X-ray – perform immediate needle decompression in 2nd ICS midclavicular line, then place chest tube. This relieves pressure on the mediastinum and restores venous return.

#### Cardiac Tamponade (Trauma)
#cardiac-tamponade
- Beck’s triad in a trauma setting (especially penetrating chest wound): hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds. Pulsus paradoxus may be present. 
- Management: Emergent subxiphoid pericardial window or pericardiocentesis to relieve tamponade; definitive surgical repair of cardiac injury by trauma surgery. 
#### Basilar Skull Fracture
#basilar-skull-fracture
- Signs: periorbital ecchymoses (raccoon eyes), mastoid ecchymosis (Battle sign), hemotympanum, CSF rhinorrhea or otorrhea. High risk of intracranial hemorrhage and CN injury. 
- Management: Head CT, neurosurgery consult. Avoid nasal intubation (could intubate into brain). Often managed conservatively, but requires close observation for complications.
#### Abdominal Trauma
#general-surgery/abdominal-trauma
- Blunt trauma with hypotension → get FAST ultrasound to check for free fluid (hemoperitoneum). If FAST positive and unstable patient: emergent exploratory laparotomy. Splenic injury is most common (left shoulder referred pain = Kehr’s sign). Liver laceration is also common. 
- Penetrating trauma:Gunshot wounds often require ex-lap if peritoneal signs or unstable; stab wounds may be managed with local wound exploration or laparoscopy if stable.
#### Splenic Rupture
#general-surgery/splenic-rupture
- Suspect in left rib fractures or trauma with shock and positive FAST in LUQ. The spleen is highly vascular; hemorrhage can cause shock. 
- Management: If unstable, exploratory laparotomy and likely splenectomy. If stable and injury moderate, non-operative management with observation is possible, or splenic artery embolization. Post-splenectomy, patient needs vaccinations against encapsulated organisms (pneumococcal, Hib, meningococcal) due to risk of overwhelming post-splenectomy sepsis.
#### Acute Abdomen
#general-surgery/acute-abdomen
- General term for acute, severe abdominal pain with guarding and rebound (peritonitis). Differential is broad (appendicitis, perforated ulcer, pancreatitis, bowel perforation, etc.). 
- Management approach: If peritonitis is diffuse and patient unstable, go to exploratory surgery. Otherwise, imaging (CT abdomen/pelvis) can help diagnose specific causes and guide management. Always consider surgical emergencies (ruptured AAA, volvulus, mesenteric ischemia) in acute abdomen.
#### Acute Appendicitis
#general-surgery/acute-appendicitis
- Most common surgical emergency. Often due to appendiceal lumen obstruction (fecalith). Presents with periumbilical pain that localizes to RLQ, fever, nausea, tenderness at McBurney’s point, positive psoas or obturator signs. Mild leukocytosis is common. 
- Management:NPO, IV fluids, antibiotics (cover Gram-negatives/anaerobes), and urgent appendectomy (can be laparoscopic). If an appendiceal abscess has formed, sometimes treat initially with antibiotics and interval appendectomy weeks later.

#### Bowel Obstruction
#general-surgery/bowel-obstruction
- Small bowel obstruction (SBO):Typically due to adhesions from prior surgeries (in developed countries) or hernias. Presents with crampy abdominal pain, vomiting, abdominal distension, and inability to pass flatus or stool (obstipation). High-pitched bowel sounds early, silent late. 
	- Diagnosis: Abdominal X-ray shows multiple air-fluid levels and dilated loops; CT can locate obstruction and check for strangulation (ischemia). 
	- Management: NPO, IV fluids, NG tube for decompression; most partial SBO resolve with conservative management. Surgical intervention if complete obstruction or signs of strangulation (fever, tachycardia, peritonitis, leukocytosis). 
- Large bowel obstruction:Commonly from colon cancer or volvulus; treat depending on cause (e.g. colon resection for tumor, endoscopic detorsion for sigmoid volvulus).
#### Mesenteric Ischemia
#general-surgery/mesenteric-ischemia
- Typically an older patient with atherosclerosis or atrial fibrillation (arterial embolus to SMA). Presents with acute onset severe abdominal pain out of proportion to exam(initially minimal findings). Later can get blood in stool and peritonitis if bowel infarction occurs. 
- Diagnosis: CT angiography of abdomen. 
- Management:Emergent – resuscitate, broad-spectrum antibiotics (risk of necrosis), and prompt revascularization (embolectomy or angiographic thrombolysis). Bowel resection if infarcted. Chronic mesenteric ischemia (intestinal angina) presents as postprandial pain and weight loss; managed with angioplasty or bypass of mesenteric arteries.
#### Aortic Aneurysm
#vascular-surgery/aortic-aneurysm
- Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA): Usually in older males, smokers. Often asymptomatic until rupture. Classic triad of ruptured AAA: abdominal or back pain, hypotension, and a pulsatile abdominal mass. Management:Emergent surgical repair for rupture (almost always fatal without intervention). Elective repair recommended for AAA ≥5.5 cm or rapid growth
- Screening: one-time abdominal ultrasound for men 65-75 who have ever smoked
#### Aortic Dissection
#vascular-surgery/aortic-dissection
- Tear in intimal layer of aorta creating a false lumen – risk factors: hypertension, connective tissue disorders. Presents with sudden “tearing” chest pain radiating to back, asymmetric blood pressures in arms. 
- Diagnosis: CT angiography (if stable) or transesophageal echo. 
- Management: Type A (ascending aorta involved) is surgical emergency (open repair) due to risk of tamponade; Type B (descending only) managed medically with aggressive BP control (IV beta-blocker) unless complications.
#### Peripheral Arterial Disease (PAD)
#vascular-surgery/peripheral-arterial-disease
- Atherosclerosis in peripheral arteries (often legs) causing intermittent claudication(calf pain with exertion, relieved by rest). Severe disease: rest pain, non-healing ulcers. Exam:decreased pulses, hair loss on legs, cool extremities. 
- Diagnosis: Ankle-brachial index (ABI) <0.90 confirms PAD; arterial Dopplers and angiography for planning intervention. 
- Management: Exercise program and risk factor modification (stop smoking, control diabetes, statin, antiplatelet like aspirin). Cilostazol (PDE inhibitor) can improve claudication symptoms. For critical limb ischemia or lifestyle-limiting claudication, revascularization (angioplasty with stent or bypass surgery) is indicated.
#### Compartment Syndrome
#compartment-syndrome
- Increased pressure in a closed muscle compartment (often leg or forearm) typically after trauma or reperfusion injury. Presents with severe pain out of proportion to exam, pain with passive stretch, tense swollen compartment, and late findings of pallor, pulselessness, paresthesias, paralysis (the 6 P’s). 
- Management: Urgent fasciotomy to relieve pressure and restore perfusion – this is a surgical emergency to prevent permanent damage.
#### Burns
#burns
- Depth: superficial (first-degree) – red, painful; partial thickness (second-degree) – blisters, very painful; full thickness (third-degree) – white or charred, painless (nerve destruction). 
- Initial management:ABCs and fluid resuscitation. Use Parkland formula for lactated Ringer’s: 4 mL × body weight (kg) × % BSA burned = total fluids in first 24h (give half in first 8h). Care: Aggressive IV fluids for burns >15% BSA. Prophylactic tetanus vaccine update. Topical antibiotics (silver sulfadiazine) on burns to prevent infection. Early excision and grafting for deep burns. Monitor for compartment syndrome in circumferential limb burns (may need escharotomy). Smoke inhalation injury can cause CO poisoning or airway burns – give 100% O₂ and intubate early if airway compromise is suspected (stridor, singed nasal hair, burns inside mouth).
## Obstetrics & Gynecology

### Obstetrics
#obstetrics
#### Ectopic Pregnancy
#obstetrics/ectopic-pregnancy
- Implantation of pregnancy outside the uterus (95% in fallopian tube). Risk factors: prior ectopic, PID, tubal surgery. Presents with abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding in first trimester; positive pregnancy test but no intrauterine pregnancy on ultrasound (transvaginal US may show adnexal mass). 
- Management: If unruptured and early (<6 weeks, β-hCG below threshold, mass <3-4 cm) – treat with methotrexate(folate antagonist) to abort the ectopic. If ruptured or hemodynamically unstable (signs of peritonitis or free fluid in pelvis), surgical emergency – do prompt surgical removal (laparoscopic salpingostomy or salpingectomy) and stabilize patient.
#### Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM)
#obstetrics/gestational-diabetes-mellitus #endocrinology/diabetes 
- Glucose intolerance first recognized in pregnancy (usually screened at 24-28 weeks with oral glucose tolerance test). Can cause fetal macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycemia, and increased risk of preeclampsia. 
- Management: Diet modification is first-line. If glucose targets not met, add insulin (does not cross placenta). Metformin is an alternative. Tight glucose control reduces fetal complications. After delivery, GDM usually resolves, but patients have high risk of developing type 2 DM – follow-up OGTT at 6-12 weeks postpartum.
#### Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy
#obstetrics/gestational-hypertension #cardiology/hypertension #obstetrics/preeclampsia #obstetrics/eclampsia #obstetrics/HELLP
- **Gestational Hypertension**: New-onset BP ≥140/90 after 20 weeks without proteinuria or end-organ signs. Manage with close monitoring
	- Medications (labetalol, methyldopa, or nifedipine) if BP persistent ≥160/110.
- **Preeclampsia**: New HTN with proteinuria(>300 mg/24h or protein/Cr ratio ≥0.3) or end-organ dysfunction after 20 weeks. Severe features include BP ≥160/110, thrombocytopenia, elevated LFTs, pulmonary edema, cerebral or visual symptoms. 
	- Management: If ≥37 weeks or any severe features, proceed to delivery(induction of labor) – it’s the only cure. Magnesium sulfate IV is given for seizure prophylaxis in severe preeclampsia. Control severe BP with IV labetalol or hydralazine. <34 weeks with severe features may benefit from corticosteroids for fetal lungs before delivery.
- **Eclampsia**: Preeclampsia + seizures. 
	- Management:ABCs, protect airway; give IV magnesium sulfateto control seizures (benzos if magnesium ineffective). Stabilize then immediate delivery (usually via induction; perform C-section if obstetrically indicated, but seizures alone are not an indication for Cesarean). Continue magnesium for 24h postpartum.
- **HELLP syndrome**: Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets – a severe form of preeclampsia. Present with RUQ pain, nausea/vomiting, in addition to preeclampsia signs. 
	- Management: Same as severe preeclampsia – magnesium and prompt delivery (often by induction; platelet transfusion if <20k or <50k with C-section).
#### Placenta Previa
#obstetrics/plaventa-previa
- Placenta implants over or near the cervical os. Presents with painlessbright red vaginal bleeding in the 3rd trimester. 
- Diagnosis: Ultrasound (transvaginal) shows placenta covering internal os. Management: No digital vaginal exams (risk provoking hemorrhage). Often requires C-section delivery (scheduled at ~37 weeks) because placenta blocks labor; earlier if heavy bleeding or fetal distress. Advise pelvic rest (no intercourse) during pregnancy.
#### Placental Abruption (Abruptio Placentae)
#obstetrics/placental-abruption
- Premature separation of a normally implanted placenta, often precipitated by hypertension, trauma, or cocaine use. Presents with painful third-trimester bleeding and a firm, tender uterus; can lead to fetal distress and maternal shock/DIC. 
- Management: If significant abruption or fetal distress, perform immediate delivery (usually emergency C-section). Ensure aggressive maternal resuscitation (IV fluids, blood products) and management of DIC if present. Mild cases at term can be induced for vaginal delivery.
#### Preterm Labor
#obstetrics/preterm-labor
- Onset of regular uterine contractions causing cervical change before 37 weeks. 
- Management: <34 weeks with labor: give antenatal corticosteroids (betamethasone) to accelerate fetal lung maturity. If <32 weeks, also give magnesium sulfate for fetal neuroprotection (reduces cerebral palsy risk). Tocolytic medications (e.g. nifedipine, indomethacin) can be used short-term to delay delivery 48 hours to allow steroid benefit. Treat any identifiable cause (e.g. antibiotics for intrauterine infection). After 34 weeks, generally allow labor to proceed.
#### Prelabor Rupture of Membranes (PROM)
#obstetrics/PROM
- Rupture of amniotic sac before onset of labor. If ≥37 weeks (term PROM), manage by inducing labor if it doesn’t start soon (to reduce infection risk). If <37 weeks (PPROM), manage based on gestational age: if under ~34 weeks with no infection, try expectant management with prophylactic antibiotics (ampicillin+erythromycin) to prolong pregnancy, give steroids for lungs, and monitor. Deliver if signs of chorioamnionitis (maternal fever, uterine tenderness, foul fluid) or fetal distress – in chorioamnionitis, give broad-spectrum antibiotics (amp + gent) and induce labor regardless of gestational age.
#### Postpartum Hemorrhage
#obstetrics/postpartum-hemorrhage
- >500 mL blood loss after vaginal delivery or >1000 mL after C-section. Common causes (the 4 T’s): Tone (uterine atony – most frequent), Tissue (retained placenta), Trauma (lacerations, uterine rupture), Thrombin (coagulopathy). 
- Management:Stepwise – fundal massage and uterotonic medications for atony (IV oxytocinfirst-line; then methylergonovine unless hypertensive; carboprost unless asthma; misoprostol can be used). Inspect for lacerations and repair if present. If suspect retained placenta, perform manual extraction or D&C. In refractory cases, employ balloon tamponade, uterine artery embolization, or surgical intervention (last resort hysterectomy).
#### Maternal Infections
#endometritis #TORCH #chorioamnionitis
- Chorioamnionitis: intra-amniotic infection (usually from prolonged ROM) – maternal fever, tachycardia (mom and fetus), uterine tenderness, foul fluid. 
	- Treat with broad IV antibiotics (ampicillin + gentamicin) and expedite delivery (induce if not already in labor). 
- Endometritis: postpartum uterine infection (more common after C-section) – fever, uterine tenderness, foul lochia. 
	- Treat with IV clindamycin + gentamicin. _
- TORCH infections:_ be aware of congenital infections: Toxo (chorioretinitis, hydrocephalus), Syphilis (snuffles, saber shins), Rubella (PDA, deafness), CMV (periventricular calcifications), Herpes (encephalitis, vesicles). 
	- Prevent with appropriate maternal screening and treatment (e.g. penicillin for syphilis, avoidance of cat litter for toxo, etc.).
#### Labor & Delivery Basics
- Stages of labor: 
	- Stage 1 latent – onset to 6 cm; 
	- Stage 1 active – 6 cm to full 10 cm dilation (active should progress ~1+ cm/hour); 
	- Stage 2 – 10 cm to delivery of baby; 
	- Stage 3 – delivery of placenta (within 30 min). 
- Indications for C-section: prior classical C-section, placenta previa, breech presentation in active labor, fetal distress, etc. 
- Vaginal birth after cesarean (VBAC):_possible if one prior low-transverse C-section and no contraindications (small risk of uterine rupture). 
- Pain management: epidural (bupivacaine) is common for labor; watch for hypotension. 
- Episiotomy: mediolateral preferred over midline to avoid 4th degree extension into rectum.
#### Gestational Trophoblastic Disease
#obstetrics/gestational-trophoblastic-disease #hydatidiform-mole #choriocarcinoma
- **Hydatidiform mole** (molar pregnancy): abnormal fertilization leads to placenta without viable fetus. Complete mole: 46,XX (paternal DNA only), very high β-hCG, “snowstorm” ultrasound, risk of choriocarcinoma. Presents with first trimester bleeding, hyperemesis, preeclampsia <20wks. 
	- Treatment: uterine evacuation (D&C) and then track β-hCG to zero; avoid pregnancy for 6-12 months. 
- **Choriocarcinoma**: malignant GTN can follow a mole, miscarriage, or pregnancy – presents with elevated β-hCG and signs of metastatic disease (lung mets common – hemoptysis); 
	- treat with chemotherapy (e.g. methotrexate or actinomycin D).

### Gynecology
#gynecology
#### Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)
#gynecology/pelvic-inflammatory-disease
- Ascending infection of upper genital tract, often from N. gonorrhoeae and C. trachomatis. Symptoms: lower abdominal pain, fever, cervical motion tenderness (Chandelier sign), adnexal tenderness, and mucopurulent cervical discharge. 
- Treatment: Outpatient – IM ceftriaxone(for gonorrhea) plus oral doxycyclinefor 14 days (for chlamydia). Inpatient (for severe cases or abscess): IV cefoxitin (or cefotetan) plus IV doxycycline. Untreated PID can lead to infertility, ectopic pregnancy, chronic pelvic pain, or tubo-ovarian abscess.
#### Sexually Transmitted Infections
#gynecology/STI #chlamydia #gonorrhea #trichomoniasis #syphilis #genital-herpes #HPV
- Chlamydia trachomatis: Often asymptomatic or causes cervicitis (discharge, friable cervix). NAAT testing is diagnostic. 
	- Treat with azithromycin 1g once (or doxycycline 7 days). Co-treat for gonorrhea due coinfection (ceftriaxone).
- Gonorrhea: Purulent discharge, can cause urethritis, cervicitis, PID. Treat with IM ceftriaxone. 
	- Co-treat chlamydia (doxy or azithro).
- Trichomoniasis: Protozoan, causes frothy green discharge, vaginal irritation, “strawberry” cervix. Diagnose on wet mount (motile trichomonads). 
	- Treat both patient and partner with metronidazole 2g once.
- Syphilis: Primary – painless chancre; _Secondary_ – diffuse rash including palms/soles, condyloma lata; Tertiary _– gumma, tabes dorsalis, Argyll Robertson pupil, aortitis. 
	- Diagnosis: RPR/VDRL screening, confirm with FTA-ABS. Treatment:IM penicillin G(long-acting benzathine) – single dose for primary/secondary, weekly ×3 for latent, IV penicillin for neurosyphilis. Desensitize if penicillin-allergic (especially in pregnancy).
- Genital Herpes (HSV-2):_Painful vesicular lesions on erythematous base, often with systemic symptoms in primary infection. 
	- Diagnosis: PCR or viral culture from lesion. 
	- Treatment:Acyclovir (or valacyclovir) – episodic treatment shortens outbreak, daily suppressive therapy reduces recurrences and transmission. In pregnancy with active lesions near delivery, do C-section.
- HPV: Causes genital warts (types 6,11) – soft, cauliflower-like lesions (condyloma acuminata); 
	- treat with cryotherapy, trichloroacetic acid, or excision. High-risk types (16,18) cause cervical dysplasia/cancer – prevent with HPV vaccinerecommended for boys and girls age 11-12 (can give up to 26; approved up to 45).
#### Contraception
#gynecology/contraception #progestin #IUD #OCP
- **Combined Estrogen-Progestin Pills/Patch/Ring:** Very effective when used correctly. Contraindications: smokers ≥35 (↑risk of thrombus), history of thromboembolism, stroke, estrogen-dependent tumor, migraine with aura. Non-contraceptive benefits: regulates menses, less dysmenorrhea, improves acne.
- **Progestin-only methods:** “Mini-pill” (for breastfeeding women or those who can’t take estrogen, must be taken same time daily), Depo-Provera injection (every 3 months, can cause weight gain and reversible bone density loss), Etonogestrel implant (Implanon/Nexplanon, effective for 3 years).
- **Intrauterine Device (IUD)**: Copper IUD(ParaGard) – lasts 10 years, no hormones, can cause heavier menses; also effective as emergency contraception if inserted within 5 days. Levonorgestrel IUD(Mirena etc.) – 3-5 years, lightens or stops menses, good for dysmenorrhea or fibroids. IUDs contraindicated if active pelvic infection or unexplained uterine bleeding.
- **Barrier**: Condoms (male or female) – prevent STIs, user-dependent effectiveness; Diaphragm – must be used with spermicide, fitted by provider, risk of UTIs and must remain 6-8h after intercourse.
- **Sterilization**: Tubal ligation for women (permanent, surgical; risk of ectopic if failure), Vasectomy for men (office procedure, semen analysis required to confirm azoospermia at ~3 months).
- **Emergency Contraception**: High-dose levonorgestrel pill (Plan B) within 72h, or ulipristal (Ella, up to 5 days), or copper IUD within 5 days.
#### Cervical Cancer
#oncology/cervical-cancer #gynecology/cervical-cancer  #HPV-vaccine #HPV 
- Almost all due to high-risk HPV (16,18). Presents with irregular or postcoital bleeding or abnormal Pap smear. 
- Screening: Begin Pap smear at 21 years. Ages 21-29: Pap every 3 years. Ages 30-65: Pap + HPV co-testing every 5 years (preferred) or Pap alone every 3 years. Abnormal results are evaluated with colposcopy and biopsy. 
- Management of cervical neoplasia: Cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) II or III lesions require excision (LEEP or cone). Invasive cervical cancer is treated with hysterectomy or chemoradiation depending on stage. 
- Prevention: HPV vaccine (covers 16,18 and more) drastically reduces risk.
#### Endometrial Cancer
#gyncecology/endometrial-cancer #oncology/endometrial-cancer
- Most common gynecologic cancer (especially in postmenopausal women). Risk factors: unopposed estrogen exposure (obesity, nulliparity, late menopause, PCOS, tamoxifen). Presents with postmenopausal bleeding. 
- Workup: Endometrial biopsy is the diagnostic test of choice for any PMB. 
- Treatment: Hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy is standard; add lymph node dissection or radiation based on stage. In young patients who desire fertility and have early endometrial carcinoma, high-dose progestin therapy is an option.
#### Ovarian Torsion
#gynecology/ovarian-torsion
- Twisting of ovary (and often fallopian tube), cutting off blood supply. Presents with sudden onset unilateral pelvic pain, nausea/vomiting, often in woman of reproductive age (risk if ovarian cyst or mass >5 cm). 
- Diagnosis: Pelvic ultrasound with Doppler shows an enlarged ovary with absent blood flow. 
- Management: Surgical emergency – prompt diagnostic laparoscopy and detorsion to save the ovary. If ovary is necrotic, may require oophorectomy.
#### Ovarian Masses
#gynecology/ovarian-cancer #CA-125
- Ovarian Cancer: Leading cause of gynecologic cancer death (often presents late with abdominal bloating, ascites). Most are epithelial carcinomas in postmenopausal women. 
	- Management: Exploratory laparotomy for diagnosis, surgical staging and debulking, plus chemotherapy (platinum-based). Tumor marker CA-125 is used to follow treatment (and in evaluating adnexal masses in older women). 
- Adnexal Mass Evaluation: Premenopausal women often have benign cysts – if simple <5 cm, observe; if >5 cm or complex, further imaging and possibly surgical evaluation. Postmenopausal women with any solid ovarian mass or elevated CA-125 should prompt oncologic referral.
#### Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)
#gynecology/PCOS
- Characterized by anovulation (oligomenorrhea), hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, acne), and polycystic ovaries on ultrasound. Associated with insulin resistance (many are overweight) and metabolic syndrome. 
- Management: Weight loss and exercise (first-line). For menstrual regulation and hirsutism, use combined OCPs (suppress ovarian androgen production). For hirsutism, can add spironolactone (after OCPs). If fertility desired, induce ovulation with clomipheneor letrozole. Metformin can help metabolic aspects and may induce ovulation in some. PCOS women should be screened for diabetes and dyslipidemia; they have increased risk of endometrial hyperplasia/cancer due to unopposed estrogen (progesterone via OCP or cyclic progestin protects endometrium).
#### Fibroids (Uterine Leiomyomas)
#gynecology/fibroids
- Benign uterine smooth muscle tumors, estrogen sensitive. Often cause heavy menstrual bleeding, pelvic pressure, and reproductive issues (infertility, recurrent miscarriage) depending on location. Exam: enlarged, irregular uterus. Diagnosis:Pelvic ultrasound. 
- Treatment: If asymptomatic, observe. For heavy bleeding or bulk symptoms: options include NSAIDs and tranexamic acid for bleeding, GnRH analogs (leuprolide) to shrink fibroids temporarily, or surgical treatment. Fertility-sparing surgery: myomectomy; definitive treatment: hysterectomy (most common indication for hysterectomy). Uterine artery embolization is another option to shrink fibroids for those who desire uterine preservation but not necessarily fertility.
#### Endometriosis
#gynecology/endometriosis
- Ectopic endometrial tissue outside uterus (ovaries common – “chocolate cysts”), causing chronic pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, and possibly infertility. Diagnosis:Definitive by laparoscopic visualization of implants (blue-black or “powder burn” lesions) and adhesions; ovary endometrioma appears as a chocolate cyst. 
- Treatment: NSAIDs and combined OCPs are first-line to suppress cycles and relieve pain. If not effective, GnRH agonists (leuprolide) or danazol can reduce lesions. Definitive treatment is surgical (laparoscopic excision/ablation of implants for fertility, or hysterectomy with oophorectomy if no future fertility desired).
#### Menopause
#gynecology/menopause
- Cessation of menses for 12 months, typically around age 50-52 (earlier if smoker or surgery). Symptoms due to estrogen withdrawal: hot flashes, night sweats, mood changes, vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, osteoporosis (accelerated bone loss), increased LDL. 
- Management: Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) is most effective for hot flashes – use lowest effective estrogen/progestin dose for shortest duration possible due to risks (↑breast cancer, thrombosis). Contraindicated if history of breast cancer or clot. Non-hormonal options for hot flashes include SSRIs or SNRIs, gabapentin, or clonidine. Treat vaginal atrophy with topical estrogen if isolated. Ensure calcium/Vit D intake and advise DEXA scan at 65 to assess bone density; treat osteoporosis with bisphosphonates or other agents as indicated.
## Psychiatry
#psychiatry
#### Major Depressive Disorder (MDD)
#psychiatry/major-depressive-disorder #SIGECAPS
- Depressed mood or loss of interest (anhedonia) ≥ 2 weeksplus ≥5 of SIGECAPS (Sleep change, Interest loss, Guilt, Energy low, Concentration poor, Appetite change, Psychomotor changes, Suicidal ideation). No history of mania. 
- Treatment: SSRIs(e.g. sertraline, citalopram) are first-line; SNRIs, bupropion, mirtazapine are alternatives based on symptoms. Psychotherapy (CBT) is effective either alone (mild depression) or combined with meds (moderate-severe). For treatment-resistant or severe depression with psychotic features or urgent suicidality, ECT(electroconvulsive therapy) is highly effective. _Suicide risk:_Always assess; if patient has intent and plan, ensure safety (possible hospitalization)
#### Bipolar Disorder
#psychiatric/bipolar-disorder
- Bipolar I: at least one manic episode(≥1 week of abnormally elevated or irritable mood and increased energy, with ≥3 of DIGFAST: distractibility, impulsivity/indiscretion, grandiosity, flight of ideas, ↑activity, ↓ need for sleep, talkativeness). Often cycles with depressive episodes (not required for diagnosis). 
- Bipolar II: at least one hypomanic episode (less severe, ≥4 days, no psychosis) and at least one major depressive episode. 
- Management: Mood stabilizers are first-line – Lithium(reduces suicide risk, but monitor levels, kidney and thyroid function), valproate(especially for acute mania, teratogenic), or carbamazepine/lamotrigine. Atypical antipsychotics (e.g. quetiapine, lurasidone) are effective, especially in bipolar depression and acute mania (many mania patients need both a mood stabilizer and antipsychotic). Antidepressants can trigger mania, so use cautiously and always with a mood stabilizer on board if at all. Psychotherapy and psychoeducation also play a role in management.
#### Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)
#psychiatry/generalized-anxiety-disorder
- Excessive, uncontrollable worry about multiple things more days than not for ≥6 months, with symptoms like restlessness, fatigue, muscle tension, sleep disturbance. 
- Treatment: SSRIs or SNRIs and buspironeare first-line pharmacotherapy (buspirone specifically for GAD, non-sedating and no dependence). Cognitive Behavioral Therapy is very effective. Short-term benzodiazepines can be used while waiting for SSRIs to take effect or for acute relief, but avoid long-term due to dependency risk.
#### Panic Disorder
#psychiatry/panic-disorder
- Recurrent unexpected panic attacks(abrupt surges of intense fear/discomfort with palpitations, sweating, chest pain, shortness of breath, dizziness, fear of dying or losing control, etc.), plus persistent concern about additional attacks or maladaptive behavior changes (avoiding situations). 
- Acute management of panic attack:Benzodiazepines(e.g. alprazolam) can abort attacks quickly. 
- Long-term treatment: SSRIs/SNRIsor TCAsfor prevention, and CBT (especially with exposure therapy) to reduce panic frequency and address agoraphobia (fear/avoidance of places where escape is difficult, common in panic disorder).
#### Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)
#psychiatry/PTSD #prazosin
- Follows a life-threatening trauma. Symptoms ≥1 month: intrusivememories/flashbacks or nightmares, avoidance of reminders, negative mood/cognitions(guilt, detachment), and hyperarousal (hypervigilance, startle response, insomnia). 
- Treatment: Trauma-focused psychotherapy(e.g. cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure therapy) is first-line. SSRIs or SNRIs are first-line medications (e.g. sertraline, paroxetine are FDA-approved). Prazosin can help reduce nightmares. Avoid benzodiazepines (not effective for core symptoms and risk addiction). _Acute stress disorder_is PTSD-like symptoms <1 month – treat with supportive counseling, as many recover, but therapy can help prevent progression to PTSD.
#### Obsessive–Compulsive Disorder (OCD)
#psychiatry/OCD
- Characterized by obsessions(recurrent intrusive thoughts, urges, or images causing anxiety, e.g. contamination, doubts) and/or compulsions(repetitive behaviors or mental acts performed to reduce anxiety, e.g. excessive handwashing, checking locks). The obsessions are ego-dystonic (patient recognizes they are irrational).
- Treatment: SSRIs at higher doses are first-line (e.g. fluoxetine, sertraline). Clomipramine (TCA) is effective second-line, especially for severe cases. CBT with Exposure and Response Prevention is equally effective and often combined with medication. Severe, refractory OCD may benefit from deep brain stimulation or psychosurgery in specialized centers.
#### Schizophrenia
#psychiatry/schizophrenia
- Chronic psychiatric disorder with ≥6 months of illness including ≥1 month of active-phase symptoms: delusions(fixed false beliefs), hallucinations(usually auditory), disorganized speech, disorganized or catatonic behavior, and negative symptoms (flat affect, avolition, social withdrawal). Social/occupational dysfunction is significant. 
- Treatment: Antipsychotic medicationsare mainstay. Second-generation (atypical) antipsychoticslike risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine, ziprasidone, aripiprazole are often first-line due to lower EPS risk than first-gen (haloperidol, etc.). Clozapine is reserved for treatment-resistant cases (agranulocytosis risk). Negative symptoms respond poorly to meds; psychosocial interventions (therapy, skills training) help functional outcomes. Ensure medication compliance – long-acting injectable antipsychotics can be useful. 
- Schizophreniform is similar but 1-6 months duration; brief psychotic disorder <1 month. 
- Schizoaffective: schizophrenia + mood episode (treat with combo of antipsychotic and mood stabilizer or antidepressant).
#### Delusional Disorder
#psychiatry/delusional-disorder
- Fixed, persistent delusions (often non-bizarre, e.g. of persecution, jealousy, erotomania, grandiosity) for ≥1 month, without other psychotic symptoms and relatively preserved functioning. The patient is not obviously odd aside from the delusion. 
- Management: Difficult – gentle confrontation in therapy, antipsychotics may be tried but often have limited benefit since insight is lacking. Build therapeutic alliance; avoid directly challenging the delusion too forcefully.
#### Personality Disorders
#psychiatry/personality-disorders 
- Borderline PD: Unstable moods, relationships, fear of abandonment, impulsivity, recurrent suicidal or self-harming behavior, identity disturbance. Splitting (all-or-none thinking) is common. Management:Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) is most effective; mood stabilizers or low-dose antipsychotics can help mood lability or transient psychotic episodes.
- Antisocial PD: Disregard for others’ rights, law-breaking, deceit, lack of remorse (must be ≥18 with history of conduct disorder before 15). Often no effective treatment – set firm limits; treat co-morbid substance abuse.
- Histrionic PD: Excessive emotionality, attention-seeking, shallow relationships. Psychotherapy can help them understand underlying issues.
- Narcissistic PD: Grandiosity, need for admiration, lack of empathy, fragile self-esteem. Psychotherapy is recommended, though patients often don’t seek treatment unless they experience a blow to ego.
#### Substance Use Disorders
#psychiatry/substance-use-disorder #alcohol-withdrawal 
- Loss of control over use, continued use despite problems, and withdrawal/tolerance in some cases.
- Alcohol Withdrawal: Can begin 6-24h after last drink (tremors, anxiety, insomnia, tachycardia), seizures around 12-48h, delirium tremensat 48-72h (confusion, fever, HTN, hallucinations). 
	- Management:Benzodiazepines(diazepam or lorazepam) in a tapering dose regimen; thiamine before glucose to prevent Wernicke’s encephalopathy; treat autonomic instability.*
- Alcohol Use Disorder: Long-term management includes psychotherapy (AA) and medication like naltrexone(reduces craving, can use while still drinking) or acamprosate (glutamate modulator, for maintaining abstinence). Disulfiram (makes patient ill if alcohol is consumed) is second-line due to adherence issues.
- Opioid Overdose: Respiratory depression, pinpoint pupils, unconsciousness. 
	- Management: Naloxone(opioid antagonist) IV or IM – reverses respiratory depression (may precipitate acute withdrawal). Monitor as naloxone can wear off before long-acting opioids.
- Opioid Withdrawal: Not life-threatening but very uncomfortable: dysphoria, flu-like symptoms(rhinorrhea, yawning, lacrimation), pupillary dilation, piloerection, muscle aches, diarrhea. 
	- Management: Methadone(long-acting opioid) or buprenorphine(partial agonist) to alleviate withdrawal and maintain long-term (medication-assisted therapy). Symptomatic relief with clonidine (for autonomic symptoms), NSAIDs, antiemetics as needed.
- Benzodiazepine Withdrawal: Similar to alcohol withdrawal – anxiety, tremors, seizures, tachycardia. Treat with benzodiazepine taper(switch to long-acting like diazepam and slowly taper) to prevent seizures. 
	- Flumazenil is the antidote for acute overdose (but rarely used due to seizure risk).
- Stimulant Intoxication (Cocaine/Amphetamines): Causes euphoria, increased energy, agitation, hallucinations (cocaine bugs), sympathetic activation(tachycardia, hypertension, mydriasis). Severe cocaine intox can cause chest pain (coronary vasospasm → MI) or stroke. 
	- Management:Benzodiazepines for agitation and BP, antipsychotics if psychotic, and supportive care. Do NOT use β-blockers in acute cocaine intox(unopposed alpha causes worsening HTN). For amphetamines (e.g. meth), similar supportive care; treat hyperthermia aggressively. There’s no specific antidote for stimulants.
- Stimulant Withdrawal: “Crash” – depression, increased appetite, hypersomnia, vivid dreams. Mostly supportive management; monitor for suicidality.
- Nicotine Dependence: Nicotine withdrawal causes irritability, anxiety, increased appetite. 
	- Treatment: Nicotine replacement therapy (gum, patch), bupropion(contraindicated in seizure or eating disorder), or varenicline (partial nicotine agonist, watch for mood changes). Counseling and support programs improve quit rates.
- Cannabis: Intoxication – conjunctival injection, increased appetite, euphoria, dry mouth, impaired short-term memory. Rarely causes anxiety or paranoia. 
	- No acute management needed (supportive). 
	- Withdrawal (if heavy use): irritable, anxious, insomnia, nausea – supportive care.
- Hallucinogens: LSD – visual hallucinations, depersonalization; PCP – nystagmus, aggression, analgesia, ataxia (treat PCP agitation with benzos or haloperidol, minimize stimulation).

## Emergency Medicine
#emergency
#### Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS)
#cardiology/acute-coronary-syndrome
- In the ER, give MONA(B) – Morphine, Oxygen (if <90% sat), Nitrates, Aspirin (chewed) immediately, plus β-blocker if no contraindications. For STEMI, activate cath lab for primary PCI. For NSTEMI, arrange early invasive management and start heparin.
#### Stroke
#neurology/ischemic_stroke  #neurology/hemorrhagic_stroke 
- In ER, if suspect stroke, check glucose (hypoglycemia can mimic). Do CT head without contrast immediately. If ischemic and within window, administer tPA (ensure BP <185/110 before and <180/105 after thrombolysis, manage BP with labetalol or nicardipine drip as needed). If any signs of hemorrhage or contraindications, no tPA – give aspirin. For hemorrhagic stroke, manage blood pressure and consult neurosurgery.
#### Cardiac Arrest
#cardiology/ACLS 
- Follow ACLS protocols. 
- Ventricular fibrillation (VF) or pulseless VTach: Immediate unsynchronized defibrillation(200J biphasic), CPR, and continue shocks every 2 minutes as needed; give epinephrine 1 mg IV every 3-5 min after the second shock, and amiodarone after third shock if rhythm persists. 
- Asystole/PEA: These are non-shockable – start CPR immediately, give epinephrine IV every 3-5 min, and search for reversible causes (Hs & Ts: Hypovolemia, Hypoxia, Hydrogen ion (acidosis), Hypo/Hyperkalemia, Hypothermia, Tension PTX, Tamponade, Toxins, Thrombosis (MI or PE)). Intubation and IV access during CPR as soon as possible. Monitor capnography to assess CPR effectiveness.
#### Shock
#shock
- Hypovolemic: (e.g. hemorrhage, dehydration) – tachycardia, hypotension, cold clammy skin, flat neck veins; treat with aggressive IV fluids (crystalloids, and blood transfusion if hemorrhagic). 
- Cardiogenic: (pump failure – MI, arrhythmia, CHF) – hypotension, JVD, pulmonary edema; treat with inotropes (e.g. dobutamine) and treat underlying cause (PCI for MI, etc.), avoid fluid overload. 
- Obstructive: (tamponade, massive PE, tension PTX) – treat specific cause (pericardiocentesis, thrombolysis or embolectomy for PE, needle decompression for tension PTX).  
- Distributive: (septic or anaphylactic shock) – warm flushed skin, bounding pulses in early phase due to low SVR. Treat sepsis with IV fluids and vasopressors (norepinephrine) plus broad antibiotics; treat anaphylaxis with IM epinephrineimmediately (plus airway support, IV fluids, antihistamines, steroids). 
- Neurogenic shock (spinal cord injury above T6) causes hypotension with bradycardia and warm skin – treat with IV fluids and vasopressors; atropine for bradycardia.
- Sepsis– Life-threatening organ dysfunction due to dysregulated response to infection. Recognize early (e.g. qSOFA: hypotension, tachypnea, altered mental status). Management (Sepsis-6 within 1 hour):Secure airway as needed, give high-flow oxygen; obtain blood cultures then start broad-spectrum IV antibiotics; aggressive IV crystalloid fluids (30 mL/kg bolus for hypotension or lactate ≥4); check lactate (and remeasure); start vasopressors (norepinephrine) if still hypotensive after fluids; and monitor urine output. Source control (drain abscess, remove infected catheter) is critical. Septic shock is sepsis with persistent hypotension requiring pressors and lactate >2 despite fluids.
#### Anaphylaxis
#anaphylaxis
- Severe allergic reaction (type I IgE) with hypotension, bronchospasm, upper airway edema (stridor), urticaria. 
- Common triggers: foods, insect stings, IV contrast. 
- Treatment: IM epinephrineinto mid-thigh immediately (can repeat every 5-15 min as needed) – this is life-saving (α and β agonist: raises BP, relieves bronchospasm. Also give IV fluids for hypotension, adjuncts like H1 blocker (diphenhydramine) and H2 blocker, and bronchodilators (albuterol nebulizer) for bronchospasm. Intubation if airway swelling. IV epinephrine infusion for persistent hypotension. Observe patients for rebound or biphasic reaction (typically ≥4 hours).
#### Status Asthmaticus
#status-asthmaticus
- Severe asthma exacerbation unresponsive to initial therapy, with risk of respiratory failure (signs: rising CO₂, fatigue, silent chest). 
- Management: Continuous nebulized albuterol/ipratropium, IV corticosteroids, oxygen. If no improvement, consider IV magnesium sulfate (bronchodilator). Prepare for possible intubation if patient tires or mental status declines – use ketamine for induction (has bronchodilatory effects). Once intubated, beware of breath-stacking and high intrathoracic pressures (allow permissive hypercapnia, use lower respiratory rates to allow exhalation).
#### Hypertensive Emergency
#cardiology/hypertension 
- Severe elevated BP (often >180/120) with end-organ damage (encephalopathy, stroke, papilledema, aortic dissection, pulmonary edema, ACS, AKI). 
- Management: Admit to ICU, IV nitroprusside, nicardipine or labetalol depending on situation (e.g. nitroprusside or labetalol for aortic dissection along with beta-blockade, labetalol for stroke if needed, hydralazine often used in eclampsia). Aim to lower MAP by no more than ~25% in first 1-2 hours to avoid ischemia (exception: aortic dissection – rapidly lower systolic <120). Hypertensive Urgency: high BP without end-organ damage – treat with oral meds gradually over 24-48h.

#### Drowning/Near-Drowning
#drowining
- Manage airway and breathing (ventilation support, intubation if needed), rewarm if hypothermic, and observe even if patient initially seems fine (secondary pulmonary edema can occur – aspiration pneumonia or ARDS). Cervical spine precautions if diving injury suspected.
#### Poisoning/Overdose Antidotes
#poisoning 
- Acetaminophen overdose:N-acetylcysteine (most effective if given within 8 hours) – prevents hepatic failure by replenishing glutathione.
- Opioids:Naloxone – reverses respiratory depression and pinpoint pupils. Start with low dose if chronic user to avoid abrupt withdrawal.
- Benzodiazepines:Flumazenil – competitive antagonist (can precipitate seizures; usually reserved for iatrogenic oversedation in procedural settings).
- Organophosphates (insecticides, nerve gas):Cause cholinergic excess (SLUDGE: salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, GI upset, emesis + bronchospasm, bradycardia). Antidote: Atropine(muscarinic antagonist) in large doses + Pralidoxime(2-PAM) to restore acetylcholinesterase if given early. Also decontaminate skin (remove clothes, wash).
- Anticholinergic toxicity (e.g. antihistamine overdose):Hot, flushed, dry skin, mydriasis, delirium, urinary retention. Antidote: Physostigmine(acetylcholinesterase inhibitor) can be used in severe cases (contraindicated if TCA overdose suspected, due to seizure/asystole risk). Mostly supportive/cooling and benzodiazepines for agitation.
- Beta-blocker overdose:Hypotension, bradycardia, hypoglycemia. Antidote: Glucagon(↑cAMP independent of β-receptor).
- Calcium channel blocker overdose:Hypotension, bradycardia. Treat with IV calcium, high-dose insulin + glucose, vasopressors.
- TCA overdose:Anticholinergic signs, CNS depression, and cardiac toxicity(wide QRS, arrhythmias). Antidote for arrhythmias: IV sodium bicarbonate(narrows QRS and treats acidosis). Also manages hypotension. Benzos for seizures. Avoid flumazenil (lowers seizure threshold further).
- Carbon Monoxide poisoning:Headache, dizziness, cherry-red lips/skin, normal pulse ox but tissue hypoxia. Often from smoke inhalation or faulty furnaces. Diagnose by carboxyhemoglobin level. Treatment:100% oxygen (nonrebreather mask); hyperbaric O₂ if severe (e.g. COHb >25% or neurologic/cardiac involvement).
- Cyanide poisoning:Suspect in smoke inhalation from burning plastics or in industrial exposure. Symptoms: headache, vertigo, almond odor breath, rapid collapse. Antidote kit: Hydroxocobalamin(binds cyanide) is first-line, or sodium thiosulfate. Older kits use nitrites to induce methemoglobinemia which binds cyanide (no longer preferred due to complications).
- Methanol or Ethylene Glycol (antifreeze):High anion gap metabolic acidosis, methanol damages eyes (optic disc hyperemia, can cause blindness), ethylene glycol damages kidneys (oxalate crystals). Antidote:Fomepizole(alcohol dehydrogenase inhibitor) or ethanol as second-line, plus IV fluids and bicarbonate; consider hemodialysis in severe cases.
- Digoxin toxicity:Nausea, vomiting, yellow halos in vision, arrhythmias (e.g. AV block, atrial tachyarrhythmias with block). Antidote: Digoxin-specific antibody fragments(Digibind) in life-threatening cases.
- Methemoglobinemia:(From dapsone, benzocaine, nitrates, etc. – patient is cyanotic, chocolate-colored blood, pulse ox ~85% and doesn’t correct with O₂). Antidote: Methylene blue(reduces methemoglobin back to hemoglobin).
#### GI Bleed
#GI-bleed
- Stabilize with IV fluids and blood transfusions as needed (target Hb ≥7 in most, higher if cardiac disease). For variceal bleed, in addition to octreotide, perform emergent endoscopic band ligation. Give IV PPI for upper GI bleeds (ulcer protection). If suspect perforation (peritonitis), do emergent surgery. For lower GI bleeds, colonoscopy after stabilization; massive bleed may need angiographic embolization or surgery.
#### Testicular Torsion
#testicular-torsion
Sudden onset testicular pain in an adolescent, often with high-riding testis and absent cremasteric reflex. This is a urologic emergency – immediate surgical detorsionand fixation (orchiopexy) of both sides within 6 hours to salvage the testis. If surgical delay, attempt manual detorsion (“opening the book” rotation, usually lateral direction) but still require surgery. Do not waste time on ultrasound if classic presentation and high suspicion – proceed to OR.
#### Cauda Equina Syndrome
#cauda-equina-syndrome 
- needs emergent MRI and neurosurgical decompression to prevent permanent deficits. Presents with severe lower back pain, sciatica, saddle anesthesia, bowel/bladder dysfunction, and leg weakness. Treat as surgical emergency (usually from a massive disc herniation compressing cauda equina).

## Preventive Medicine

#### Cancer Screening Recommendations
#oncology/screening
- Colorectal Cancer: Screen average-risk adults starting at age 45 (recent USPSTF update) until 75. Preferred method: colonoscopy every 10 years. Alternatives: annual fecal occult blood or FIT, stool DNA test (q3y), CT colonography (q5y), or flexible sigmoidoscopy (q5y, with FIT in between). If first-degree relative with colon cancer <60, start at age 40 or 10 years younger than their diagnosis (whichever earlier) and colonoscopy every 5 years.
- Breast Cancer: USPSTF now recommends biennial mammography for women aged 40 to 74. Previously it was 50-74, but updated to start at 40 given rising breast cancer in younger women. Shared decision-making for earlier screening or additional MRI in high-risk (e.g. BRCA carriers). Continue until about 74 if life expectancy >10 years.
- Cervical Cancer: Screen women 21-29with Pap smear cytology every 3 years. Women 30-65:either Pap every 3 years, or high-risk HPV test every 5 years, or co-testing (Pap+HPV) every 5 years. Stop at 65 if adequate prior screening and no high-risk history. No screening if uterus and cervix removed for benign reasons.
- Lung Cancer: Annual low-dose CT scanfor adults 50-80with ≥20 pack-year smoking history, who currently smoke or quit within past 15 years. Stop after 15 years of quitting or if patient develops health issues limiting life expectancy or ability to have curative lung surgery. 
- Prostate Cancer:Routine PSA screening is not universally recommended. USPSTF grade C for ages 55-69 – individualize decision (discuss potential benefits vs harms). No screening ≥70 (grade D). If screening, do PSA ± DRE; frequency often every 2 years if PSA is low.
#### Lipid Disorders
- Start screening for hyperlipidemia around age 40 (USPSTF: adults 40-75 for consideration of statin). Many experts do fasting lipid panel in men ≥35 and women ≥45 every 5 years (younger if risk factors). Use pooled cohort equations to assess 10-year ASCVD risk and consider statin if risk ≥7.5% (moderate intensity for 7.5%-20%, high intensity if ≥20% or LDL ≥190)
#### Diabetes
- USPSTF: Screen for abnormal glucose in adults 35-70 who are overweight/obese every 3 years (ADA recommends everyone ≥45 regardless of weight, or younger if BMI ≥25 + risk factors). Use fasting glucose, HbA1c, or 2h OGTT.
#### Hypertension
- Check BP at all adult visits or at least every 1-2 years. USPSTF recommends screening starting at 18. Confirm diagnosis with measurements outside office (home or ambulatory).
#### Osteoporosis:
- DEXA scan for all women ≥65, or younger postmenopausal women with equivalent fracture risk (e.g. 10-year risk ≥9.3%). Men: no routine screening rec, but consider if risk factors.
#### Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm
- One-time abdominal ultrasound for men 65-75 who have ever smoked(≥100 cigarettes/lifetime). Not routinely for women or nonsmoking men, though some advocate for men >65 with family history.	
#### STI Screening
- All sexually active women <25: annual chlamydia and gonorrhea NAAT screen; older women if risk factors. Screen for HIV in everyone 15-65 at least once (opt-out testing) and annually if high risk; all pregnant women for HIV, syphilis, Hep B. Syphilis: screen high-risk individuals (MSM, sex workers, etc.) and all pregnant women early in pregnancy. Hep C: one-time screening for adults 18-79 (USPSTF).
#### Vaccinations (Adults)
- Influenza:Every year for all persons ≥6 months (especially important for pregnant women and chronic diseases).
- Tdap/Td:Tdap once as adult (especially for pregnant women each pregnancy at ~27-36 weeks to protect newborn from pertussis), then Td booster every 10 years. If severe dirty wound and last Td >5 years, give a booster.
- Varicella:Ensure all adults have immunity (either vaccine or history). If not, give 2 doses of varicella vaccine (unless contraindicated).
- Herpes Zoster:Recombinant zoster vaccine (Shingrix)recommended for all adults ≥50 years, 2-dose series (separated by 2-6 months). Even those who had chickenpox or prior live zoster vaccine should get it. It greatly reduces shingles and postherpetic neuralgia risk.
- HPV:Recommended for all adolescents (boys and girls) at age 11-12 (as early as 9). If not done, catch-up through age 26 (2 or 3 dose series depending on age). FDA-approved up to 45, but routine catch-up after 26 is not recommended unless risk factors – discuss individually.
- Pneumococcal Vaccines:Two types – PCV13 (conjugate) and PPSV23 (polysaccharide). As of latest guidelines, for immunocompetent adults ≥65, either give PCV20once (new conjugate covering 20 strains) or PCV15 followed by PPSV23 one year later. For adults <65 with risk factors (chronic heart/lung/liver disease, diabetes, smokers) or immunocompromised (HIV, asplenia, renal failure), earlier vaccination is indicated (schedule depends on condition – often one dose of PPSV23 before 65, plus PCV sequence at 65).
- Hepatitis B:Universal vaccination for infants and unvaccinated up to age 18. Also vaccinate high-risk adults (healthcare workers, diabetics <60, MSM, IV drug users, inmates, dialysis patients, those with chronic liver disease). As of 2022, ACIP recommends HepB vaccine for all adults age 19-59 (and ≥60 if risk factors) – essentially broadening adult HepB vaccination.
- Hepatitis A:Vaccinate those at risk (chronic liver disease, MSM, IV drug users, travelers to endemic areas) or who want it. Two-dose series.
- Meningococcal:Give conjugate (MenACWY) at 11-12 and booster at 16. High-risk groups (asplenic, complement deficiency, college dorms, military recruits, microbiologists, travelers to sub-Saharan Africa or Hajj) also need it. MenB vaccine for ages 16-23 selectively (like college outbreaks or high-risk like asplenia).
- COVID-19:mRNA or other approved vaccines for all eligible ages per current CDC schedules; boosters as recommended (guidance evolving).
#### Lifestyle Counseling
- Advise all patients on smoking cessation– offer behavioral support and pharmacotherapy (quitlines, nicotine replacement, bupropion, varenicline). Counsel on diet(limit saturated fats, sugar; eat fruits, vegetables, whole grains) and exercise(≥150 min of moderate exercise per week). 
- Weight management to achieve BMI in healthy range (18.5-24.9). Moderate alcohol use (≤2 drinks/day men, ≤1 for women) if any. 
- Safety:use seatbelts, avoid drinking & driving, gun safety (safe storage), fall prevention in elderly (home modifications, exercise). 
- Sexual health:encourage safe sex practices (condoms) to prevent STIs and contraception planning to avoid unintended pregnancies.
#### Cholesterol Targets & Statins
- For patients with diabetes (age 40-75) or LDL ≥190, or those with ASCVD, use high-intensity statin to reduce LDL by ≥50%. For primary prevention with risk >7.5%, moderate intensity. Emphasize diet/exercise for all with elevated cholesterol.
