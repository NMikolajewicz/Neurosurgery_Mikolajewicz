**Classification**

* Severity
	* Mild hyponatremia: 130–135 mEq/L
	* Moderate hyponatremia: 125–129 mEq/L
	* Severe hyponatremia (profound): < 125 mEq/L
		* give 3% saline if symptomatic
* Disease onset
	* Acute hyponatremia: < 48 hours
		* give 3% if symptomatic
	* Chronic hyponatremia: ≥ 48 hours or duration unknown**\[2\]**

**Risk factors:** plasma cell dyscrasia, jaundice, diabetes, TURP, hysteroscopy, renal failure, thiazide diuretics, heart failure, cirrhosis/ascites, SIADH
**Work-up**

* ==volume status==
* serum sodium
* serum glucose
* lipids
* bilirubin
* serum osmolality
* eGFR, creatinine
* ==urine sodium==
* ==urine osmolality==

Questions

1. acute vs. chronic?
	* 48 h chronicity threshold
	* acute = quicker reversal, fewer complications
2. symptomatic?
	* decreased LOC, seizures
	* 3% saline to treat
3. true hypotonic hyponatremia?
	* rule out hypernatremia, hypertriglyceridemia
4. volume status

Required values to determine etiology: 1) volume status, 2) urine sodium, 3) urine osmolality

==Notes:==
* urine electrolytes = kidney's volume status (RAAS activity ~ 1/renal perfusion ~ 1/urine sodium)
* physical exam = patient's volume status
* urine osmolality = ADH acting or not (ADH activity ~ urine osmolarity)
* UOsm ~ 1/UNa (normal); UOsm ~ UNa (SIADH)

**SIADH (euvolemic hyponatremia)** — Persistent antidiuretic hormone (ADH) release and water retention, not associated with true hypovolemia or reduced effective arterial blood volume. Major causes include CNS disease, malignancy, drugs, and recent surgery.

* increased ADH
* increased water retention
* euvolemia
* increased urine osmolality
* hyponatremia
* urine sodium >40 mEq/L

cerebral salt wasting (CSW) vs SIADH :

* The **urine osmolality is inappropriately high** in the presence of hyponatremia (which normally suppresses ADH release) due to increased release of ADH. This response is appropriate in CSW, due to the volume depletion, but inappropriate in SIADH.
* SIADH is euvolemic, CSW is hypovolemic.
* The **urine sodium is usually >40 mEq/L** due to volume expansion in SIADH and putative salt wasting in CSW.
* The serum uric acid concentration is typically reduced due to urinary losses, perhaps due to a putative hormone such as BNP in CSW and to volume expansion and a direct effect of ADH on the V1 receptor in SIADH

General Approach
1) – is the hyponatremia real?

	check the serum osmolality
	
	causes of hyperosmolar/euosmolar hyponatremia: elevated paraproteins, lipids, glucose, etc.
	

(2) – Now that it’s real, is it symptomatic? (i.e. is the patient seizing)

	If it’s symptomatic, rapidly correct the sodium by 1-2mmol/L until the patient stops seizing.
	
	Treatment: 200cc of 3% NaCl solution rapidly infused.
	

(3) – Now that it’s real, and asymptomatic, is it acute or chronic?

	Most of the time you won’t know whether it’s acute or chronic
	
	Acute hyponatremia: corrected acutely without risk of ODS.
	
	Chronic hyponatremia (48-72h): corrected slowly to avoid ODS.
	

(4) – Now that it’s real, asymptomatic, and chronic, Q1 - what is the patient’s volume status? Q2 - What volume status is the kidney seeing?

	Ordering the **urine lytes** answers the second question (if the kidney is seeing low volume, the urine sodium will be under 20). Diuretics will interfere with this.
	
	The rest of your **physical exam** answers the first question.
	
	Many people will order urine osmolality as well. This tells you whether ADH is acting or not (it is high if ADH is acting).
	
	An example of hyponatremia when ADH is _not_ acting would be psychogenic polydipsia, or insufficient solute intake.

THREE mechanisms that can produce hyponatremia:
(1) – free water is unable to be excreted by the kidney leading to an elevated water:sodium ratio in the body (this is the case in extreme renal failure).
(2) – Antidiuretic hormone is turned on, and is the _only_ mechanism that separates salt from water retention. With this on, water is preferentially reabsorbed by kidneys leading to an increased water:sodium ratio in the body.
(3) – there is insufficient solute to _excrete_ the free water.  Our kidneys can only dilute urine down to around 50 mOsm/L meaning that guzzling free water and eating things without any _solute_ (sodium, proteins, etc.) will invariably lead to, you guessed it, an increased water:sodium ratio in the body.
