### Etiology

* dAVFs account for ==10-15% of all cerebral vascular malformations.==
* no clear origin for most
* Possible causes:
	* dural sinus thrombosis
	* trauma
	* infection
	* prior craniotomy.
* Those dAVFs involving the larger brain veins usually arise from progressive narrowing or blockage of one of the brain's venous sinuses, which route circulated blood from the brain back to the heart.

### Pathophysiology

* main abnormality is ==connection between dural arteries and veins within the venous sinus wall==
	* connected via small vessels that are approximately 30 micrometers on average.
* In patients with a provoking event, neovascularization is induced by a previously thrombosed dural venous sinus, typically the transverse sinus.

### Borden Classification

Describes lesions based on 1) direction of flow and 2) presence of cortical venous drainage.
Behaviour is dictated by extent of retrograde flow into cortical veins.

* Type I: anterograde flow into a dural venous sinus or meningeal vein.
	* Usually benign
* Type II: anterograde flow into a dural venous sinus. However, they also have ==retrograde cortical venous reflux==.
	* high-grade lesions with aggressive behavior in 39%.
* Type III: ==direct retrograde flow== from the fistula into cortical veins, thereby causing venous hypertension.
	* They have aggressive behavior in 79%.

### Management

* The goal of treatment is to achieve a ==complete disconnection of the fistula from its venous drainage.==
