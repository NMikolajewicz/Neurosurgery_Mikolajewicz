# Peripheral Nerve — Royal College High-Yield Resource

> [!summary] Exam target
> For any peripheral nerve station, score by **localizing the lesion**, **grading severity**, **checking for recovery**, and choosing the correct **timing + operation**: observe, decompress, neurolyse, repair, graft, transfer, tendon transfer, or tumor resection.

## 1. Universal oral answer scaffold

Use this structure for **trauma, entrapment, plexus, iatrogenic injury, and tumor** questions.

| Step | Say this | High-yield details |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Stabilize/context | “I would first assess patient factors and associated injuries.” | ATLS/vascular/bony/tendon/skin injury; pain; diabetes, smoking, anticoagulation; occupational needs |
| 2. Localize | “I would localize to root, plexus, peripheral nerve, NMJ, or muscle.” | Motor map, sensory map, reflexes, provocative tests, Tinel progression |
| 3. Grade severity | “I would determine whether this is neurapraxia, axonotmesis, or neurotmesis.” | Complete deficit ≠ always transection; serial recovery matters |
| 4. Define timing/mechanism | “Timing and mechanism drive management.” | Sharp/open, blunt/traction, compression, iatrogenic, tumor, radiation |
| 5. Investigate | “I would use studies to answer a management question.” | EMG/NCS, ultrasound, MRI/MR neurography, X-ray/CT for fracture, PET/biopsy for malignant tumor concern |
| 6. Treat | “I would choose the least morbid option that restores function before motor end plates are lost.” | Observe, decompress, neurolysis, primary repair, graft, transfer, tendon/free muscle transfer |

> [!tip] One-liner
> **Peripheral nerve surgery is a race between axonal regeneration and target-organ denervation.**

## 2. Numbers to know cold

| Concept | Royal College value |
|---|---|
| Axonal regeneration | **~1 mm/day** or **~1 inch/month** after latency |
| Wallerian degeneration | Begins within days distal to axonal injury |
| EMG fibrillations/positive sharp waves | Typically appear after **~2–3 weeks** |
| Useful baseline EMG/NCS after closed injury | **~3–4 weeks** post-injury |
| Watchful waiting for closed traction injury | Reassess serially; if no clinical/EMG recovery by **~3 months**, plan reconstruction |
| Best adult brachial plexus reconstruction window | Usually **3–6 months**; avoid delay beyond **6 months** when possible |
| Denervated motor end plate viability | Best before **12–18 months**; late motor recovery poor |
| Sensory recovery | More forgiving than motor recovery, but earlier is better |

> [!warning] Trap
> A dense palsy immediately after trauma may be **neurapraxia**. Do not label it neurotmesis unless the mechanism, imaging, exploration, or serial studies support discontinuity.

## 3. Core anatomy and localization

### Connective tissue layers

| Layer | Contains | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Endoneurium | Individual axons/Schwann cells | Preserved tubes guide regeneration |
| Perineurium | Fascicle | Disruption worsens prognosis |
| Epineurium | Whole nerve trunk | Used for epineurial repair; disrupted in neurotmesis |
| Mesoneurium/paraneurium | Gliding tissue/vasa nervorum | Scarring contributes to entrapment and tethering |

### Brachial plexus map

| Level | Components |
|---|---|
| Roots | C5, C6, C7, C8, T1 |
| Trunks | Upper, middle, lower |
| Divisions | Anterior/posterior |
| Cords | Lateral, posterior, medial |
| Terminal branches | Musculocutaneous, axillary, radial, median, ulnar |

### Root screen

| Root | Motor | Reflex/sensation pearl |
|---|---|---|
| C5 | Deltoid, biceps | Biceps reflex; lateral arm |
| C6 | Biceps, wrist extension | Brachioradialis reflex; thumb/radial forearm |
| C7 | Triceps, wrist/finger extension | Triceps reflex; middle finger |
| C8 | Finger flexors | Ring/small finger |
| T1 | Hand intrinsics | Medial forearm; Horner if sympathetic chain involved |

### Nerve localization table

| Finding | Localizes to | Key differentiator |
|---|---|---|
| Deltoid weakness + lateral shoulder sensory loss | Axillary nerve | Quadrilateral space/surgical neck humerus |
| Scapular winging | Long thoracic nerve | Serratus anterior; root avulsion clue in plexus trauma |
| Shoulder external rotation weakness | Suprascapular nerve/C5–6 | Infraspinatus wasting; suprascapular notch |
| Elbow flexion weakness | Musculocutaneous/C5–6 | Biceps/brachialis |
| Wrist drop + sensory loss | Radial nerve | Level depends on triceps/brachioradialis involvement |
| Finger/thumb extension weakness, no sensory loss | PIN | Wrist extension often preserved with radial deviation |
| FPL + FDP index + pronator quadratus weakness, no sensory loss | AIN | Weak “OK” sign |
| Thenar weakness + radial 3.5 digit paresthesias | Median nerve/CTS | Thenar eminence sensation spared in CTS |
| Intrinsics + ulnar sensory loss | Ulnar neuropathy | Dorsal ulnar cutaneous helps elbow vs wrist |
| Foot drop + eversion weakness | Common peroneal nerve | Inversion preserved |
| Foot drop + inversion weakness | L5 radiculopathy/sciatic | Back/radicular pain; tibialis posterior weak |


### Peripheral nerve vs nerve root neuropathy differentials

> [!tip] Master rule
> **Radiculopathy is myotomal; mononeuropathy is nerve-territory.** Test muscles supplied by the **same root but different peripheral nerves**. On NCS, sensory responses are usually **normal in radiculopathy** because the lesion is proximal to the dorsal root ganglion; sensory responses are often abnormal in post-ganglionic plexus or peripheral nerve lesions.

#### General differentiators

| Feature | Nerve root/radiculopathy | Peripheral mononeuropathy |
|---|---|---|
| Pain | Neck/back pain; Spurling, straight-leg raise, or femoral stretch may reproduce symptoms | Local compression pain; focal tenderness/Tinel at entrapment site |
| Weakness pattern | **Myotomal**; spans multiple named nerves | Muscles supplied by one named nerve distal to lesion |
| Sensory pattern | Dermatomal; can include proximal limb/forearm/thigh | Cutaneous territory of named nerve; respects branch points |
| Reflexes | May be reduced at root-specific reflex | Usually preserved unless the mononeuropathy interrupts that reflex arc |
| SNAPs | Usually normal | Reduced/absent if sensory fibres involved distal to DRG |
| Needle EMG | Paraspinals may be denervated; abnormalities in same root across different nerves | Paraspinals normal; abnormalities follow named nerve distribution |
| Imaging target | Spine/foramen ± plexus | Entrapment site, nerve continuity, mass/neuroma |

#### Upper limb: common traps

| Differential | Root lesion clues | Peripheral nerve clues |
|---|---|---|
| **C5 radiculopathy vs axillary neuropathy** | Deltoid weakness **plus** biceps/supraspinatus/infraspinatus/rhomboid involvement; biceps reflex may drop; neck pain | Deltoid/teres minor weakness; “regimental badge” sensory loss; biceps and reflex preserved |
| **C6 radiculopathy vs CTS/median neuropathy** | Biceps, brachioradialis, wrist extensors affected; biceps/brachioradialis reflex changes; thumb/radial forearm sensory symptoms; SNAP usually normal | CTS causes nocturnal radial 3.5 digit symptoms, APB/thenar weakness, thenar eminence sensation spared; median sensory NCS abnormal at wrist |
| **C7 radiculopathy vs radial/PIN lesion** | Triceps weakness/reflex loss; may also affect wrist flexors/pronator teres because C7 travels outside radial nerve too | Radial palsy gives wrist/finger extension weakness ± dorsoradial sensory loss; PIN is pure motor finger/thumb extension weakness with normal triceps and no sensory loss |
| **C8 radiculopathy vs ulnar neuropathy** | Weakness extends beyond ulnar muscles: median/AIN or radial C8 muscles may be weak, e.g., FPL, FDP index, APB/lateral lumbricals, EIP/EPL; medial forearm sensory symptoms; ulnar SNAP often normal | Weak FCU/FDP ring-small if high lesion, interossei/adductor pollicis/hypothenar; median thenar/AIN muscles spared; ulnar SNAP/DUC abnormal depending level; focal elbow or Guyon findings |
| **T1 radiculopathy/lower trunk vs ulnar neuropathy** | Thenar/APB weakness via median nerve, medial forearm sensory loss, possible Horner syndrome if preganglionic/lower trunk | Ulnar intrinsics affected but APB usually spared; no medial forearm sensory loss; no Horner syndrome |

> [!example] C8 vs ulnar neuropathy oral pearl
> “I would look for **median-innervated C8–T1 muscles**. If APB, lateral lumbricals, FPL or FDP to index are weak, this is not an isolated ulnar neuropathy. I would also check medial forearm sensation and NCS: a normal ulnar SNAP supports radiculopathy, while an abnormal ulnar sensory response supports a post-ganglionic ulnar lesion.”

#### Lower limb: common traps

| Differential | Root lesion clues | Peripheral nerve clues |
|---|---|---|
| **L2–4 radiculopathy vs femoral neuropathy** | Back/anterior thigh pain; hip adduction may be weak via obturator nerve; paraspinal EMG changes; patellar reflex may drop | Hip flexion/knee extension weakness with saphenous sensory loss; adductors relatively spared; lesion may follow pelvic/retroperitoneal surgery, hematoma, diabetes |
| **L5 radiculopathy vs common peroneal neuropathy** | Dorsiflexion/toe extension weakness **plus inversion weakness** via tibialis posterior, hip abduction weakness via gluteus medius; back/radicular pain; SNAP usually normal | Foot drop with weak eversion but **preserved inversion**; sensory loss dorsum foot/lateral leg; Tinel/compression at fibular neck; superficial peroneal SNAP may be abnormal |
| **S1 radiculopathy vs tibial/tarsal tunnel neuropathy** | Plantarflexion/hamstring or gluteal weakness, reduced Achilles reflex, lateral foot symptoms, positive straight-leg raise; SNAP usually normal | Plantar foot pain/paresthesias, intrinsic foot weakness; focal Tinel behind medial malleolus in tarsal tunnel; Achilles reflex usually preserved in distal tarsal tunnel |

#### EMG strategy for root vs nerve questions

| Suspected root | Sample muscles from different nerves | Why |
|---|---|---|
| C5 | Deltoid/axillary, biceps/musculocutaneous, supraspinatus/suprascapular, rhomboid/dorsal scapular | Separates C5 root/upper trunk from isolated axillary neuropathy |
| C6 | Biceps/musculocutaneous, brachioradialis/radial, pronator teres/median, paraspinals | Separates C6 root from median/radial mononeuropathy |
| C7 | Triceps/radial, pronator teres/median, FCR/median, EDC/PIN, paraspinals | Root lesion crosses named nerves |
| C8–T1 | FDI/ulnar, APB/median, FPL or FDP index/AIN, EIP/PIN, paraspinals | Key for C8–T1 radic vs ulnar neuropathy |
| L4 | Vastus/femoral, tibialis anterior/deep peroneal, adductors/obturator, paraspinals | Separates root from femoral neuropathy |
| L5 | Tibialis anterior/deep peroneal, peroneus longus/superficial peroneal, tibialis posterior/tibial, gluteus medius/superior gluteal, paraspinals | Key for L5 radic vs peroneal neuropathy |
| S1 | Gastrocnemius/tibial, biceps femoris/sciatic, gluteus maximus/inferior gluteal, paraspinals | Separates S1 root from tibial neuropathy |

## 4. Injury classification

### Seddon

| Type | Pathology | Prognosis | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Neurapraxia** | Segmental demyelination/conduction block; axon intact | Excellent; days–weeks to a few months | Observe, remove compression |
| **Axonotmesis** | Axon disrupted; connective tissue scaffold partly/fully preserved | Regeneration possible | Observe initially if closed; serial exam/EMG; explore if no recovery |
| **Neurotmesis** | Complete disruption of axon and supporting tissue | No meaningful spontaneous recovery | Surgical repair/reconstruction |

### Sunderland

| Grade | Structural injury | Seddon equivalent | Prognosis/pearl |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Myelin only | Neurapraxia | Conduction block; no Wallerian degeneration |
| II | Axon disrupted; endoneurium intact | Axonotmesis | Good regeneration |
| III | Endoneurium disrupted; perineurium intact | Axonotmesis | Variable; misdirection/scarring |
| IV | Perineurium disrupted; epineurium intact | Severe axonotmesis | Neuroma-in-continuity; often needs surgery |
| V | Complete transection | Neurotmesis | Repair/graft/transfer |
| VI | Mixed grades along same nerve | Mixed | Patchy recovery; intra-op NAP useful |

> [!tip] Exam pearl
> Prognosis worsens as injury progresses from **myelin → axon → endoneurium → perineurium → epineurium**.

## 5. Assessment and investigations

### Bedside assessment

- Document **MRC motor grade** for each key muscle.
- Map sensory loss: light touch, pinprick, two-point discrimination when relevant.
- Check reflexes and upper motor neuron signs to exclude central lesions.
- Look for autonomic/trophic signs: sweating, colour, temperature, ulcers.
- Palpate for mass, tenderness, nerve subluxation, and a **Tinel sign**.
- Follow the **advancing Tinel**: distal migration suggests regeneration.
- Examine joints and tendons: contractures and tendon injuries can mimic nerve palsy.

### EMG/NCS

| Study finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Conduction block across lesion | Demyelination/neurapraxia |
| Low CMAP amplitude | Motor axonal loss |
| Low SNAP amplitude | Post-ganglionic lesion |
| Preserved SNAP despite sensory loss | Preganglionic/root lesion |
| Fibrillations/positive sharp waves | Active denervation |
| Nascent/reinnervating motor units | Recovery underway |
| No voluntary motor units | Severe axonal injury or poor activation |
| Paraspinal denervation | Radiculopathy/root lesion |

### Preganglionic vs postganglionic plexus injury

| Feature | Preganglionic root avulsion | Postganglionic plexus/nerve injury |
|---|---|---|
| SNAP | Preserved | Reduced/absent |
| Paraspinal denervation | Present | Absent |
| Tinel | Usually absent | May be present and progress |
| MRI | Pseudomeningocele/root avulsion signs | Neuroma/rupture/edema in plexus |
| Direct repair/graft | Not possible | Possible |
| Reconstruction | Nerve transfer | Repair, graft, transfer |

### Imaging

| Modality | Best use | Exam wording |
|---|---|---|
| X-ray/CT | Fracture, dislocation, foreign body, callus | “I would image associated bony injury.” |
| Ultrasound | Superficial nerve continuity, neuroma, entrapment, dynamic subluxation, guided injection | Fast, dynamic, operator-dependent |
| MRI/MR neurography | Plexus/root avulsion, deep entrapment, nerve tumor, denervated muscle | Shows continuity, caliber, T2 signal, fascicular pattern, muscle edema/atrophy |
| PET/CT | Suspected MPNST | Adjunct for malignant transformation; does not replace biopsy when indicated |

## 6. Timing and management after nerve injury

### Decision algorithm

| Scenario | Management |
|---|---|
| Open sharp laceration with nerve deficit | Early exploration and repair if clean and tension-free |
| Open contaminated/crush wound | Debride, tag nerve ends, delayed repair/graft once zone of injury declared |
| Vascular injury requiring repair | Explore nerve at same setting if safe |
| Closed blunt/traction palsy | Observe initially with serial exam; baseline EMG/NCS at 3–4 weeks |
| No clinical/EMG recovery by ~3 months | Consider exploration/reconstruction |
| Imaging shows transection/compressive lesion | Earlier exploration |
| Progressive neurologic deficit or compressive hematoma | Urgent decompression/exploration |
| Late presentation >12 months with motor deficit | Tendon transfer, free functional muscle transfer, arthrodesis, bracing; nerve reconstruction less reliable |

### Reconstruction ladder

1. **External neurolysis** — intact nerve compressed/tethered; positive NAP across lesion.
2. **Internal neurolysis** — selected intraneural scarring; risk of fascicular injury.
3. **Primary repair** — clean transection, minimal gap, **no tension**.
4. **Interposition autograft** — gap after trimming to healthy fascicles.
5. **Nerve transfer** — proximal stump unavailable, root avulsion, long regeneration distance, or need to reinnervate close to target.
6. **Tendon/free muscle transfer** — late denervation or failed nerve reconstruction.

### Intraoperative nerve action potentials (NAP)

| NAP across lesion | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Present | Some axons crossing; recovery potential | Neurolysis/observe depending context |
| Absent | No meaningful conduction through lesion | Resect neuroma-in-continuity and graft/transfer |

> [!warning] Do not wait forever
> Motor recovery is limited by distance and time. A proximal injury to a distal target may not recover even if axons regenerate at 1 mm/day, because **motor end plates degenerate**.

## 7. Principles of repair, grafting, and transfer

### Primary nerve repair

**Requirements**

- Healthy trimmed nerve ends.
- Tension-free coaptation through full passive range of motion.
- Microscopic technique, minimal handling, meticulous hemostasis.
- Correct orientation and fascicular/group fascicular alignment when possible.
- Few precise epineurial or group fascicular sutures; fibrin glue may be adjunct.
- Protect repair post-op with splinting/immobilization when appropriate.

**Contraindication to direct repair:** tension. If tension is present, use a graft.

### Nerve grafting

| Donor | Use/pearl |
|---|---|
| Sural nerve | Workhorse; long expendable sensory graft |
| Medial antebrachial cutaneous | Useful upper-limb donor |
| Lateral antebrachial cutaneous | Alternative upper-limb donor |
| Superficial radial sensory | Selected short sensory grafts |

**Graft pearls**

- Autograft remains the standard for major mixed/motor gaps.
- Cable graft large nerves; reverse graft orientation if needed to reduce branching loss.
- Match diameter and fascicular groups.
- Outcomes worsen with long gaps, proximal injuries, delay, mixed motor/sensory nerves, smoking/diabetes, and older age.
- Conduits/allografts are mainly for selected short gaps, especially sensory nerves; be cautious for long mixed motor gaps.

### Nerve transfers

**Principle:** expendable, redundant donor → critical recipient, **close to target**, preferably synergistic.

| Goal | Common transfer |
|---|---|
| Shoulder abduction | Triceps motor branch → axillary nerve |
| Shoulder external rotation/stability | Spinal accessory → suprascapular nerve |
| Elbow flexion | Ulnar fascicle → biceps branch (**Oberlin**) |
| Elbow flexion | Median fascicle → brachialis branch |
| Elbow flexion in global palsy | Intercostals → musculocutaneous/biceps branch |
| High ulnar palsy/intrinsics | AIN → ulnar motor branch “supercharge” end-to-side |
| Femoral function | Obturator branch → femoral branch |

**Counselling:** donor weakness, incomplete recovery, cortical re-education, long rehab, realistic MRC goals.

## 8. Brachial plexus injuries

### Adult traumatic brachial plexus injury

**Mechanisms**

- High-energy traction: motorcycle/MVC, shoulder depression + neck lateral flexion.
- Penetrating trauma: laceration, gunshot.
- Iatrogenic: positioning, surgery, regional anesthesia.

### Patterns

| Pattern | Roots | Findings |
|---|---|---|
| Upper trunk / Erb | C5–C6 | Weak shoulder abduction/external rotation, elbow flexion; lateral arm/forearm sensory loss |
| Middle trunk | C7 | Triceps/finger extension deficits |
| Lower trunk / Klumpke | C8–T1 | Intrinsic hand weakness, finger flexion weakness, ulnar sensory loss; may have Horner |
| Pan-plexus | C5–T1 | Flail insensate limb; severe pain suggests avulsion |

### Root avulsion clues

- Horner syndrome: ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis.
- Severe deafferentation pain.
- Scapular winging: long thoracic/C5–7.
- Rhomboid weakness: dorsal scapular/C5.
- Paraspinal denervation on EMG.
- Preserved SNAP despite sensory loss.
- Pseudomeningocele or absent rootlets on MRI/CT myelography.

### Management priorities

1. **Elbow flexion**.
2. Shoulder stability, abduction, and external rotation.
3. Protective sensation and pain control.
4. Hand function if feasible.

### Adult BPI oral script

> “I would first determine whether the lesion is preganglionic or postganglionic using exam, MRI/MR neurography and EMG/NCS. Root avulsion is suggested by Horner syndrome, pseudomeningocele, preserved SNAPs, and paraspinal/rhomboid/serratus denervation. For closed traction injuries, I would obtain baseline studies at about 3–4 weeks and follow closely. If there is no meaningful recovery by about 3 months, I would plan reconstruction, ideally within 3–6 months, prioritizing elbow flexion and shoulder stability.”

### Obstetric brachial plexus palsy — minimum to know

| Point | High-yield content |
|---|---|
| Common pattern | Upper plexus C5–6 ± C7; shoulder adduction/internal rotation, elbow extension/pronation |
| Natural history | Many improve, but persistent antigravity biceps deficit is concerning |
| Investigations | Mostly clinical; imaging/EMG selectively |
| Surgical trigger | Poor recovery by ~3–6 months, especially absent biceps recovery; centre-specific thresholds |
| Reconstruction | Neurolysis, grafting, nerve transfers; later tendon transfers for shoulder imbalance |

## 9. Entrapment neuropathies

### Median nerve

#### Carpal tunnel syndrome

| Category | High-yield details |
|---|---|
| Site | Median nerve under transverse carpal ligament |
| Symptoms | Nocturnal paresthesias; numbness thumb, index, middle, radial ring; hand clumsiness |
| Motor | Thenar weakness/atrophy; APB most useful |
| Sensory pearl | Thenar eminence sensation spared because palmar cutaneous branch arises proximal to tunnel |
| Tests | Durkan/compression, Phalen, Tinel; NCS if atypical/severe/pre-op confirmation |
| Non-op | Night neutral wrist splint; steroid injection can help short term but not durable |
| Operative indications | Persistent symptoms despite conservative care, severe NCS, denervation, thenar atrophy, fixed sensory loss |
| Operation | Complete transverse carpal ligament release; protect recurrent motor branch and palmar cutaneous branch |

**Complications/traps:** incomplete release, pillar pain, scar tenderness, recurrent motor branch injury, ulnar neurovascular injury, double crush.

#### Pronator syndrome

| Feature | Pronator syndrome | CTS |
|---|---|---|
| Pain | Proximal volar forearm pain common | Wrist/hand; may radiate proximally |
| Nocturnal symptoms | Less common | Common |
| Thenar eminence sensation | May be involved | Spared |
| Compression sites | Lacertus fibrosus, pronator teres heads, FDS arch | Carpal tunnel |
| Treatment | Activity modification, therapy; decompression if refractory | Splint/injection/release |

#### Anterior interosseous nerve syndrome

- Pure motor median branch.
- Weak **FPL**, **FDP index/middle**, **pronator quadratus**.
- Cannot make a normal “OK” sign.
- No sensory loss.
- Consider neuritis/Parsonage-Turner vs compression; many observed initially, decompress if persistent/compressive lesion.

---

### Ulnar nerve

#### Cubital tunnel syndrome

| Category | High-yield details |
|---|---|
| Sites | Arcade of Struthers, medial intermuscular septum, cubital tunnel/Osborne ligament, FCU aponeurosis |
| Symptoms | Ring/small finger numbness, worse with elbow flexion; clumsiness |
| Motor | FDI weakness, intrinsic wasting, Froment, Wartenberg |
| Non-op | Avoid prolonged flexion/pressure; night extension splint |
| Surgery | In situ decompression, anterior transposition, medial epicondylectomy |
| Transposition favoured when | Nerve subluxation/instability, deformity, revision, significant scarring/compression requiring move |

#### Guyon canal syndrome

| Zone | Fibres affected | Deficit |
|---|---|---|
| Zone 1 | Mixed motor + sensory | Intrinsic weakness + palmar ulnar sensory loss |
| Zone 2 | Deep motor branch | Motor only |
| Zone 3 | Superficial sensory branch | Sensory only |

**Causes:** ganglion, hook of hamate fracture, cyclist handlebar compression, ulnar artery thrombosis.

**Localization pearl:** dorsal ulnar cutaneous sensation is **spared** in Guyon canal because it branches proximal to the wrist.

**Ulnar paradox:** clawing can be worse in a **low** ulnar lesion because FDP remains intact.

### Radial nerve

| Syndrome | Findings | Management pearl |
|---|---|---|
| Spiral groove palsy | Wrist/finger drop, dorsoradial sensory loss; triceps often spared | Humeral shaft fracture/compression; observe many closed palsies, explore open/penetrating/progressive or after fixation if entrapped |
| PIN syndrome | Finger/thumb extension weakness; wrist extension preserved with radial deviation; no sensory loss | Compression at arcade of Frohse/supinator; image if mass; decompression if refractory |
| Radial tunnel syndrome | Lateral proximal forearm pain; pain with resisted supination or middle finger extension; no objective weakness | Pain diagnosis; rule out lateral epicondylitis/PIN palsy |

### Lower limb and other compressions

| Syndrome | Site | Findings | Key differentiator/management |
|---|---|---|---|
| Common peroneal neuropathy | Fibular neck | Foot drop, weak eversion, dorsum foot sensory loss | Inversion preserved; remove compression, AFO, decompress if persistent/structural |
| Tarsal tunnel | Tibial nerve under flexor retinaculum | Plantar pain/paresthesias, worse standing/walking | Look for ganglion, varicosities, tenosynovitis, trauma |
| Meralgia paresthetica | Lateral femoral cutaneous near ASIS/inguinal ligament | Pure sensory burning/numbness anterolateral thigh | Weight loss, avoid tight belts, meds/injection; decompression/neurectomy if refractory |
| True neurogenic thoracic outlet | Lower trunk compression, often cervical rib/fibrous band | C8–T1 pain/paresthesia, intrinsic atrophy, medial forearm sensory symptoms | Rare; PT first; surgery selected cases after objective confirmation |

## 10. Peripheral nerve sheath tumors

### Tumor differentiation

| Tumor | Pathology/imaging | Clinical/surgical pearl |
|---|---|---|
| Schwannoma | Eccentric, encapsulated, displaces fascicles | Usually benign; often enucleated while preserving fascicles |
| Neurofibroma | Intraneural; fascicles traverse tumor | More infiltrative; resection may require sacrificing involved fascicles |
| Plexiform neurofibroma | Multinodular “bag of worms”; NF1 association | Malignant transformation risk |
| MPNST | Painful enlarging mass, neurologic deficit, heterogeneous/necrotic imaging, often NF1 | Oncologic workup and wide resection when feasible |

### MPNST red flags

- Rapid growth or change in known plexiform neurofibroma.
- New severe or persistent pain.
- New neurologic deficit.
- Large/deep lesion.
- Heterogeneous enhancement, necrosis, perilesional edema.
- FDG avidity.
- NF1 background or prior radiation.

### Tumor management script

> “I would assess growth rate, pain, neurologic deficit, NF history, and prior radiation. MRI defines the lesion and relationship to fascicles. A typical symptomatic schwannoma can often be removed by intracapsular enucleation with stimulation and fascicle preservation. Neurofibromas are more infiltrative and require counselling about deficit. Red flags for MPNST prompt sarcoma-style workup with MRI, PET/CT as adjunct, biopsy planning through the treating oncologic team, and wide resection when feasible.”

### Operative pearls

- Make incision to allow proximal/distal nerve control.
- Use microscope/loupes, nerve stimulation, and internal neurolysis.
- Enter through a non-functioning fascicular window when possible.
- Counsel: sensory loss, neuropathic pain, motor deficit, recurrence, malignant risk if NF1.

## 11. Neurocutaneous syndromes

| Syndrome | Peripheral nerve relevance |
|---|---|
| **NF1** | Café-au-lait macules, axillary/inguinal freckling, Lisch nodules/choroidal abnormalities, cutaneous/subcutaneous/plexiform neurofibromas, optic pathway glioma, osseous dysplasia, learning issues; important because plexiform neurofibromas can transform to MPNST |
| **NF2-related schwannomatosis** | Bilateral vestibular schwannomas classically; also non-vestibular schwannomas, meningiomas, ependymomas, juvenile cataracts |
| **SMARCB1/LZTR1-related schwannomatosis and related subtypes** | Multiple schwannomas, pain often prominent; vestibular schwannoma depends on subtype; meningiomas especially SMARCB1 |

> [!note] Naming update
> Modern terminology uses **NF2-related schwannomatosis** rather than simply “NF2.”

## 12. Classic signs and exam traps

### Classic signs

| Sign | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Froment | Ulnar neuropathy; thumb IP flexion compensates for weak adductor pollicis |
| Wartenberg | Ulnar neuropathy; small finger abduction from interosseous weakness |
| Benediction sign | Median nerve palsy when attempting fist |
| Ape hand | Median/thenar wasting with loss of thumb opposition |
| Claw hand | Ulnar palsy; worse with low ulnar lesion |
| Wrist drop | Radial nerve palsy |
| OK sign weakness | AIN palsy |
| Tinel | Irritated/regenerating nerve; distal progression suggests regeneration |
| Phalen/Durkan | Carpal tunnel syndrome |

### Trap tables

#### CTS vs C6 radiculopathy

| Feature | CTS | C6 radiculopathy |
|---|---|---|
| Neck pain | Usually absent | Common |
| Reflex | Normal | Biceps/brachioradialis may be reduced |
| Thenar eminence sensation | Spared | May be affected |
| SNAP | Median neuropathy at wrist | Usually normal in radiculopathy |

#### Ulnar neuropathy at elbow vs wrist

| Feature | Elbow/cubital tunnel | Wrist/Guyon canal |
|---|---|---|
| Dorsal ulnar hand sensation | Affected | Spared |
| FCU/FDP ulnar weakness | May be present | Absent |
| Intrinsics | Affected | Affected if motor zone involved |

#### PIN vs radial nerve palsy

| Feature | PIN | Radial nerve palsy |
|---|---|---|
| Sensory loss | No | Yes, depending level |
| Wrist extension | Often preserved, radially deviated | Weak |
| Triceps | Normal | May be weak if high lesion |

#### Peroneal neuropathy vs L5 radiculopathy

| Feature | Peroneal neuropathy | L5 radiculopathy |
|---|---|---|
| Dorsiflexion | Weak | Weak |
| Eversion | Weak | Often weak |
| Inversion | Preserved | Weak |
| Sensory | Dorsum foot/lateral leg | Dermatomal; may include medial foot |
| Pain | Fibular neck/local compression | Back/radicular pain common |

## 13. Oral exam scripts

### Acute sharp nerve transection

> “I would document pre-op motor and sensory deficits, examine vascular/tendon/bony injuries, give antibiotics and tetanus as indicated, and proceed with early exploration. Under the microscope I would trim to healthy fascicles. If the nerve ends approximate without tension through range of motion, I would perform epineurial or group fascicular repair. If there is a gap, I would use interposition autologous nerve grafting. Postoperatively I would protect the repair, start hand therapy, and follow serial exam and electrodiagnostics.”

### Closed traction peripheral nerve injury

> “In a closed traction injury, I would not assume transection. I would localize clinically, obtain imaging if there is concern for discontinuity or compression, and get baseline EMG/NCS at about 3–4 weeks. I would follow serial motor recovery and Tinel progression. If there is no clinical or EMG evidence of reinnervation by about 3 months, or if imaging shows transection/neuroma-in-continuity, I would explore and reconstruct before the optimal motor window closes.”

### Carpal tunnel syndrome

> “I would confirm typical median neuropathy symptoms, examine APB strength and thenar bulk, and use NCS when atypical, severe, or before surgery. Mild disease can start with neutral night splinting; steroid injection may be a short-term measure. Severe disease with denervation, thenar atrophy, fixed sensory loss, or failed conservative therapy should undergo carpal tunnel release with complete division of the transverse carpal ligament while protecting the recurrent motor branch.”

### Cubital tunnel syndrome

> “I would localize ulnar compression at the elbow, document intrinsic strength and dorsal ulnar sensation, and obtain NCS/EMG to grade severity and exclude radiculopathy. Mild cases can use activity modification and night extension splinting. Persistent or severe cases require decompression. I would consider anterior transposition if the nerve is unstable, there is deformity/scarring, or this is a revision.”

### Peripheral nerve sheath tumor

> See [[Neurosurgery/Peripheral Nerve/High yield.md#Tumor management script|tumor script above]].

## 14. Final 2-minute cram sheet

- **Localize first**: root vs plexus vs peripheral nerve vs NMJ/muscle.
- **Root vs nerve**: radiculopathy = myotomal weakness across named nerves, reflex/paraspinal changes, usually normal SNAPs; mononeuropathy = named nerve territory with abnormal sensory NCS if sensory fibres involved.
- **Seddon**: neurapraxia = myelin/conduction block; axonotmesis = axon disrupted with scaffold; neurotmesis = complete disruption.
- **Sunderland**: I myelin, II axon, III endoneurium, IV perineurium/neuroma-in-continuity, V transection, VI mixed.
- **Regeneration**: ~1 mm/day; motor end plates best before 12–18 months.
- **EMG/NCS**: baseline at 3–4 weeks; fibrillations ~2–3 weeks; nascent units = reinnervation.
- **Preganglionic lesion**: preserved SNAP with sensory loss, paraspinal denervation, Horner/pseudomeningocele; needs transfer not graft.
- **Closed traction injury**: observe initially; if no recovery by ~3 months, reconstruct ideally within 3–6 months.
- **Primary repair** only if tension-free; otherwise graft.
- **Nerve transfer**: expendable donor to critical recipient close to target.
- **BPI priorities**: elbow flexion → shoulder stability/abduction/external rotation → hand/sensation/pain.
- **CTS**: thenar eminence sensation spared; severe/denervation = release.
- **AIN**: pure motor, weak OK sign.
- **Cubital tunnel**: dorsal ulnar sensation may be affected; Guyon canal spares dorsal ulnar cutaneous.
- **PIN**: pure motor finger/thumb extension; radial tunnel = pain without objective weakness.
- **Peroneal neuropathy**: foot drop with preserved inversion.
- **Schwannoma**: eccentric/encapsulated/displaces fascicles.
- **Neurofibroma**: intraneural/fascicles traverse tumor; plexiform + NF1 = MPNST risk.
- **MPNST red flags**: rapid growth, new pain, deficit, large/deep, heterogeneous/necrotic, FDG avid, NF1/radiation.
