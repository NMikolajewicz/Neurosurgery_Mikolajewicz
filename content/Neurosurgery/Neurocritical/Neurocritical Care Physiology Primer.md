## Quick reference 

> [!info] Typical bedside targets (adult, generalized)
> 
> - **ICP:** normal 5–15 mmHg; treat when **> 20–22 mmHg**
> 
> - **CPP:** aim **~60–70 mmHg**, avoid **< 50 mmHg**
> 
> - **Severe TBI SBP:** keep **≥ 100–110 mmHg** (age-dependent), **never < 90 mmHg**
> 
> - **PaCO₂:** usually **35–40 mmHg**
> 
> - **Hyperventilation for ICP crisis:** temporary, **PaCO₂ ~30–35 mmHg**, avoid < 25 mmHg
> 
> - **PaO₂:** typically **80–120 mmHg**, SpO₂ **> 94–95%**
> 
> - **ICH:** rapidly lower SBP from >180 to about **140 mmHg** (if no contraindication)
> 
> - **SAH pre-secure:** SBP **< 160 mmHg**
> 
> - **SAH post-secure with vasospasm risk:** often tolerate SBP up to **~180–200+ mmHg** if needed for DCI

## 1. Hemodynamics in Neurocritical Care

### 1.1 CPP, ICP, and the "Goldilocks" zone

- **Cerebral perfusion pressure:**  CPP = MAP - ICP
- **Normal ICP:** ~5–15 mmHg
- **ICP treatment threshold:** ≥ 20–22 mmHg is generally considered elevated and warrants therapy.
- In a **normal brain**, static autoregulation maintains CBF relatively constant across **MAP ~50–150 mmHg**.
- After acute brain injury:
    - The **autoregulation curve may shift or narrow**.
    - CBF may become **pressure-passive** (see Autoregulation section).

**Implications:**
- **Elevated ICP** ↓CPP → risk of ischemia.
- **Lowering ICP** (CSF drainage, osmotherapy, sedation, paralysis, decompression) at a given MAP **improves CPP**.
- Management therefore always has **two levers**:
    1. Optimize **MAP** (volume + vasopressors).
    2. Reduce **ICP** (tiered ICP therapy).

> [!warning] CPP extremes
> 
> - **CPP < 50 mmHg** → high risk of cerebral ischemia.
> 
> - Routine attempts to maintain **CPP > 70 mmHg** with aggressive fluids/pressors **have not improved outcomes** and can precipitate **ARDS**, cardiac complications, and worsen cerebral edema.

### 1.2 CPP targets in practice

- **Severe TBI**
    - Avoid **CPP < 50 mmHg**.
    - Typical **target CPP: ==60–70 mmHg**==.
    - Avoid "super-normal" CPP (>70+) unless guided by autoregulation/brain oxygen monitoring.
- **Other pathologies**
    - **Poor autoregulation / vasospasm / malignant stroke:** may need **higher CPP** (e.g. 70–80 mmHg) if monitors suggest ischemia.
    - **Intact autoregulation, high ICP, fragile lungs:** can sometimes accept CPP **closer to 60** (or even 50–60) if brain oxygenation is adequate.

---

### 1.3 Systemic blood pressure targets

#### Severe TBI
- Even a **single SBP < 90 mmHg** is associated with **substantially worse outcomes**.
- Brain Trauma Foundation:
    - Ages 15–49 or >70: maintain **SBP ≥ 110 mmHg**.
    - Ages 50–69: maintain **SBP ≥ 100 mmHg**.
    - **Strictly avoid SBP < 90 mmHg.**
- Rationale: hypotension → ↓MAP → ↓CPP → secondary brain injury.

#### Ischemic stroke
- Large ischemic strokes:
    - Hypotension is equally hazardous – large infarcts are often **pressure-dependent**.
    - If not receiving thrombolysis or thrombectomy, many centers avoid reducing SBP below about **140 mmHg**, allowing **permissive hypertension** (within guideline limits) to support penumbral perfusion.
    - If planned thrombolysis: follow protocol-based BP thresholds (e.g. must be controlled below specific limits before/after lysis).

#### Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH)
- **Before securing the aneurysm:**
    - Aim to **reduce rebleeding risk** → keep **SBP < 160 mmHg** (or institutional threshold).
    - Avoid hypotension—rebleeding is catastrophic, but so is infarction from low CPP.
- **After aneurysm secured (clip/coil):**
    - BP strategy flips toward **preventing delayed cerebral ischemia (vasospasm)**:
        - Allow **higher SBP** (often **140–220 mmHg**, depending on patient and center).
        - Induced hypertension is a standard tool for treating **vasospasm-related DCI** if cardiac function can support it.

#### Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH)
- In acute ICH, **early rapid lowering of SBP to ~140 mmHg** (from >180) is:
    - Generally **safe**.
    - May **reduce hematoma expansion**.
- Balance:
    - Don't overshoot into hypotension; autoregulation may be impaired.
    - Chronic hypertensives may need **slightly higher MAP** to maintain CBF.

### 1.4 Systemic hemodynamics and cerebral circulation
- **Hypovolemia / cardiogenic shock / distributive shock**
    - ↓MAP → ↑risk of cerebral ischemia (especially in pressure-passive brain).
    - Volume resuscitation + **vasopressors (norepinephrine commonly first-line)** are standard to maintain MAP/CPP.
- **Septic/vasodilatory shock**
    - Sepsis can **impair cerebral autoregulation** and reduce cerebral perfusion.
    - Neuro ICU often uses **higher MAP targets (e.g. 75–85 mmHg)** than in standard sepsis care to safeguard the brain.
- **Severe hypertension**
    - If autoregulation intact:
        - Cerebral arterioles constrict → relatively stable CBF; ICP may not rise much.
    - If autoregulation impaired:
        - **CBF becomes pressure-dependent**.
        - MAP spikes → increased cerebral blood volume → ↑ICP, hyperemia, hemorrhagic transformation/edema.
- **Monitoring**
    - Continuous **arterial line**: beat-to-beat MAP.
    - **ICP monitoring** (ventricular/ parenchymal) → real-time CPP.
    - **Multimodal monitoring** (PRx/autoregulation indices, PbtO₂, TCD, NIRS) can identify **patient-specific CPPopt** instead of one-size-fits-all targets.

> [!tip] Practical hemodynamic strategy
> 
> - Aim for **euvolemia**, avoid both hypovolemia and fluid overload.
> 
> - Target **MAP** to achieve **CPP ~60–70 mmHg** in most patients, adjusting:
> 
>     - **Upward** for impaired autoregulation or vasospasm.
> 
>     - **Downward** if lungs or heart are failing and autoregulation is intact.
> 
> - Remember: **anything that lowers ICP (sedation, CSF drainage, decompression)** effectively "raises" CPP at a given MAP.

---

> [!summary] Hemodynamics – key points
> 
> - **CPP = MAP – ICP.** Low MAP and high ICP both compromise perfusion.
> 
>     - Target **CPP ~60–70 mmHg**, **avoid CPP < 50 mmHg**.
> 
> - **Avoid hypotension at all costs.**
> 
>     - Even a **single SBP < 90 mmHg** significantly increases mortality in severe TBI.
> 
>     - Maintain **SBP ≥ 100–110 mmHg** (age-dependent) in severe TBI and adequate pressure in large ischemic strokes (often **> 140 mmHg** if not lysing).
> 
> - **Controlled hypertension** is often tolerated or induced to support CPP:
> 
>     - **ICH:** lower SBP to ~140 mmHg to limit expansion.
> 
>     - **SAH:** pre-secure SBP < 160 mmHg; post-secure, allow higher SBP (often up to ~200 mmHg) to treat vasospasm-related ischemia.
> 
>     - Avoid "pressors to CPP > 70–80 mmHg" as a default—risk of **ARDS, myocardial stress, edema**.
> 
> - **Individualize BP and CPP:**
> 
>     - Chronic hypertension → autoregulation shifted right → may need **higher MAP**.
> 
>     - Sepsis and other systemic insults often **blunt autoregulation**.
> 
>     - Use invasive monitoring (art-line, ICP, CPP, PRx, PbtO₂) to titrate **vasopressors and ICP therapies together**, rather than treating them in isolation.

## 2. Autonomic Physiology in Neurocritical Care

### 2.1 Sympathetic vs parasympathetic tone

- **Sympathetic system**
    - Origin: thoracolumbar spinal cord, regulated by brainstem centers.
    - Effects: ↑HR, ↑contractility, ↑SVR via catecholamine release (norepinephrine from nerves, epinephrine from adrenal medulla).
- **Parasympathetic system**
    - Primarily via **vagus nerve**.
    - Effects: ↓HR, visceral vasodilation, "rest and digest" functions.
- **Baroreceptor reflex**
    - **Sensors:** ==carotid sinus + aortic arch==.
    - **Afferent pathways:** glossopharyngeal (==IX==) and vagus (==X==) to **nucleus tractus solitarius (==NTS==)** in medulla.
    - High BP → ↑baroreceptor firing → **inhibits sympathetic** output + **activates vagal** tone → bradycardia, vasodilation.
    - Low BP → ↓baroreceptor firing → **disinhibits sympathetic** + **withdraws vagal** → tachycardia, vasoconstriction.
- **Brain injury impact**
    - Many acute injuries → **massive sympathetic activation** with **reduced vagal tone**:
        - Elevated catecholamines.
        - Decreased HR variability.
        - Reduced baroreflex sensitivity.
    - Clinically: persistent **tachycardia, hypertension**, sometimes alternating with hypotensive "lulls."
- **Loss of sympathetic outflow**
    - High cervical cord injury → **neurogenic shock**:
        - Profound **bradycardia + hypotension** from unopposed vagal tone.
    - Brain death → essentially absent baroreflex, fixed HR/BP dependence on vasopressors.

### 2.2 Baroreflex failure: Cushing reflex and beyond

- **Cushing reflex / triad**
    - Occurs when **ICP approaches or exceeds MAP** → brainstem ischemia.
    - Sequence:
        1. Brainstem ==ischemia== → massive ==**sympathetic surge**== → systemic hypertension (to preserve CPP).
        2. ↑BP → baroreceptors trigger ==**reflex bradycardia**==.
        3. Ongoing brainstem compression → **irregular respirations**.
    - Classic triad: **hypertension, bradycardia, respiratory irregularity**.
    - This is a **late, pre-terminal sign** of herniation; by bradycardia, medullary function is severely compromised.
- **Subtle baroreflex impairment**
    - Common after TBI, stroke, cardiac arrest.
    - ↓baroreceptor sensitivity → HR and vascular tone fail to adapt properly:
        - Labile BP (sudden swings).
        - Predisposition to arrhythmias.
    - Lesions in **central autonomic structures** (insular cortex, hypothalamus, brainstem) can cause striking cardiovascular instability:
        - Right insular stroke is well-known for **arrhythmias and labile BP**.

### 2.3 Catecholamine surge and "neuro-cardiac" injury
- **Adrenergic storm**
    - Seen in SAH, massive stroke, severe TBI.
    - Dramatic ↑norepinephrine/epinephrine → systemic end-organ effects.
- **Neurogenic stunned myocardium (NSM)**
    - Transient LV systolic dysfunction mimicking acute MI:
        - ECG: deep T-wave inversions, ST changes, prolonged QTc.
        - Troponin: usually modest elevations.
        - Echo: wall-motion abnormalities **not confined to a single coronary territory** (often basal or mid-ventricular).
    - Mechanisms:
        - Direct catecholamine toxicity to myocytes.
        - Coronary vasospasm.
    - Clinical course:
        - Can cause **hypotension, pulmonary edema**, complicating neurocare.
        - Typically **reversible over days–weeks** with supportive management (inotropes, afterload control, careful fluids).
- **Takotsubo cardiomyopathy**
    - Overlaps with NSM; both are **stress-induced cardiac stunning syndromes**.
- **Neurogenic pulmonary edema (NPE)**
    - Caused by acute sympathetic discharge → severe pulmonary vasoconstriction, ↑pulmonary capillary pressure, endothelial injury → fluid transudation into alveoli.
    - Presentation:
        - Sudden respiratory distress.
        - Pink frothy sputum.
        - Bilateral infiltrates on imaging.
        - Often within hours of SAH or abrupt intracranial catastrophe.
    - Management: supportive (ventilation, PEEP, diuretics); typically resolves over a few days as catecholamine surge abates.

### 2.4 Autonomic instability syndromes

- **Paroxysmal Sympathetic Hyperactivity (PSH)**
    - AKA "==sympathetic storms==," dysautonomia.
    - Clinical pattern:
        - Paroxysmal **tachycardia, hypertension, tachypnea, diaphoresis, fever, dystonic posturing**.
        - Often triggered by stimulation: turning, suctioning, pain.
    - Epidemiology:
        - Up to **~==33% of severe TBI== survivors**.
        - Also reported after anoxic injury, stroke, hydrocephalus.
    - Pathophysiology:
        - "==Disconnection syndrome==" – **loss of cortical inhibitory control** over brainstem sympathetic centers.
    - Management:
        - Rule out mimics: **seizures, infection, withdrawal, pain.**
        - Multimodal therapy:
            - **Analgesia/sedation** (opioids, benzodiazepines).
            - **β-blockers** (e.g. propranolol).
            - Central agents: **clonidine, dexmedetomidine, bromocriptine**.
        - Episodes usually attenuate over weeks but can markedly complicate ICU care.
- **Autonomic failure**
    - **High cervical cord injury**:
        - Loss of sympathetic outflow → **neurogenic shock**:
            - Hypotension, bradycardia, warm/vasodilated extremities.
            - Requires **fluids, vasopressors, atropine/pacing** as needed.
        - Early aggressive pressor support helps preserve **spinal cord perfusion**.
    - **Brain death**:
        - Essentially absent autonomic variability.
        - BP entirely dependent on vasopressors; HR often fixed.

> [!summary] Autonomic physiology – key points
> 
> - **Baroreflex:** carotid + aortic baroreceptors → NTS → modulate sympathetic/vagal tone.
> 
>     - High BP → bradycardia + vasodilation; low BP → tachycardia + vasoconstriction.
> 
>     - Acute brain injury frequently **blunts** this reflex, yielding hemodynamic volatility.
> 
> - **Cushing's triad:** hypertension + bradycardia + irregular respirations = **late sign of critical ICP** and brainstem ischemia → treat ICP **before** this triad appears.
> 
> - **Catecholamine excess in acute brain injury** can cause:
> 
>     - **Neurogenic stunned myocardium** (transient LV dysfunction with ECG changes, troponin leak).
> 
>     - **Neurogenic pulmonary edema** (acute pulmonary edema from catecholamine-driven capillary leak).
> 
>     - Both are usually **reversible with supportive care**.
> 
> - **Paroxysmal Sympathetic Hyperactivity:** episodic hypertension, tachycardia, fever, diaphoresis, posturing (classically after severe TBI).
> 
>     - Treat with trigger minimization, analgesia, β-blockers, and central sympatholytics.
> 
> - **Neurogenic shock:** high cervical SCI or brain death → hypotension + bradycardia from loss of sympathetic tone.
> 
>     - Treat with **fluids, vasopressors, atropine/pacing**, and strict spinal precautions in cord-injured patients.

## 3. Respiratory Physiology & Mechanical Ventilation in Neurologic Injury

### 3.1 Ventilation, PaCO₂, and ICP

- **CO₂ is a potent regulator of CBF.**
    - For each 1 mmHg **decrease** in PaCO₂ → **CBF falls by ~3%**.
    - Hyperventilation → ↓PaCO₂ → cerebral vasoconstriction → ↓CBV → ↓ICP.
- **Therapeutic hyperventilation**
    - Very effective as an **acute rescue** for ICP spikes.
    - But **prolonged/prophylactic hyperventilation** can:
        - Critically reduce CBF.
        - Exacerbate ischemia, especially in already compromised regions.
- **Guideline-based use**
    - Avoid **prophylactic hyperventilation to PaCO₂ < 25 mmHg** in TBI.
    - Hyperventilation is a **temporizing measure** for refractory intracranial hypertension:
        - Typical target **PaCO₂ ~30–35 mmHg**.
        - Use **neuromonitoring** (jugular venous saturation, PbtO₂) to ensure brain oxygenation remains safe.
    - First 24 h after severe TBI:
        - CBF may be **already low** → aggressive hyperventilation particularly contraindicated.
- **Hypercapnia**
    - PaCO₂ > 45–50 mmHg → vasodilation → ↑CBV → ↑ICP.
    - Must be avoided in active intracranial hypertension.
    - In ARDS, "permissive hypercapnia" is **much less acceptable** in neuro patients; any tolerance of mild hypercapnia must be balanced with ICP control strategies.
- **Routine target**
    - Most neuro ICU patients are ventilated to **normocapnia (PaCO₂ ~35–40 mmHg)**.

### 3.2 Oxygenation targets

- **Hypoxemia is extremely toxic to injured brain.**
    - Even brief desaturation can worsen outcomes.
    - Hypoxia → cerebral vasodilation → ↑CBV and ↑ICP → plus direct ischemic injury.
- **Targets**
    - Aim for **PaO₂ ~80–120 mmHg**, SpO₂ **> 94–95%**.
    - These targets are **higher** than "permissive hypoxemia" used in some ARDS strategies.
- **Hyperoxia**
    - Very high PaO₂ (e.g. > 200 mmHg) has been associated with **oxidative stress** and worse outcomes in TBI.
    - Practical approach:
        - Use the **lowest FiO₂** that maintains SpO₂ around **95–99%**.
        - Some centers accept PaO₂ up to 150 mmHg for safety margin but avoid sustained high hyperoxia.
- **Advanced monitoring**
    - **Brain tissue oxygen (PbtO₂)**:
        - Often target **PbtO₂ > 20 mmHg**.
        - Can guide FiO₂ and CPP adjustments.

### 3.3 Mechanical ventilation strategy: "lung-protective" and "brain-protective"

- **PEEP and intrathoracic pressure**
    - High intrathoracic pressure → ↑JVP → impaired cerebral venous drainage → ↑ICP.
    - Data suggest:
        - **PEEP ≤ 10–12 cmH₂O** usually has **minimal ICP effect** in most patients.
        - Very high PEEP (> 15–20) can **raise ICP and lower CPP**, especially with:
            - Compliant lungs (pressure transmitted to thorax).
            - Poor intracranial compliance.
    - Practical approach:
        - Use the **minimum PEEP** that achieves acceptable oxygenation.
        - If high PEEP is mandatory (e.g. ARDS), counterbalance with:
            - **Higher MAP** (vasopressors) to preserve CPP.
            - Close ICP/CPP monitoring.
- **Tidal volumes**
    - Use **lung-protective tidal volumes (6–8 mL/kg)**:
        - Prevent ventilator-induced lung injury.
        - Reduce large intrathoracic pressure swings that can perturb ICP.
- **Ventilator synchrony**
    - Coughing, bucking, Valsalva on the ventilator can produce **dramatic ICP spikes**.
    - Management:
        - **Deep sedation** (e.g. propofol, opioids) for patients with significant ICP issues:
            - Improves synchrony.
            - Reduces cerebral metabolic rate.
            - Helps lower ICP.
        - **Neuromuscular blockade**:
            - Consider short-term paralysis for refractory ICP or severe ARDS requiring strict vent control.
            - Neuro exam is lost; ensure robust ICP monitoring.

### 3.4 Special situations

- **ARDS in a neuro patient**
    - Standard ARDS strategy (low tidal volume, higher PEEP, permissive hypercapnia) collides with **ICP/CPP constraints**.
    - Possible compromises:
        - Accept **mild** hypercapnia (e.g. PaCO₂ ~40 rather than 50) if necessary.
        - Use **osmotherapy, CSF drainage, or brief hyperventilation** to offset ICP rises.
        - Consider **advanced rescue** (e.g. ECMO) in salvageable brains rather than tolerating severe hypoxemia.
- **Prone positioning**
    - Improves oxygenation in ARDS but may:
        - ↑central venous pressure.
        - Impair CSF outflow.
        - ↑ICP, particularly if ICP already elevated.
    - Not absolutely contraindicated:
        - Feasible with **careful head positioning (neutral, no venous obstruction)** and **continuous ICP monitoring**.
        - However, if **baseline ICP > 20 mmHg**, proning carries high risk (many patients develop spikes > 20).
- **Airway care**
    - Suctioning, chest physiotherapy, turning:
        - Strong stimuli → ↑ICP.
        - Strategies:
            - Pre-oxygenation.
            - Briefly increased minute ventilation.
            - Sedation bolus as needed.
- **Tracheostomy**
    - Often done **early once ICP control is stable**:
        - Facilitates weaning.
        - Improves pulmonary toilet.
        - Reduces risk of ventilator-associated pneumonia.
- **Aspiration precautions**
    - Many neuro patients have impaired airway reflexes:
        - Maintain **head of bed 30°**.
        - Use **NG/OG tube** as needed.
        - Aggressive oral care and swallowing assessment.

> [!summary] Respiratory – key points
> 
> - **Avoid hypoxemia.**
> 
>     - Intubate early if airway or ventilation are unreliable.
> 
>     - Aim for **PaO₂ ~80–120 mmHg**, SpO₂ **> 94–95%**; do _not_ accept the lower PaO₂ targets used in routine ARDS unless the brain is clearly safe.
> 
> - **CO₂ management is central to ICP control.**
> 
>     - Maintain **PaCO₂ mid-30s** in stable patients.
> 
>     - Hypercapnia → vasodilation → ↑ICP: avoid unless absolutely necessary and watched closely.
> 
>     - Hyperventilation is a **rescue tool** for acute ICP crises; limit to **PaCO₂ ≥ ~30 mmHg** and avoid prolonged use.
> 
> - **PEEP and positioning:**
> 
>     - Moderate PEEP (≤ 10–12) usually OK; higher PEEP may ↑ICP, especially with poor autoregulation.
> 
>     - Elevate head of bed ~30° and keep head midline to optimize venous drainage.
> 
>     - Prone positioning for ARDS is possible but requires **tight ICP surveillance**.
> 
> - **Sedation and synchrony:**
> 
>     - Prevent cough/buck/bearing down on the vent in patients with elevated ICP.
> 
>     - Use propofol/opioids; add short-term paralysis if necessary, accepting loss of neuro exam.
> 
> - **Integrate lung and brain goals:**
> 
>     - Use **low tidal volumes** for lung protection.
> 
>     - Avoid excessive fluids (worsen pulmonary and cerebral edema).
> 
>     - Treat neurogenic pulmonary edema with **PEEP, diuresis, time**.
> 
>     - When lung and brain goals conflict, prioritize **adequate brain oxygen delivery**, using advanced support (e.g. ECMO) if the neurological injury is survivable.

## 4. Cerebral Autoregulation: Mechanisms, Monitoring, Clinical Use

### 4.1 Physiology and the Lassen curve

- **Definition:** Autoregulation keeps CBF relatively constant despite changes in CPP/MAP by **arteriolar constriction/dilation**.
- **Classic Lassen curve** (static autoregulation):
    - CBF plateaus across **MAP ~50–150 mmHg** in normotensive individuals.
    - Below ~50 mmHg:
        - Arterioles maximally dilated → further BP drop → steep fall in CBF → ischemia.
    - Above ~150 mmHg:
        - Arterioles maximally constricted → further MAP rise → ↑CBF → hyperperfusion, edema, hemorrhage.
- **Mechanisms**
    - **Myogenic** (Bayliss effect): vessel wall tension triggers smooth muscle contraction/relaxation.
    - **Metabolic**: local CO₂, H⁺, adenosine, etc.
    - **Neurogenic**: autonomic and local neuronal influences.
- **Chronic hypertension**
    - Shifts autoregulatory curve **to the right** (higher pressures needed to maintain flow).
    - Aggressive BP lowering in such patients may push them **below their lower limit** of autoregulation.
- **Dynamic autoregulation**
    - Describes how quickly CBF recovers after a sudden BP change:
        - **Intact**: transient CBF dip followed by rapid recovery.
        - **Impaired**: prolonged CBF reduction.

### 4.2 Autoregulatory failure in critical illness

- **Pressure-passive brain**
    - When autoregulation fails, CBF becomes **linearly dependent on CPP/MAP**.
    - Consequences:
        - Hypotension → direct CBF drop → ischemia.
        - Hypertension → direct CBF rise → hyperemia, edema, ↑ICP.
- **Representative conditions**
    - **Severe TBI**: autoregulation variably impaired and dynamic over time.
    - **Acute ischemic stroke**:
        - Autoregulation often lost in **infarct core and penumbra** → these regions are pressure-dependent.
    - **ICH & SAH**:
        - Early global impairment from acute ↑ICP and blood breakdown products.
        - Later in SAH, vasospasm and delayed ischemia often correspond to **regional autoregulatory failure**.
    - **Sepsis, systemic inflammation**:
        - Sepsis-associated encephalopathy often includes **impaired autoregulatory responses**, making the brain vulnerable to relatively modest hypotension.

### 4.3 Monitoring autoregulation

#### Pressure Reactivity Index (PRx)

- **Definition:** Moving correlation between slow waves of **ICP** and **MAP**.
    - **Intact autoregulation:** ↑MAP → arteriolar constriction → CBV falls → ICP stable or ↓ → **ICP & MAP move in opposite/independent directions** → PRx ~0 or negative.
    - **Impaired autoregulation:** ↑MAP → ↑CBV → ↑ICP → **ICP & MAP move together** → PRx becomes positive.
- **Typical threshold:** PRx > +0.3 often used as **impaired**.
- **CPPopt concept:**
    - Plot PRx vs CPP → U-shaped curve.
    - **CPPopt** = CPP at which PRx is lowest (best autoregulation).
    - Some centers **titrate vasopressors** to keep CPP near CPPopt.

#### Transcranial Doppler (TCD)

- Measures **flow velocity** in basal arteries.
- Dynamic autoregulation tests:
    - Induce brief BP dip (e.g. thigh cuff release, sit-to-stand).
    - Intact: flow velocity transiently dips then recovers quickly.
    - Impaired: flow velocity stays depressed.
- Metrics like **Mx** (correlation of MAP and TCD flow) parallel PRx.
- CO₂ reactivity tests (hyperventilation / CO₂ inhalation) assess vasoreactivity.

#### Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS)

- Measures **regional cortical oxygenation** (often frontal).
- Indices like **TOx** correlate NIRS saturation with MAP.
    - Positive correlation → pressure-passive behavior.
- Noninvasive and continuous but **regional and superficial**.

#### Brain tissue oxygen (PbtO₂) and metabolic monitoring

- **PbtO₂ catheter:**
    - If MAP falls and PbtO₂ remains stable → autoregulation likely intact.
    - If PbtO₂ falls with modest MAP reduction → pressure-dependence.
- **Microdialysis (lactate/pyruvate, etc.)**
    - Rising lactate/pyruvate ratio with MAP drop suggests **insufficient perfusion**.

#### Integrated multimodal platforms

- Systems (e.g. ICM+) integrate ICP, MAP, TCD, NIRS, PbtO₂, etc., to:
    - Display PRx, CPPopt.
    - Provide a **real-time autoregulation dashboard**.

### 4.4 Clinical implications

- **Intact autoregulation**
    - Brain tolerates a **broader MAP range**.
    - Rely more confidently on "standard" CPP targets (60–70 mmHg).
    - Raising BP typically **does not markedly increase ICP**, as arterioles constrict.
    - However, pushing BP very high rarely improves CBF further; systemic complications still matter.
- **Impaired autoregulation (pressure-passive)**
    - **MAP essentially becomes CBF.**
    - Management:
        - Avoid hypotension "like the plague."
        - Often benefit from **higher CPP targets (e.g. 70–75 mmHg)** until PRx/imaging improve.
        - Be wary of BP swings: hypertension can abruptly raise ICP since vessels can't constrict appropriately.
- **Autoregulation-guided therapy**
    - TBI data suggest:
        - Patients kept within ±5 mmHg of **CPPopt** have better outcomes compared to those far above or below.
    - Not yet universal standard, but trend is toward **individualized CPP** rather than blanket 60–70 for everyone.
- **Prognostication**
    - Severely impaired PRx is associated with:
        - Higher mortality.
        - Worse neurological outcomes.
    - Improvement over time can correlate with better recovery.
- **Regional loss of autoregulation**
    - **Malignant MCA infarct**:
        - Penumbra may be pressure-dependent.
        - Higher BP may help, but **decompressive hemicraniectomy** often needed to reduce ICP and restore perfusion.
    - **SAH vasospasm**:
        - Induced hypertension exploits pressure-dependent flow in vasospastic segments.
        - But intact regions may be pushed toward edema → again, balance supported by multimodal monitoring.
- **Anesthesia and sedation effects**
    - **Volatile anesthetics**:
        - Dose-dependent impairment of autoregulation.
    - **Intravenous agents** (e.g. propofol):
        - Tend to **preserve autoregulation** better, reduce CBF/CBV globally.
    - In the ICU, propofol and dexmedetomidine are favored partly for this hemodynamic/CBF profile.

> [!summary] Autoregulation – key points
> 
> - **Mechanism:** Autoregulation keeps CBF ~constant over **MAP ~50–150 mmHg** by arteriolar dilation (low pressure) and constriction (high pressure).
> 
> - **Loss of autoregulation:** Common in severe TBI, large stroke, SAH, cardiac arrest, sepsis.
> 
>     - The brain becomes **pressure-passive**: CBF rises and falls directly with MAP.
> 
>     - If ICP rises significantly with modest BP increases, suspect **impaired autoregulation**.
> 
> - **Monitoring:**
> 
>     - **PRx** (ICP–MAP correlation): positive values (e.g. > 0.3) suggest impaired autoregulation.
> 
>     - **TCD, NIRS, PbtO₂, microdialysis** all offer complementary views.
> 
>     - If available, determine **CPPopt** and aim CPP around that rather than a generic 60–70 mmHg.
> 
> - **Individualize BP and CPP:**
> 
>     - Poor autoregulation → often need **higher CPP (70–80 mmHg)** to prevent ischemia.
> 
>     - Intact autoregulation (with decent PbtO₂) may allow **lower CPP (50–60 mmHg)** in selected cases, easing cardiac/pulmonary stress.
> 
>     - Extremely low CPP (< 50) is dangerous; very high CPP (> 70–80) is often unnecessary and may harm lungs and heart.
> 
> - **Therapeutic implications:**
> 
>     - **SAH with vasospasm:** use induced hypertension to drive flow through narrowed, pressure-dependent arteries.
> 
>     - **Hypertensive ICH:** lower BP carefully; overshoot could drop CBF in territories with impaired autoregulation.
> 
>     - Future neurocritical care is trending toward **autoregulation-directed** therapy—treating each brain's pressure–flow curve as unique and adjusting hemodynamics accordingly.
