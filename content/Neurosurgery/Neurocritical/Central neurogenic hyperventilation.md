
Central neurogenic hyperventilation (CNH) is a rare respiratory pattern characterized by sustained, rapid, and deep breathing without any apparent metabolic or pulmonary cause. It arises due to disruption of central respiratory centers in the brainstem.

  

  

Pathophysiology:

  

  

- CNH is typically due to lesions in the midbrain or upper pons, particularly near the parabrachial-Kölliker-Fuse complex or periaqueductal gray, which modulate respiratory drive.
- The key mechanism is loss of inhibitory cortical input or disinhibition of medullary respiratory centers, leading to unchecked activation of the inspiratory drive.
- This leads to a hypocapnic respiratory alkalosis, sometimes severely so.

  

  

  

Etiologies include:

  

  

- Brainstem tumors (especially gliomas)
- Trauma
- Pontine hemorrhage
- Brainstem infarcts
- Large midline diencephalic lesions
- Rarely, postictal states or encephalitis

  

  

  

Clinical relevance:

  

  

- In the neurosurgical ICU, this may be seen in comatose patients with midbrain pathology, or as a harbinger of brain herniation.
- Persistent hyperventilation in an intubated, sedated patient should prompt neuroimaging to rule out structural brainstem insult.

  

  

  

Management:

  

  

- CNH is not responsive to volitional control, differentiating it from anxiety-driven hyperventilation.
- Treatment is supportive, aimed at underlying etiology. Ventilator management may require adjusting minute ventilation to avoid iatrogenic alkalosis.
- Prognosis is typically poor, depending on the underlying brainstem insult.

  

  

While the Greenberg Handbook discusses hyperventilation in ICP management—primarily as a means of acutely reducing cerebral blood volume and intracranial pressure by decreasing PaCO₂ —central neurogenic hyperventilation is a separate entity arising from brainstem dysfunction and carries diagnostic and prognostic significance distinct from therapeutic hyperventilation.

  

If you’re seeing this in a patient, it’s worth correlating with brain imaging and considering it a red flag for structural midbrain/pontine compromise.

Management of central neurogenic hyperventilation (CNH) centers on treating the underlying structural lesion—there is no specific therapy that directly suppresses this aberrant respiratory pattern. Below is a focused approach:

  

  

  

  

🧠 

1. Identify and Address Underlying Cause

  

  

- Neuroimaging (MRI/CT) is essential to localize the lesion.
- Common causes:  
    

- Brainstem gliomas
- Pontine hemorrhage
- Trauma with herniation
- Infarcts, encephalitis, or tumors

-   
    

  

  

Management depends on etiology:

  

- Tumors: debulking, biopsy, or radiation
- Hemorrhage: supportive vs. surgical
- Edema/herniation: hyperosmolar therapy, decompressive craniectomy

  

  

  

  

  

🫁 

2. Ventilatory Management

  

  

- Mechanical ventilation may be required due to excessive respiratory drive or associated neurologic deterioration.
- Avoid aggressive ventilation if the patient is already hypocapnic (PaCO₂ < 30 mmHg), as further alkalosis may reduce cerebral perfusion.
- Target PaCO₂ cautiously, usually aiming for low-normal range (~35 mmHg) to avoid cerebral vasoconstriction.

  

  

  

  

  

💉 

3. Pharmacologic Support (Limited Role)

  

  

- Sedation (e.g., propofol, midazolam) may blunt the respiratory drive, but with variable efficacy.
- Neuromuscular blockade is not typically indicated unless ventilation is severely dysregulated and other measures fail.
- In some case reports, opioids (e.g., morphine or fentanyl) have been used to suppress respiratory drive, though evidence is weak.

  

  

  

  

  

🧪 

4. Monitor for Complications

  

  

- Respiratory alkalosis → Monitor ABGs regularly.
- Hypokalemia, hypocalcemia from alkalosis → Electrolyte monitoring and correction.
- Seizure threshold may lower in severe alkalosis → Consider prophylaxis if clinically indicated.

  

  

  

  

  

📉 

5. Prognosis

  

  

- CNH typically indicates a poor neurologic prognosis—especially when associated with coma or irreversible brainstem injury.
- If reversible cause is identified (e.g., compressive lesion), prognosis depends on timeliness and efficacy of intervention.
