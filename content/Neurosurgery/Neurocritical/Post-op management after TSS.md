> [!summary] Post-TSS management (exam + bedside)
> 
> - **0–24h ICU/stepdown:** q1h neuro checks + **immediate post-extubation visual acuity/fields**; any decline = **sellar hematoma until proven otherwise** → stat CT/MRI, urgent decompression.
>     
> - Maintain **normotension** (often SBP ~110–150).
>     
> - **Sinonasal precautions:** smooth extubation; avoid cough/strain; **avoid CPAP/BiPAP/positive pressure** if skull-base defect/CSF leak risk.
>     
> - ==**DI watch:** strict I/O; suspect DI if **UOP ≥250 mL/h x1–2h + SG <1.005 (<1.003)** ± rising Na → replace free water; **DDAVP only if Na rising/can’t drink**==.
>     
> - **Triphasic risk:** DI → SIADH-like retention → recurrent DI; avoid “stacking” DDAVP.
>     
> - **POD 5–14:** delayed hyponatremia (“readmission week”) usually **SIADH** → fluid restrict; **CSW** if hypovolemic → saline/salt (± fludrocortisone).
>     
> - **HPA axis first:** AM cortisol POD1–2; replace if low/symptomatic (Cushing’s protocols may withhold steroids to assess remission).
>     
> - **CSF leak** (clear rhinorrhea/salty drip) + meningitis/epistaxis vigilance; discharge POD2–3 if stable with Na plan and hyponatremia warning.
>

## Key principles
- Detect and treat ==**acute mass effect**== (sellar hematoma → visual loss), CSF leak, vascular injury
- Prevent/manage ==**water–electrolyte derangements**== (DI ↔ SIADH/CSW; triphasic response)
- Preserve/replace ==**pituitary axes**== (HPA first)
- Safe ==sinonasal== care + mobilization + discharge planning

---
## Post-op Timeline

### 0–24 h

**Disposition**
- NICU or high-dependency observation for at least 24h (common practice; institution-specific)

**Neuro + vision**
- Hourly neuro checks initially (level of consciousness, pupils, motor)
- **Immediate post-extubation visual acuity/fields** (baseline)
    - Any decline = **treat as compressive sellar hematoma until proven otherwise**

> [!danger] Post-TSS emergency: visual decline  
> Sudden ↓vision, ophthalmoplegia, severe headache, declining mental status → **stat CT/MRI**, call MRP; prepare for urgent decompression if hematoma suspected.

**Hemodynamics**
- Maintain normotension; many pathways ==target SBP ~110–150== (balance hemorrhage risk vs perfusion; local protocol)

**Airway & sinonasal precautions**
- Smooth emergence/extubation: avoid coughing/straining ("bucking") to reduce CSF leak risk
- ==**Avoid positive pressure**== (CPAP/BiPAP; aggressive bag-mask) if concern for skull base defect/CSF leak
- Humidification often via **face tent** when desired (practice varies)

**Activity & diet**
- If no intraop CSF leak and stable: early ambulation + clear liquids within hours (typical)
---
### POD 1–3 

**Primary tasks**
- Continue **vision checks** at least daily (and PRN)
- Aggressive **I/O + sodium surveillance** ("DI watch")
- Decide **steroid plan** based on preop axis + tumor type (esp. Cushing's pathway)
- Monitor for CSF leak, meningitis, epistaxis

**Packing**
- Many surgeons keep nasal packing several days
- Greenberg notes ==**nasal packs removed POD 3–6**==.
---
### POD 4–14 (delayed complications window)

**Key issue: delayed hyponatremia**
- Classic "readmission week": nausea, malaise, confusion, seizures
- ==Usually **SIADH**==, occasionally CSW
- Many centers obtain a **serum Na check around POD 5–7** (even if discharged)

> [!warning] Delayed hyponatremia window  
> Counsel every patient: if nausea, vomiting, headache, confusion, lethargy, or seizure **~1 week post-op**, get a sodium level urgently.

---
### 6–12 weeks (recovery + definitive assessment)
- Formal VF testing and baseline post-op MRI (often 8–12 wks; practice varies)
- Full pituitary axis reassessment (HPA, thyroid, gonadal, GH/IGF-1 as indicated)
---
## Core Monitoring and Nursing Orders 
- **Neuro checks**: q1h initially → space per stability
- **Vision**: immediate post-op baseline + daily + PRN
- **Strict I/O**: hourly early; Foley often helpful if "DI watch"
- **Urine specific gravity**: q4h and whenever UOP high (Greenberg example: check SG q4h and whenever UOP >250 mL/hr)
- **Serum electrolytes + osmolality**: q6h during DI risk periods (Greenberg example)
- Daily weight (helps volume status in SIADH vs CSW)
- DVT prophylaxis: SCDs early; chemoprophylaxis timing per surgeon/bleeding risk

## Fluid & Electrolyte Management

### Diabetes Insipidus (DI)

**When to suspect**
- UOP >250 mL/hr for 1–2 hours **and** dilute urine (SG <1.005, usually <1.003) ± rising Na

**Greenberg diagnostic anchors**
- ==Dilute urine: urine osm <200 mOsm/L or **SG <1.003**==

**Post-op patterns**
- After transsphenoidal surgery, DI is often transient; avoid long-acting therapy until trajectory is clear
- Injury to posterior pituitary/stalk can produce:
    - transient DI normalizing ~12–36h
    - prolonged DI (months/permanent)
    - **triphasic response**

**Triphasic response (practical)**
- Phase 1: ↓ADH for ~4–5 days → DI
- Phase 2: ADH release ~4–5 days → normalization or SIADH-like retention
- Phase 3: ↓/absent ADH → recurrent DI (transient or prolonged)
- **Pitfall:** continuing vasopressin/DDAVP into phase 2 can cause major hemodilution

> [!danger] DI pitfall  
> Treat the number (UOP/Na/SG), not reflexively with DDAVP. Over-treatment can convert early DI into dangerous water intoxication during a SIADH-like phase.

**Immediate management**
- Replace free water losses (often 1:1 initially; adjust to sodium trend and hemodynamics)
- If euvolemic and awake: allow **PO to thirst** + measure
- If can't keep up or Na rising: consider DDAVP

**DDAVP (common dosing)**
- Intranasal DDAVP range **10–40 mcg BID PRN** 
- Use the **lowest effective dose**, reassess frequently (avoid "stacking")

practical approach
- DI often shows up early and is **best managed initially with fluid replacement**; if needed, **short-acting vasopressin** may be used to reduce risk if a SIADH-like phase develops.
---
### SIADH (delayed hyponatremia; usually POD 5–14)

**Typical picture**
- Euvolemic hyponatremia with inappropriately concentrated urine

**diagnostic criteria (high yield)**
- Hyponatremia (often <134)
- Effective serum osmolality <275
- Urine Na usually >18 (often much higher)
- No renal/adrenal dysfunction; no hypothyroidism

**Management (practical)**
- Mild–moderate, asymptomatic: **fluid restriction** (commonly <800–1000 mL/day) + trend Na q6–12h
- Severe or symptomatic (seizure, obtundation): ICU, **3% hypertonic saline** with controlled correction and frequent labs
- Avoid rapid correction (risk osmotic demyelination)

---
### Cerebral Salt Wasting (CSW) (less common after routine TSS, but must distinguish)

**Clue**
- Hyponatremia + **hypovolemia** (orthostasis, low CVP, rising BUN/Cr, weight loss)

**differentiation + warning**
- Labs can overlap with SIADH; definitive difference is **volume status**
- CVP/PCWP/plasma volume low in CSW
- **Elevated K with hyponatremia is incompatible with SIADH** (think adrenal insufficiency/CSW/other)

**Management**
- **Volume + salt repletion** (NS / hypertonic saline depending severity)
- Consider fludrocortisone in refractory cases (common practice)

## Endocrine Axis Management

### HPA axis (priority)

**General approach**
- Strategy depends on preop testing, intraop course, and tumor type
- Many pathways: ==check **early AM cortisol** POD1–2, replace if low or symptomatic==

**Cushing's disease pathway (key nuance)**
- Some protocols withhold routine periop steroids to assess remission; 
- early morning ACTH/cortisol drawn POD1–2 
	- **early remission defined by lowest cortisol ≤5 mcg/dL**.

> [!warning] Cushing's post-op  
> "No routine steroids" requires **tight monitoring** and a low threshold to treat symptomatic hypocortisolism.

**Non-Cushing patients (common practice)**
- If AM cortisol clearly adequate → no replacement
- Borderline/low or symptomatic (nausea, anorexia, headache, arthralgias, hypotension) → physiologic hydrocortisone and outpatient reassessment

**postoperative steroid use (practice variation)**
- Some surgeons routinely use post-op steroids until adequacy of endogenous steroids is established
---
### Thyroid axis
- Preop central hypothyroidism rarely "fixes" immediately; continue replacement; recheck at follow-up (often 6–8 wks)
### GH axis / Acromegaly
- Early GH can be checked (institution-specific); definitive remission usually based on IGF-1 and suppression testing later
### Prolactinoma
- Usually medical therapy primary; postop prolactin trends can help but management is typically endocrine-driven

## Surgical / Neuro Complications (what to watch for)

### CSF leak
**Signs**
- Clear rhinorrhea, salty/metallic postnasal drip, positional headache
**Initial management (typical)**
- HOB up, stool softeners, avoid Valsalva/nose blowing
- Confirm suspected CSF with beta-2 transferrin
- Consider lumbar drainage vs re-exploration depending on defect size/repair and clinical course

> [!warning] Shunting + leak risk  
> shunting in the setting of a prior CSF leak site can create ==**tension pneumocephalus**== due to air being drawn in through the former leak site.
### Pneumocephalus / tension pneumocephalus
- Worsening headache, confusion, focal deficits; consider CT
- Avoid positive pressure ventilation when possible if skull base defect suspected
### Epistaxis / vascular injury
- Profuse epistaxis, expanding nasal/oropharyngeal bleeding, new neuro deficits → emergent imaging and potential angiography (carotid injury/pseudoaneurysm is the feared mechanism)
### Meningitis
- Fever, meningismus, mental status change—especially with CSF leak
- Treat promptly; coordinate with ENT/neurosurgery/endocrine

## Discharge Criteria and Patient Instructions

### Typical discharge (POD 2–3 if uncomplicated)
- Stable neuro + vision baseline
- No active CSF leak/major epistaxis
- Sodium stable (or plan for outpatient check)
- DI controlled without frequent rescue dosing
### Must-give discharge counseling
- **Hyponatremia warning** (POD 5–14): nausea, vomiting, confusion, severe fatigue, seizure → ED
- **DI warning**: extreme thirst + high urine volumes, nocturia, dehydration symptoms
- Sinonasal precautions for ~2–6 weeks (surgeon-specific):
    - No nose blowing; sneeze with mouth open
    - Avoid heavy lifting/straining; use stool softeners
    - Avoid CPAP/BiPAP until cleared
- Follow-up:
    - Endocrinology (axis testing plan)
    - Ophthalmology/visual fields
    - Post-op MRI baseline ~8–12 weeks

## Quick Algorithms 

### "Polyuria after TSS" bedside algorithm
1. Confirm **UOP trend** (≥250 mL/hr × 1–2h) + **SG** (dilute?)
2. Check serum **Na/osm** q6h while unstable
3. If DI likely → replace losses, allow PO to thirst if safe
4. Give DDAVP only if:
    - rising Na / symptomatic dehydration **or**
    - cannot maintain intake
5. Reassess frequently; anticipate triphasic pattern risk
### "Hyponatremia around POD7"
1. Check: serum Na, serum osm, urine osm, urine Na + volume status
2. Euvolemic + concentrated urine → SIADH → fluid restriction
3. Hypovolemic → think CSW → saline + salt (± fludrocortisone)
4. Severe symptoms → ICU + controlled 3% correction (avoid rapid correction)
