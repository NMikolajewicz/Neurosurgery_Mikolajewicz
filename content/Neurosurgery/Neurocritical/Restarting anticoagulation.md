### safety of resumption

Five [systematic reviews](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/systematic-review "Learn more about systematic reviews from ScienceDirect's AI-generated Topic Pages") have examined OAC resumption post-ICH, and their findings are summarized in [Table 1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1538783623007900?utm_source=chatgpt.com#tbl1) [[2](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1538783623007900?utm_source=chatgpt.com#bib2),[10](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1538783623007900?utm_source=chatgpt.com#bib10),[[12]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1538783623007900?utm_source=chatgpt.com#bib12), [[13]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1538783623007900?utm_source=chatgpt.com#bib13), [[14]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1538783623007900?utm_source=chatgpt.com#bib14)]. Together, they suggest that resuming OAC is associated with reduced thromboembolic events (relative risk [RR], 0.31-0.56) and all-cause mortality (RR, 0.27-0.6). By comparison, the risk of ICH recurrence appears to be at equipoise, albeit with some variation between individual studies (RR, 0.89-1.14) and only one of the 5 reviews finding a statistically significant difference when compared to patients who did not resume therapy.

### Timing of resumption

long-term follow-up data indicate that the risk of recurrent ICH remains elevated for at least the first 12 months

benefit of resuming OAC in reducing the risk of vascular death and nonfatal stroke was greatest if treatment was restarted 7 to 8 weeks after ICH.

Additionally, 4 of the 5 meta-analyses presented in [Table 1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1538783623007900?utm_source=chatgpt.com#tbl1) reported that the median times to resumption were within 40 days. Accordingly, the 2020 European Society of [Cardiology](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/cardiology "Learn more about Cardiology from ScienceDirect's AI-generated Topic Pages") [AF](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/atrial-fibrillation "Learn more about AF from ScienceDirect's AI-generated Topic Pages") guidelines make the level IIb recommendation that OAC can be reinitiated 4 to 8 weeks after ICH provided the cause of hemorrhage is controlled

The more recent 2022 American Heart Association/American Stroke Association spontaneous ICH guidelines are more conservative, with a later and narrower suggested timeframe of 7 to 8 weeks after weighing individual benefit and risk

The optimal time for resumption is less clear, but available data suggest a window of between 7 and 14 days and 6 and 8 weeks depending on individual bleeding and thromboembolism risk, respectively. Resumption in the early part of this window should be considered for patients where the thromboembolism risk outweighs the rebleeding risk, and conversely, resumption should be delayed until the latter part of the window in patients where the risk of rebleeding predominates. In the majority of patients, the composite risk of both thromboembolism and rebleeding is likely minimized somewhere between 4 and 6 weeks after ICH, in line with the recommendations of the European Society of [Cardiology](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/cardiology "Learn more about Cardiology from ScienceDirect's AI-generated Topic Pages") and American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guidelines.

# after brain tumor resection

  

After brain tumor resection / craniotomy (postoperative neurosurgical patients)

  

  

Here the key issue is postoperative intracranial bleeding risk, which is highest very early.

  

  

What the postoperative literature suggests

  

  

- A 2024 JNS analysis found catastrophic ICH risk was significantly increased when therapeutic anticoagulation was initiated within 48 hours of cranial surgery.  
- A 2024 retrospective study (“early therapeutic AC in postoperative craniotomy patients”) suggests therapeutic anticoagulation can be started post-craniotomy with manageable complication rates in selected patients, but it still reported a notable rate of postoperative ICH (many not requiring surgery).  
- Older series also discuss therapeutic anticoagulation after craniotomy without “mandatory ramps,” but these are generally small/retrospective and often emphasize agent choice (UFH for reversibility) over dose-titration rituals.  

  

  

  

Does a stepwise LMWH ramp reduce LOS or bleeding?

  

  

- I could not find high-quality comparative data showing that a percentage-dose LMWH ramp (25→50→75→100%) is superior to starting therapeutic anticoagulation outright once imaging is stable and the surgeon is comfortable.
- What is evidence-supported in many centers is:  
    

- Avoiding very early (<48h) therapeutic AC when possible (higher catastrophic bleed risk).  
- Using UFH when therapeutic AC is unavoidable early because it’s rapidly titratable and reversible (pragmatic safety logic, not RCT-level evidence).  

-   
    

  

  

Bottom line post-craniotomy: The “ramp” itself isn’t clearly evidence-based; the strongest signal is about how soon you anticoagulate (especially the first 48h) and how reversible the agent is if bleeding occurs.

## Practical takeaways if your goal is reducing hospital days

  

  

1. Don’t conflate “ramp” with “earlier.” If you’re restarting later anyway (e.g., weeks post-ICH), a ramp likely just delays therapeutic effect without proven safety benefit.
2. For DOAC restarts (AF/VTE), if/when restart is chosen and surgical/hematoma stability is confirmed, starting full-dose DOAC directly is often the cleanest path (no bridging, no ramp), and bridging may add bleeding risk in some data.  
3. For very high thrombotic-risk scenarios (mechanical valve, LVAD, acute massive PE, etc.), shortening time to therapeutic AC may be necessary—but the postoperative data argue strongly for respecting the first 48 hours after cranial surgery when possible.
