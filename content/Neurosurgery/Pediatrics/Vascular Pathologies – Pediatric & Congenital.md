## **Vein of Galen Malformation (VGAM)**

> [!summary] **Overview**  
> Congenital high-flow arteriovenous shunt between choroidal/mesencephalic feeders and the median prosencephalic vein (precursor to the vein of Galen). Produces severe venous hypertension, high-output cardiac failure, and hydrocephalus. Endovascular therapy is mainstay; timing and physiologic status heavily dictate outcome.

### **Key Features**

- ==AVM== subtype (~30%).
- Direct arterial shunting into the vein of Galen **without parenchymal nidus**.
- Internal cerebral veins typically not visualized.
- Embryologic origin: **6–7 weeks gestation**.
- Incidence: **1:25,000 births**, M > F.
- Primarily neonatal/pediatric.

![[Neurosurgery/Pediatrics/_resources/Vascular_pathologies.resources/image.2.png|500]]!![[Neurosurgery/Pediatrics/_resources/Vascular_pathologies.resources/image.1.png|500]]

### **Arterial Feeders (common)**

- Posterior choroidal (78%)
- Anterior choroidal (71%)
- P1 PCA (70%)
- Pericallosal arteries (60%)

### Classification

#### **Yasargil Classification**

1. **Type I:** Direct AV shunt at the ampulla  
    – Neonatal presentation, ==**severe CHF**==
    
2. **Type II:** Transmesencephalic / transdiencephalic AVM  
    – Later presentation
    
3. **Type III:** Mixed AVM  
    – Infants & young children
    
4. **Type IV:** AVM draining proximally into VoG

#### **Berenstein & Lasjaunias Classification**

- **Mural type (39%)** – few direct arterial connections into wall of the median prosencephalic vein
- **Choroidal type** – multiple feeders from choroidal & perforator vessels

![[Neurosurgery/Pediatrics/_resources/Vascular_pathologies.resources/image.3.png|300]]

### **Clinical Presentation**

- **Neonates:**  
    – Severe ==high-output CHF== (most common)
    
- **Infants / young children:**  
    – Hydrocephalus (venous hypertension)  
    – Macrocephaly  
    – Developmental delay  
    – Seizures  
    – "==Melting brain syndrome==" (progressive cerebral atrophy from venous hypertension)
    
- **Older children/adults:**  
    – SAH, headache, less commonly CHF

### **Work-Up**

- Antenatal US (often first detection)
- Fetal MRI
- Postnatal transfontanelle US
- MRI (preferred)
- CT (rarely primary)
- **DSA (gold standard)**

![[Neurosurgery/Pediatrics/_resources/Vascular_pathologies.resources/image.5.png|500]]

![[Neurosurgery/Pediatrics/_resources/Vascular_pathologies.resources/image.png|500]]

### **Management**

- Historically palliative (>90% mortality).
- Open treatment (1940s–90s): ~60% mortality.
- **Endovascular therapy (modern): ~80% survival**  
    – Target age 3–5 months unless unstable  
    – Aim: staged reduction of shunt flow  
    – Partial embolization beneficial  
    – Complications: ischemia, glue migration, transvenous occlusion (infarct/hemorrhage), "excessive transfusion syndrome"

### **Bicêtre (Lasjaunias) Neonatal Evaluation System (21 points)**

- **<8:** Very poor prognosis → no treatment
- ==**8–12:** Emergency embolization==
- **>12:** Stable neonate → delay embolization until safer physiology

## **Brain AVMs (Pediatric)**

> [!summary] **Overview**  
> Developmental vascular anomaly with a nidus of dysplastic vessels allowing high-flow arteriovenous shunting. Pediatric AVMs rupture more frequently over a lifetime and may recur after complete treatment.

see also [[Neurosurgery/Vascular/Arteriovenous malformations]] 
### **General**

- Developmental vascular error.
- ==**Pial AV fistula**== (no nidus; distinct entity).  
    – Strongly associated with **HHT**, **RASA-1** mutations.  
    – Presents earlier: CHF, seizures, headaches.
    
- Pediatric prevalence: 3–20%.

### **Initial Presentation**

- ICH (most common)
- Seizures
- Headaches

### **Hemorrhage Risk**

- Unruptured annual risk: **2–4%**
- Re-rupture in first 6 months: **~6%**
- =="Lifetime risk": 105 − age (approximation)==
- Outcomes generally better than adults

### **Work-Up**

- MRI/MRA or TOF sequence
- **DSA (gold standard)**  
    – If first DSA negative after hemorrhage, repeat at 6–8 weeks

### **Management**

- Observation
- Microsurgery ± embolization (**>90% cure** for SM I–II)
- Radiosurgery ± embolization (75% cure)
- Embolization alone or adjunctively
- Treat aggressively in children due to higher cumulative rupture risk & documented recurrence
- **Pial AVFs:** primarily endovascular

### **Surgery**

- Indications: ==**Spetzler-Martin I–II**==
- Timing: 4–6 weeks after hemorrhage
- Post-op: CTH → DSA confirmation; tight BP control; pain management

### **Embolization**

- For rupture sites, intranidal aneurysms, or pial AVFs
- Complications: access issues (very small femoral arteries), hypovolemia, radiation burden  
    – Umbilical artery access in neonates <7 days

### **Radiosurgery**

- Latency 3–6 years
- ~2.2% cumulative bleed risk during latency
- Obliteration ~50–80%

## **Cerebral Cavernous Malformations (CCM)**

> [!summary] **Overview**  
> Low-flow, sinusoidal vascular lesions without arterial shunting. Often asymptomatic; hemorrhage and seizures are the main clinical issues. Familial forms present with multiplicity due to CCM gene mutations.

see also [[Neurosurgery/Vascular/Cavernous malformation]]
### **Basics**

- "Occult" lesions, no shunting.
- Annual hemorrhage risk: **0.4–2%** (higher if prior bleed).
- 8–10% of pediatric vascular malformations.
- > 60% incidental; ~25% symptomatic.

### **Genetics**

- **Sporadic:** usually isolated lesion.
- **Familial:** multiple lesions; autosomal dominant.  
    – **CCM1** (7q11–22): most common; neuronal expression; cell adhesion.  
    – **CCM2** (7q11).  
    – **CCM3** (3): apoptosis-related; more severe.

### **Pathophysiology**

- Embryologic origin; may be de novo.
- Prior radiation is a risk factor.

### **Histology**

- Hemosiderin ring
- HIF1α positive

### **Localization**

- Supratentorial (80%)
- Infratentorial (20%) – higher ICH risk

### **Clinical Features**

- Seizures (70–80%) → 40% medically refractory
- Hemorrhage (50%)
- Focal deficits (brainstem, BG)
- Stepwise fluctuations common

### **Work-Up**

- MRI (T1/T2/SWI)  
    – =="Popcorn" appearance==, hemosiderin rim
    
- Often associated with DVA

### **Management**

- Conservative: observation, serial MRI, seizure management
- Surgery:  
    – Indicated if symptomatic or hemorrhagic  
    – Remove hemosiderin ring when safe (not in brainstem)  
    – Avoid DVA resection  
    – Brainstem CCMs: consider perioperative steroids
    
- Radiosurgery: for inaccessible, symptomatic lesions (20 Gy typical)

## **Pediatric Intracranial Aneurysms**

> [!summary] **Overview**  
> Rare but often large and morphologically complex in children. Dissecting, infectious, and traumatic etiologies are more common than in adults.

### **Key Points**

- Rare overall; **~11% traumatic/dissecting**.
- Mycotic aneurysms possible.
- Hemodynamic & structural factors dominate pathogenesis.
- **Newborn aneurysms → 10% are giant.**

### **Presentation**

- Hemorrhage (60–70%)
- High morbidity with untreated ruptures
- Frequent partial thrombosis in giant aneurysms (50–60%)

### **Management**

- Microsurgical clipping or bypass
- Endovascular coiling/embolization
- Long-term surveillance:  
    – DSA after treatment  
    – MRI/MRA annually, eventually q5y

## **Moyamoya Disease**

> [!summary] **Overview**  
> Progressive ==steno-occlusive arteriopathy of the terminal ICA== with compensatory basal collaterals ("puff of smoke"). Children present with ischemia; adults with hemorrhage.

see also [[Moyamoya disease & syndrome]]
### **Etiology**

- Idiopathic; familial clusters (autosomal dominant, 17q25)
- Associations: trisomy 21, sickle cell, NF1, thyroid disease, prior cranial radiation

### **Epidemiology**

- Bimodal: ages 5–10 and >40
- Asian predominance
- Unilateral may progress to bilateral

### **Clinical Presentation**

- TIAs, ischemic stroke
- Headache
- Seizures
- Chorea / involuntary movements
- Pediatric "childhood arteriopathy" pattern

### **Work-Up**

- MRI with CVR (perfusion)
- CTA
- **DSA (definitive)**: stenosis/occlusion, collateral network
- Assess cerebrovascular reserve (steal phenomenon)

![[Neurosurgery/Pediatrics/_resources/Vascular_pathologies.resources/image.6.png|500]]

### **Management**

- **Revascularization is mainstay**  
    – Goal: augment perfusion  
    – Perioperative stroke risk ~5%  
    – Delay 4–8 weeks after acute infarct
    
- **Direct bypass:** STA → MCA (preferred when feasible)
- **Indirect bypass:** STA, OA, galea, dura, temporalis, periosteum, or omentum
- **Perioperative care:**  
    – Maintain BP; dopamine preferred  
    – Normocapnia  
    – Maintain Hgb  
    – EEG/EP monitoring  
    – PICU postop  
    – ASA starting POD1  
    – Prehydrate 24–48 h prior; hold ASA 24 h pre-op
