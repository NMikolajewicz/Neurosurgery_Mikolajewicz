Dural arteriovenous fistulas (dAVFs) and brain arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) are both pathological arteriovenous shunts, but they differ fundamentally in **location, etiology, angioarchitecture, classification, and management strategy**.

## Core Distinction

AVMs are congenital tangles of dysplastic vessels (the nidus) embedded within or adjacent to brain parenchyma, with direct arteriovenous shunting bypassing a capillary bed. dAVFs, by contrast, are acquired arteriovenous connections **confined within the dural leaflets**, typically at or near a venous sinus, without a true nidus. dAVFs account for roughly 10–15% of all intracranial arteriovenous shunts.[1][2][3]

## Etiology & Pathogenesis

AVMs are generally considered congenital, arising from disrupted angiogenesis, and may be associated with hereditary syndromes (HHT) or sporadic somatic mutations (e.g., *KRAS*). dAVFs are classically acquired, associated with dural sinus thrombosis, venous hypertension, prior craniotomy, or trauma — though many are idiopathic. The working theory for dAVF formation involves angiogenic remodeling driven by venous outflow obstruction and upregulation of VEGF.[2][4][1]

## Angioarchitecture

| Feature | Brain AVM | Dural AVF |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Parenchyma/pia | Dural leaflets |
| Nidus | Present (compact or diffuse) | Absent — direct fistulous connection |
| Arterial supply | Pial/medullary arteries | Meningeal arteries (ECA, ICA dural branches) |
| Venous drainage | Cortical/deep veins | Dural sinus ± cortical venous reflux |
| Etiology | Congenital | Acquired |
| Associated aneurysms | Yes (~10–15% flow-related) | Rare |

## Classification Systems

**AVMs** are graded by the **Spetzler-Martin scale** (size, eloquence, deep venous drainage; grades I–V), which predicts surgical morbidity.[1]

**dAVFs** are classified by venous drainage pattern, which directly correlates with hemorrhage risk and aggressiveness:
- **Borden I**: Drains into dural sinus only — benign, often managed conservatively[5]
- **Borden II**: Drains into sinus *with* retrograde cortical venous reflux (CVR) — high risk[5]
- **Borden III**: Drains exclusively into cortical veins — highest hemorrhage risk[5]
- The **Cognard system** is more granular, differentiating anterograde vs. retrograde sinus flow and direct cortical venous drainage[6]

The single most important prognostic feature in dAVFs is the **presence of cortical venous drainage (CVD)** — its detection mandates intervention.[3]

## Clinical Presentation

AVMs typically present in younger patients (mean ~30s) with **hemorrhage (~50%), seizures (~20%), or headache**. dAVFs tend to present in middle-aged to older adults with symptoms highly dependent on location and drainage pattern — **pulsatile tinnitus** (cavernous/transverse sinus), **proptosis/chemosis** (cavernous sinus), **progressive myelopathy** (spinal), or **intracranial hemorrhage/venous congestion** with aggressive drainage patterns.[4][1]

## Treatment

For **AVMs**, the triad of microsurgery, endovascular embolization (pre-op), and stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is used based on Spetzler-Martin grade, with grade I–III lesions generally amenable to surgery. The ARUBA trial controversially suggested medical management for unruptured AVMs outperforms intervention, though this remains debated in the neurosurgical community.[1]

For **dAVFs**, treatment is driven by Borden/Cognard grade:[7]
- **Borden I without symptoms**: Observation acceptable
- **Borden II/III (CVD present)**: Prompt treatment indicated — transvenous embolization is often first-line and highly curative (targeting the draining vein/sinus segment)
- **Transarterial embolization** (Onyx) can also achieve cure, especially for Type III
- **Surgical disconnection** of cortical venous drainage is highly effective when endovascular options are exhausted[7]
- **SRS** reserved for low-risk lesions or residual disease[8]

The key principle for dAVF cure is **complete elimination of the arteriovenous shunt** — partial treatment risks collateral recruitment and persistent hemorrhage risk.[2]
