## Concise Guide
### 1\. Purpose
* Real-time assessment of neural function during surgery
* Early warning for potential injury to brain, spinal cord, or nerves
* Enhances safety and helps prevent permanent deficits
### 2. Common Modalities

| **Modality**     | **Target**                       | **Key Use**                          | **Alarm Criteria**                               |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------ |
| SSEP             | Dorsal column sensory pathways   | Spine, brain perfusion               | ==↓Amplitude >50%, ↑Latency >10%==               |
| MEP              | Corticospinal motor tracts       | Spine, motor cortex surgery          | ↓Amplitude >50–75%, loss of signal               |
| Free-running EMG | Cranial/spinal/peripheral nerves | Nerve root, cranial nerve protection | Spontaneous bursts/trains (nerve irritation)     |

| EEG / ECoG       | Cortical activity                | Ischemia, epilepsy, perfusion        | Slowing, attenuation, seizure discharges         |
### 3\. Interpretation Principles
* Baseline comparison essential
* Multimodal monitoring increases reliability
* Key patterns:
	* SSEP/MEP loss: Ischemia or mechanical injury
	* EMG trains: Nerve irritation/compression
	* BAEP wave V delay/loss: Cochlear nerve risk
	* EEG slowing: Cortical ischemia or anesthesia
### 4\. Troubleshooting & Team Response
* Confirm signal loss: check electrodes, stim settings
* Optimize physiology: raise BP, correct temp/O2
* Adjust anesthesia: avoid inhaled agents, no paralytics if using MEPs
* Reverse surgical maneuver: reduce retraction, loosen rods, remove clip
* Observe for recovery: if signals return, proceed with caution
### 5\. Anesthesia Considerations
* Use ==TIVA== (e.g. propofol, remifentanil)
* Avoid muscle relaxants (except for intubation)
* Keep MAC < 0.5 if using inhaled agents
* Coordinate boluses with neuromonitoring timing
### 6\. Surgical Applications
#### Spine
* Scoliosis/deformity: SSEP + MEP + EMG
* Tumor resection: SSEP/MEP for cord monitoring
* Tethered cord: EMG in sphincter/lower limbs
#### Cranial
* Brainstem/skull base: BAEPs, cranial nerve EMG, SSEP/MEP
* Acoustic neuroma: BAEP + CN VII EMG
* Microvascular decompression: Facial EMG, BAEPs
#### Epilepsy/Functional
* ECoG: seizure focus mapping
* Direct cortical stimulation: motor/language mapping
* EEG: ischemia/seizure monitoring
#### Peripheral Nerve
* Brachial plexus/tumor: Free/triggered EMG
* Nerve repair/transfer: Stimulate to confirm continuity
* ENT surgery: CN VII, RLN monitoring via EMG
### 7\. Takeaways for Residents

* Know which modality is appropriate for each surgery
* Understand anesthesia-IONM interaction
* Interpret changes in context (not all alerts = injury)
* Communicate rapidly with the team when alerts occur
* Document signal changes, interventions, and outcomes

## Detailed Guide
### Introduction to IONM

Intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM) is used during neurosurgical procedures to assess the functional integrity of neural structures in real-time. By monitoring electrical signals from the nervous system, the surgical team can detect evolving injury to the brain, spinal cord, or peripheral nerves early enough to intervene, thus helping prevent permanent postoperative deficits . The fundamental principle is that physiological changes (e.g. loss of evoked potentials or new EMG activity) often precede irreversible structural damage. IONM serves as a “warning system” to alert surgeons of neural compromise, allowing timely corrective actions (such as stopping traction or improving blood flow) before irreversible injury occurs. It has become standard of care in many spine and brain surgeries where there is risk of neurologic injury . Another key principle is that special anesthetic techniques are required – for example, minimizing inhalational anesthetics and muscle paralytics – to avoid suppressing the neurophysiological signals being monitored . Overall, IONM is a team effort involving surgeons, anesthesiologists, and neurophysiologists working in concert to enhance patient safety in the operating room.
### Key IONM Modalities

Modern IONM employs multiple modalities, each targeting specific neural pathways or structures. Using multimodal monitoring (e.g. combining sensory and motor evoked potentials with EMG) is often recommended to improve sensitivity and reduce false alarms  . The major IONM modalities used in neurosurgery include:
#### Somatosensory Evoked Potentials (SSEPs)

SSEPs are electrical responses recorded from the nervous system after stimulating a peripheral nerve (typically a limb nerve). They primarily monitor the ==dorsal column–medial lemniscus== sensory pathways that carry proprioception and vibration sense to the brain. In practice, a ==peripheral nerve== (e.g. posterior tibial nerve at the ankle or median nerve at the wrist) is ==repetitively stimulated,== and the resulting ascending signals are ==recorded from the scalp or spinal cord==. The key metrics are the ==amplitude and latency== of the evoked waveform at various points (e.g. the cortical SSEP waveform). During surgery, a significant decrease in SSEP amplitude (commonly >50%) or increase in latency (>10%) from baseline is a warning criterion suggesting possible compromise of the sensory pathways . SSEPs are especially useful for monitoring spinal cord dorsal column function during spine surgeries (like scoliosis correction or spinal tumor resection) and brain perfusion during intracranial procedures. They provide continuous feedback on sensory pathway integrity, but note that SSEP signals can be affected by anesthesia depth, hypotension, or hypothermia (which can prolong latency) .
#### Motor Evoked Potentials (MEPs)
MEPs assess the functional integrity of the ==corticospinal (motor) pathways==. They are typically elicited by ==transcranial electrical stimulation== of the motor cortex, producing a descending volley along the spinal cord, and are ==recorded as muscle responses in the limbs== . In other words, a stimulating electrode (often on the scalp or over the spinal cord) triggers the motor tract, and electrodes in target muscles (e.g. thenar hand muscles or tibialis anterior) detect the evoked muscle contractions. MEP monitoring is very sensitive to anesthesia and neuromuscular blockade – muscle relaxants are avoided after intubation, and total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) is usually employed to preserve MEP signals . During surgery, loss or significant reduction of MEPs from baseline is a critical warning for potential motor pathway injury. Unlike SSEPs which may change gradually, MEPs can be more all-or-none – a sudden disappearance of MEPs often warrants immediate action. Some protocols also track the minimum stimulation intensity (“threshold”) needed to evoke an MEP; a large increase in threshold (e.g. >100 V higher) or a >50% drop in MEP amplitude can indicate compromised spinal cord perfusion or mechanical injury to motor tracts . MEPs are indispensable in spine deformity surgeries (e.g. scoliosis corrections) to guard against anterior cord (motor) injury, and in brain surgeries near the motor cortex or corticospinal tract. They have largely replaced the historical “wake-up test” by allowing motor function to be monitored continuously while the patient remains under anesthesia.
#### Electromyography (EMG) – Free-Running and Triggered
EMG monitoring involves recording spontaneous or evoked muscle electrical activity to monitor peripheral or cranial nerve function. There are two forms: free-running EMG and triggered EMG.
* ==Free-running EMG==: This is the continuous surveillance of muscle activity without intentional stimulation. Electrode needles are placed in muscles supplied by the nerves at risk (for example, tibial anterior to monitor L4/L5 root, or facial muscles to monitor the facial nerve). Burst activity or sustained trains of EMG spikes (“neurotonic discharges”) during surgery can indicate that a nerve is being irritated, stretched, or compressed. For instance, during spinal screw placement, a sudden train of EMG activity in a leg muscle might suggest a screw is breaching a pedicle and impinging a nerve root. Similarly, in cranial surgery, continuous EMG can detect manipulation of cranial nerves (e.g. facial nerve irritation in acoustic neuroma surgery manifests as EMG firing in facial muscles). The surgeon can be alerted to unintended nerve stimulation or traction if such EMG activity appears, ideally in time to reverse the maneuver before permanent injury.
* ==Triggered EMG==: In this mode, the surgeon or monitoring technician intentionally stimulates a structure and listens for a muscle response. Triggered EMG is often used to identify nerves or test their integrity. For example, each pedicle screw in spine surgery can be stimulated with an electrical pulse; a muscle response at a very low threshold (e.g. <5–10 mA) suggests the screw is too close to a nerve (pedicle breach), whereas a higher current threshold to evoke a response indicates the screw is safely within bone. In peripheral nerve or plexus surgery, the surgeon may stimulate a structure in the operative field – if the corresponding muscle EMG fires, it confirms the structure is neural tissue. Cranial nerve mapping uses triggered EMG as well (e.g. stimulating the facial nerve during parotid tumor resection to identify and preserve it). Thus, EMG (free-running and triggered) is a versatile modality for monitoring motor nerves across many procedures . Unlike evoked potentials, EMG is largely qualitative (presence or absence of activity) and doesn’t provide a quantitative waveform to measure, but it gives immediate feedback on nerve irritation or continuity.
#### Brainstem Auditory Evoked Potentials (BAEPs)
BAEPs (also called brainstem auditory evoked responses, BAERs) are used to monitor the auditory nerve (cranial nerve VIII) and the brainstem auditory pathways. They are elicited by repetitive auditory clicks delivered to the ear (via earphones), and the responses are recorded from scalp electrodes. The BAEP waveform has several characteristic peaks (waves I through V) representing neural activity from the cochlea through the brainstem auditory relays. In intraoperative monitoring, wave V (generated in the midbrain inferior colliculus) is the most robust and clinically significant peak. Prolongation of the wave V latency by ~1 millisecond or an amplitude drop >50% is typically used as an alarm criterion indicating possible injury to the cochlear nerve or brainstem auditory pathways  . For example, during vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma) resection, a gradual increase in wave V latency or disappearance of later waves alerts the team that the tumor manipulation or stretching might be compromising cochlear nerve blood supply. The surgeon can then adjust technique (reducing retraction or irrigation to prevent heating) to prevent permanent hearing loss. BAEPs are relatively resistant to anesthetic effects (since they are short-latency potentials generated in the brainstem), allowing their use even under deep anesthesia. They are primarily used in posterior fossa and skull base surgeries – classic examples include acoustic neuroma removal, brainstem tumor resection, microvascular decompressions, and any surgery where cranial nerve VIII or the brainstem is at risk . By providing real-time feedback on the auditory pathway, BAEP monitoring has significantly improved hearing preservation rates in relevant surgeries.
#### Electroencephalography (EEG)
EEG monitoring refers to the continuous recording of the brain’s spontaneous electrical activity via scalp (or cortical surface) electrodes. Unlike evoked potentials which are responses to stimuli, EEG provides a real-time picture of the brain’s background electrical function (cortical activity). In the operating room, EEG is most often used to monitor for cerebral ischemia or seizure activity. For instance, during carotid endarterectomy or intracranial aneurysm clipping, a sudden loss of fast activity and emergence of slow delta waves on EEG in one hemisphere may indicate insufficient blood flow (ischemia) to that side of the brain, prompting the need for an intervention (such as raising blood pressure or placing a shunt) . EEG can also confirm adequate anesthetic depth or detect intraoperative seizures (which is particularly useful during epilepsy surgery or functional cortical mapping). A specialized form of EEG used in neurosurgery is electrocorticography (ECoG), where electrodes are placed directly on the cortex to record epileptiform spikes during epilepsy surgery or to map functional cortex. In some epilepsy and tumor surgeries, ECoG helps identify abnormal seizure-generating tissue or ensure a lesionectomy has removed the epileptic focus. Generally, significant EEG changes such as new onset of rhythmic slowing, loss of amplitude, or burst-suppression outside of expected anesthetic patterns are concerning for neural compromise (often due to ischemia). EEG is very sensitive to global cerebral function but relatively insensitive to small focal injuries; thus, it complements evoked potentials which monitor specific pathways. It’s commonly employed in vascular neurosurgery (aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations) and in any case where global cerebral perfusion is a concern , as well as in epilepsy surgery for monitoring seizure activity.
### Interpretation of IONM Signals and Warning Criteria
Accurate interpretation of IONM changes is critical. Baseline recordings are obtained after induction of anesthesia (but before any high-risk surgical manipulation) for comparison throughout the case. The neuromonitoring team defines alarm criteria – thresholds of change beyond which a significant neural compromise is suspected and the surgical team must be alerted. Key interpretation principles include:
* Evoked Potentials (SSEPs, MEPs, BAEPs): The primary metrics are waveform amplitude (signal strength) and latency (signal timing). A significant amplitude drop (typically 50% or more) or a latency prolongation (10% or more) from baseline is widely used as a warning threshold . For SSEPs, a >50% decrease in cortical SSEP amplitude or >10% increase in peak latency suggests possible dorsal column or perfusion compromise in the pathway. For MEPs, criteria can include a dramatic amplitude reduction or complete disappearance of the muscle potentials (some centers also use >50–75% amplitude reduction as a warning) . Because MEPs can vary beat-to-beat, many practitioners treat loss of MEPs (especially if sustained after troubleshooting) as the critical warning sign of motor tract injury. BAEP changes are interpreted by looking at increases in wave latencies (especially wave V) or loss of waves – for example, a 1 ms delay in wave V or loss of wave V during acoustic neuroma surgery is highly concerning for auditory nerve damage . The “10–50 rule” (10% latency, 50% amplitude) is a common guideline across these modalities, though each patient’s baseline and the context are considered . Important: transient or small changes may occur due to reversible factors (like temporary retractor pressure or blood pressure dips) – the neuromonitoring team typically looks for changes that are persistent and significant before declaring an official alert, to balance sensitivity with avoiding false alarms.
* EMG (Free-running): Since free-running EMG is an ongoing stream of muscle activity, interpretation focuses on the appearance of abnormal bursts or sustained trains of activity that were not present before. A single muscle fiber twitch or brief burst might occur from a sudden movement or stimulus and usually is not worrisome if it’s instantaneous. However, repetitive firing (especially a train of spikes or a “tonic” discharge) correlates with significant nerve irritation – for example, a pedicle screw compressing a nerve root often causes a train of EMG activity that continues until the stimulus is relieved. Neurophysiologists also distinguish “A-trains”, which are trains of EMG activity in cranial nerve monitoring that have a characteristic frequency and amplitude, often indicating stretch or compression of a cranial nerve (such as the facial nerve during skull base surgery). The presence of sustained EMG activity prompts the surgeon to inspect the surgical field for any undue pressure or stretching on the monitored nerve. In triggered EMG, the interpretation is straightforward: if a structure is stimulated at a given current, the threshold current to evoke a muscle response is noted. A low threshold (meaning the nerve responds to very low stimulus) implies the nerve is extremely close or has low resistance (possible breach), whereas an intact insulating barrier (like bone around a screw) requires higher current. Thus, a triggered EMG threshold drop is interpreted as loss of structural protection around the nerve.
* EEG: In the operating room setting, EEG interpretation focuses on global patterns. Warning signs on EEG include the development of delta waves (slow activity), loss of faster frequencies, or an overall drop in amplitude in one or both hemispheres, suggesting cerebral depression. Focal slowing or attenuation can indicate focal ischemia if it corresponds to a vascular territory. In carotid surgery, for instance, unilateral slowing when the carotid is clamped is a strong indicator of inadequate collateral flow to that hemisphere. Conversely, the appearance of epileptiform discharges (spikes or sharp waves) would signal an oncoming seizure, which could be provoked by surgical manipulation (especially in epilepsy surgery) and would prompt cessation of stimulation and administration of antiseizure measures. Overall, EEG changes must be correlated with the surgical timeline; for example, diffuse EEG suppression shortly after a bolus of anesthetic is not an ischemic warning but a drug effect. Thus, context is crucial.
Common patterns indicating neural compromise across modalities often relate to either ischemia or mechanical injury. Ischemia (loss of blood supply) typically causes decreased amplitudes in SSEPs/MEPs (neurons not firing well) and possibly prolonged latencies as neurons slow down . For instance, if blood pressure drops or a vessel is occluded, within minutes one might see SSEP amplitudes shrink or disappear. Mechanical compression or stretch of neural tissue often leads to increased latencies and then amplitude loss in SSEPs (as conduction slows and then fails) ; in MEPs it often causes immediate loss of the signal if the corticospinal tract is significantly compressed. EMG will fire intensely if a nerve root is stretched or irritated by mechanical forces. Transient EMG bursts with drilling or cautery can occur, but sustained activity is more indicative of true compression/stretch. In BAEPs, stretch or ischemia of the auditory nerve causes wave V to delay and diminish, possibly progressing to loss of all waves if the nerve is severely injured. A critical pattern is complete loss of any previously present signal (e.g. flatline SSEPs or absent MEPs in a limb) – this is strongly suggestive of significant injury and is usually treated as an emergency warning unless it can be quickly attributed to a technical issue. The monitoring team must differentiate such true neurologic changes from artifacts or reversible factors (like a sudden change in anesthesia, blood pressure, or technical malfunction) through systematic troubleshooting.
### Intraoperative Decision-Making and Troubleshooting

When an IONM change (alert) occurs, the surgical team must respond rapidly and systematically. The general approach to intraoperative decision-making based on IONM findings involves: 1) immediate communication of the alert, 2) prompt investigation of reversible causes, and 3) protective actions for the patient.
* Communication: As soon as a significant monitoring change is detected that meets alarm criteria, the neurophysiology personnel must immediately inform the surgeon and anesthesiologist. Clear, concise communication is crucial (e.g. “Alert: left arm SSEPs have dropped 60% from baseline” or “MEPs to both feet just disappeared”). The surgical team and anesthesiology should acknowledge the alert and all parties should be on the same page regarding next steps. Regular communication and a plan in advance for what constitutes an alert are important, so the surgeon isn’t caught off guard. Effective team communication and cooperation have been shown to improve patient outcomes by allowing timely intervention . Often, the neuromonitoring team will call out a “warning” or “alert” level change, and if the issue is not immediately obvious, they might request a surgical pause to sort out the cause.
* Troubleshooting and Verification: The first step after an alert is to verify that the change is real and not an artifact. The neuromonitoring technologist will check technical factors: electrode connections, stimulus output, and noise on the recordings. Sometimes simply repositioning a limb or adjusting an electrode fixes a technical issue that mimicked a change. The monitoring team may repeat the stimulation or try an alternate modality to confirm the finding. For example, if SSEPs drop, they might double-check MEPs or vice versa to see if multiple modalities are affected (true injury) or only one (possibly technical). Stimulus intensity can be increased for evoked potentials to see if a response can be restored . Ensuring the reference electrodes and filters are correct is also part of troubleshooting, since improper filter settings or grounding issues can attenuate signals . Throughout this, the anesthesiologist may be asked if there were any recent changes (e.g. was a bolus of propofol given, or did the blood pressure drop suddenly?) that could explain a transient change.
* Physiologic Optimization: If the signal change appears genuine, the team next addresses systemic factors that could be corrected easily. Blood pressure support is often the first intervention – e.g. using vasopressors or increasing fluids to improve spinal cord perfusion if SSEPs/MEPs are waning . If the patient is anemic or hypoxic, improving oxygenation or transfusing blood can help neural function. Body temperature is checked; even mild hypothermia can depress evoked potentials, so warming the patient might be indicated . The anesthetic depth is assessed: excessive inhalational agent or recent increases in anesthesia can be reversed (switch more to intravenous agents or lighten anesthesia if safely possible) to improve signal amplitude. Importantly, avoidance of muscle relaxants is re-emphasized if MEPs are in use (ensure no additional paralytic was given that could explain MEP loss). If the change was in EEG (suggesting brain ischemia), raising mean arterial pressure or releasing surgical retraction might be urgent steps to restore perfusion. These physiologic tweaks are often done simultaneously with the next step (surgical adjustment), as they are generally low-risk to implement.
* Surgical Maneuver Adjustments: The surgeon must consider what was happening at the moment the change occurred. If the alert coincided with a specific action (e.g. rod rotation in a scoliosis case, tumor retraction near the brainstem, clipping of an aneurysm, or coagulation near a nerve root), reversing or pausing that maneuver is the priority. In spine surgery, this might mean loosening the recently tightened screws/rods or relieving traction on the cord. In tumor resection, it could mean releasing the retractor or removing a temporary clip to alleviate pressure on neural tissue. If a pedicle screw is suspected to cause EMG activity, the screw can be backed out or redirected. The surgeon may also irrigate the area with warm saline or papaverine (a vasodilator) if a vessel spasm is suspected (common in microvascular decompression or aneurysm surgery). In some cases, changing strategy is required – for instance, if MEPs are lost during an aggressive resection, the surgeon might decide to stage the surgery or leave part of a tumor intact rather than risk paralysis. The decision-making often involves weighing the risk of proceeding (with compromised signals) versus the benefit of the surgical goal; patient safety takes precedence, so significant unresolved IONM changes often compel a more conservative approach.
* Recovery and Further Action: After any intervention, the team watches to see if the monitoring signals recover. Many IONM changes, if addressed quickly (e.g. raising blood pressure or easing retraction), will show partial or full recovery of signals within minutes. A return toward baseline is very reassuring and the surgery may proceed with caution, potentially with more frequent monitoring checks. If signals do not recover despite all reasonable maneuvers, the team faces a difficult decision. In spine cases, one option historically was the “wake-up test” – waking the patient enough to obey commands and checking motor function directly; this is rarely needed now but could be a last resort to assess if the patient can move their feet, for example, before proceeding. If a wake-up test is failed or impossible and signals remain absent, the surgeon might abort or substantially alter the procedure to prevent likely neurologic deficit. Documentation of the sequence of events, interventions, and outcomes is important for postoperative analysis. Postoperatively, any IONM changes can guide recovery expectations – e.g. if a transient loss occurred but recovered after raising blood pressure, one might expect a reversible neurapraxia or no deficit, whereas a permanent loss of signals might predict a neurologic deficit that should be managed in ICU.
* Anesthetic Considerations Recap: Throughout the surgery, the anesthesia team tailors their plan to facilitate monitoring. Typically a propofol-based TIVA (total IV anesthesia) is used for cases needing MEPs, often supplemented with opioids and agents like dexmedetomidine. Inhalational agents (if used at all) are kept at low concentrations (0.5 MAC or less) to avoid obliterating evoked potentials . Short-acting muscle relaxants are given only for intubation, and then either allowed to wear off or maintained at very low twitch (if absolutely needed for surgical reasons) so that MEPs and EMG are interpretable . Certain drugs have specific effects: ketamine and etomidate tend to enhance EP amplitudes, whereas benzodiazepines and inhaled agents depress them . The anesthesiologist and neuromonitorist coordinate timing of any necessary doses (for example, pausing stimulation during moments of unavoidable muscle relaxant use, or avoiding boluses during critical monitoring windows). In summary, maintaining a stable anesthetic plane, adequate perfusion, and normal physiology is key to obtaining reliable IONM signals and quickly discerning true warnings from benign fluctuations.
### IONM in Various Neurosurgical Procedures
IONM techniques are employed across a wide range of neurosurgical operations. The choice of modalities is tailored to the structures at risk in each type of surgery. Below we highlight how IONM is applied in spine, cranial (brainstem/skull base and epilepsy), and peripheral nerve surgeries, with examples:
#### Spine Surgery
Spine surgeries (especially those involving the spinal cord or nerve roots) are among the most common indications for IONM. Examples include deformity corrections (scoliosis or kyphosis instrumentation), spinal tumor resection, cervical or thoracolumbar spinal cord decompressions, and tethered cord release  . In these cases, the primary concern is avoiding injury to the spinal cord or spinal nerve roots due to traction, compression, or ischemia. Typical monitoring setup for major spine cases is multimodal: SSEPs and MEPs, often supplemented by EMG.
* SSEPs in Spine: By stimulating peripheral nerves in the limbs, SSEPs help ensure the dorsal columns and sensory pathways in the cord remain intact during procedures like rod placement or laminectomy. If a corrective maneuver (e.g. rod rotation in scoliosis surgery) begins to compromise the cord’s posterior columns or perfusion, SSEP amplitude will drop, warning the team to adjust instrumentation tension . SSEPs are also useful in intramedullary spinal cord tumor surgeries (monitoring the dorsal column while the surgeon resects tissue, to avoid posterior column damage) and in tethered cord releases (monitoring for any abrupt loss of sensory pathway function when detethering). However, SSEPs primarily reflect dorsal (sensory) tract function; a patient could, in theory, lose motor function while SSEPs remain if only the anterior cord is affected – which is why adding MEPs is crucial for comprehensive monitoring.
* MEPs in Spine: MEPs provide real-time monitoring of corticospinal (motor) tract integrity, which is critical in spine deformity surgery or any case where the anterior spinal cord may be at risk (e.g. during correction of a high thoracic scoliosis, which could stretch the cord). The ability to continuously assess motor function has nearly eliminated the need for the classic wake-up test during scoliosis surgery. If MEPs attenuate or disappear (for instance, during distraction of the spine or placement of hardware), the surgeon can promptly loosen the apparatus or reduce traction to prevent permanent motor deficit. In complex spine tumor resections (such as an extradural tumor encasing the cord), MEP feedback helps guide how aggressively the surgeon can manipulate the cord or roots. Sometimes only one modality changes: e.g. isolated SSEP loss with preserved MEPs might suggest dorsal column ischemia (possibly posterior spinal artery compromise), whereas isolated MEP loss with intact SSEPs could indicate anterior spinal artery compromise or selective motor tract injury. Thus, both are monitored to localize the potential injury.
* EMG in Spine: Free-running EMG is commonly used in lumbar spine surgeries to monitor nerve root irritation. For example, during a discectomy or foraminal decompression, if an instrument irritates a nerve root, EMG bursts in the corresponding leg muscle alert the surgeon to back off. In pedicle screw placement (common in spinal fusions), triggered EMG testing is routine – after a screw is placed in a vertebra, the technician stimulates the screw or adjacent pedicle cortex. A response in the myotome at a low current (e.g. 2–5 mA) would raise concern for a breached pedicle (screw touching a nerve), prompting repositioning of that screw. Free-running EMG can also detect positioning or traction injuries (e.g. if the brachial plexus is stretched due to patient positioning on the table, EMG might show irritation in an upper limb muscle, signaling the team to adjust arm positioning). During tethered cord surgery, monitoring of EMG in anal sphincter or lower limb muscles can warn if rootlets that control those muscles are being stretched while untethering – often used in conjunction with electrical stimulation mapping of each filum or rootlet to identify functional vs. nonfunctional fibers.
Overall in spine surgery, IONM has greatly improved safety: large series have shown that timely detection of SSEP/MEP changes and prompt intervention (like raising blood pressure or easing a correction) reduces the incidence of postoperative paralysis  . It is now considered standard of care in high-risk spine procedures such as adult and pediatric deformity corrections, spinal cord tumor resections, and any surgery where spinal cord manipulation or ischemia is possible.
#### Cranial Surgery – Brainstem and Skull Base
Cranial neurosurgery often involves working around critical neural structures like the brainstem and cranial nerves. IONM is tailored to the specific structures at risk in these delicate regions:
* Brainstem Tumors & Cavernous Malformations: For intrinsic brainstem lesions (e.g. a pontine cavernoma or glioma), surgeons will use SSEPs and MEPs to monitor the long tracts running through the brainstem. Any change in those during resection indicates possible injury to sensory or motor pathways in the brainstem, prompting the surgeon to adjust their approach. Additionally, if the lesion is near the surface, cranial nerve EMG can monitor nearby nuclei or nerve roots (for example, abducens or facial nerve if in the pons). In some cases, auditory pathways are at risk, so BAEPs are also measured, especially if the surgical field is close to the cochlear nucleus or nerve (for example, a tumor at the cerebellopontine angle). Brainstem auditory evoked monitoring is particularly standard in acoustic neuroma (vestibular schwannoma) surgery, which straddles both brainstem and skull base realms: here the cochlear nerve is at risk and BAEPs are used continuously , along with facial nerve EMG (to protect cranial nerve VII). If BAEPs show significant wave V latency prolongation or disappearance, the surgeon may have to ease retraction or stop heating (cauterization) to preserve hearing .
* Skull Base Tumors (e.g. Acoustic Neuroma, Meningiomas): Skull base surgeries often endanger cranial nerves because many cranial nerves course through foramina at the skull base. Free-running and triggered EMG of cranial nerves is a mainstay. For instance, in vestibular schwannoma resection, electrodes in the facial muscles monitor facial nerve EMG continuously – spontaneous spikes or trains warn of facial nerve irritation, and the surgeon can adjust traction. The facial nerve can also be directly stimulated (triggered EMG) to identify it within the tumor capsule or confirm its integrity after dissection. Similarly, in surgery for trigeminal schwannoma or meningioma near the cavernous sinus, trigeminal nerve EMG (masseter or temporalis muscle recordings) can detect manipulation of the trigeminal nerve. Lower cranial nerves (IX, X, XI) can be monitored with EMG from palate, vocal cords (via special endotracheal tube electrodes for the recurrent laryngeal nerve), or trapezius muscle as needed. BAEPs are used in any posterior fossa skull base case where VIIIth nerve or auditory brainstem could be affected (e.g. brainstem compression by a tumor). MEPs and SSEPs might be added for surgeries that risk the long tracts (for example, a large petroclival meningioma causing brainstem distortion). In short, skull base monitoring is often a multimodal cocktail: cranial nerve EMGs, BAEPs, plus SSEPs/MEPs if needed. This has led to improved outcomes, such as higher rates of facial nerve preservation in acoustic neuroma surgery and lower incidence of brainstem infarcts during aggressive skull base tumor resections.
* Microvascular Decompression (MVD): In MVD surgeries for hemifacial spasm or trigeminal neuralgia, IONM is also utilized. For hemifacial spasm, the goal is to alleviate compression on the facial nerve by an artery. In these cases, an abnormal EMG response called the “lateral spread response” (LSR) is often present pre-decompression. The disappearance of this abnormal response after placing the decompression pad is an indicator of surgical success (this is a specialized use of EMG monitoring). Additionally, BAEPs are sometimes monitored in trigeminal neuralgia MVD to ensure the hearing is not affected when manipulating near cranial nerve VIII. These are nuanced applications, but they underline how specific physiologic monitoring endpoints can be tailored to the cranial procedure.
#### Epilepsy Surgery and Functional Neurosurgery
Epilepsy surgeries (such as temporal lobectomies, lesionectomies, or corpus callosotomies) and other functional neurosurgeries present a different use-case for neuromonitoring, often focusing on EEG and functional mapping rather than long-tract monitoring:
* Electrocorticography (ECoG): In many epilepsy surgeries, especially those aiming to remove an epileptogenic focus, surgeons place electrodes on the cortical surface to record EEG directly from the brain. This electrocorticography can capture epileptiform spikes or seizures emanating from the pathological area. After resection, ECoG may be repeated to ensure that these discharges have been eliminated or reduced, which is correlated with a successful removal of the epileptic focus. It’s a form of intraoperative EEG specifically targeting seizure activity. If during resection, continuous ECoG shows evolving discharges, the surgeon might pause as this could indicate irritation of cortex (or even an oncoming seizure, in which case cold saline or medication can be applied to abort it).
* Functional Cortical Mapping: While not “monitoring” in the same continuous sense, an important neurophysiological technique in epilepsy and brain tumor surgery is direct cortical stimulation mapping to identify and spare eloquent areas (motor, sensory, language cortex). For example, in a patient with a lesion near the motor strip, the neurophysiologist may stimulate the cortex and record MEPs or observe movements to map the motor homunculus. Similarly, for language mapping (often done in awake craniotomies), stimulation is used to localize speech areas. These techniques guide the surgeon to avoid critical cortex. If stimulation mapping elicits afterdischarges on the ECoG (sign of triggering a seizure), the team will apply interventions (like brief anesthesia or cold irrigation) to stop it – so monitoring for afterdischarge activity is another role of intraoperative EEG. In summary, IONM in epilepsy surgery is geared toward seizure detection and functional preservation, ensuring that the resection treats the epilepsy while avoiding new deficits.
* Depth Electrodes and Stereo-EEG Procedures: In some cases, placement of depth electrodes for seizure monitoring (stereo-EEG) or responsive neurostimulator implants might involve monitoring EEG to ensure signals are being recorded properly from targets and no acute complications (like seizures during implantation). While not as common as other modalities, neuromonitoring concepts still apply – for instance, making sure there’s no loss of function on EEG when electrodes are inserted (which could indicate hemorrhage or ischemia).
#### Peripheral Nerve Surgery
Peripheral nerve surgeries include operations on nerve plexuses (brachial or lumbosacral plexus) and individual peripheral nerves. IONM is used in these cases primarily to identify and protect nerves and to assess nerve continuity:
* Brachial Plexus Surgery: Operations to repair brachial plexus injuries or remove brachial plexus tumors (neurofibromas, schwannomas) often employ EMG and nerve stimulation techniques. For example, when dissecting a plexus tumor, the surgeon will stimulate nerve roots or cords within the plexus to discern which fibers go to which muscle (e.g. stimulate a root and observe finger flexion via EMG to know it’s the median nerve root). Free-running EMG in target muscles can alert to any traction on the plexus causing irritation. Because these nerves are often already injured preoperatively, baseline signals may be absent or abnormal, but any new EMG activity or particularly exuberant firing could signal intraoperative insult to a healthier portion of the plexus. Triggered EMG (or direct nerve stimulation) is also used after a repair (neurotization or graft) – stimulating proximal to the repair and seeing a muscle response across the repair confirms continuity. SSEPs and MEPs are generally less useful in peripheral nerve surgeries since the central pathways are not at risk; however, if a proximal injury like a brachial plexus avulsion is being addressed (e.g. nerve root grafting into the cord), SSEPs/MEPs might be used to verify intact connections to the cord .
* Peripheral Nerve Tumor Resection: For tumors of individual nerves (like a schwannoma of the sciatic nerve), the surgeon wants to remove the tumor while preserving functioning fascicles. Intraoperatively, stimulating each visible fascicle within the nerve can tell which fascicles produce distal muscle action (these should be preserved) versus which are silent (likely tumor or non-functional fibers). This is essentially a form of triggered EMG mapping. Meanwhile, continuous EMG in the muscles innervated by that nerve can signal if any functional fascicle is being traumatized during dissection (it will fire if irritated). The goal is to avoid postoperative weakness by keeping all functional fascicles intact.
* Nerve Transfers and Repairs: When surgeons perform nerve transfers (rerouting a donor nerve to reinnervate a denervated muscle) or direct nerve repairs, they may use IONM to confirm a successful connection. For instance, after suturing a nerve repair, stimulating proximal to the repair and recording distal CMAP (compound muscle action potential) or observing muscle twitch confirms that conduction is present. Additionally, monitoring ensures that as dissection is done to free nerve ends, no excessive traction is causing distal EMG activity (which could indicate injury).
* Other Peripheral Applications: In surgeries like thyroidectomy or parotidectomy, certain cranial/peripheral nerves (recurrent laryngeal nerve in thyroid, facial nerve in parotid) are at risk. Here, a form of peripheral nerve monitoring is widely used: e.g. an endotracheal tube with vocal cord electrodes will pick up EMG activity from the vocalis muscles, allowing identification and protection of the recurrent laryngeal nerve during thyroid surgery . Similarly, facial nerve monitoring in parotid surgery helps avoid inadvertent injury to facial nerve branches by giving instant feedback if the nerve is stimulated. These are essentially peripheral nerve cases where the same principles of EMG monitoring apply.
In all these peripheral scenarios, IONM serves to map nerve anatomy, prevent inadvertent damage, and test nerve function immediately after repair. This can improve surgical precision (identifying structures correctly) and outcomes (e.g. ensuring the recurrent laryngeal nerve is intact so the patient awakens without vocal cord palsy). While peripheral nerve IONM may be less “continuous” than spinal cord monitoring, it is critically useful in high-stakes moments of the operation.
### Conclusion
Intraoperative neuromonitoring has become an indispensable tool for neurosurgeons. By providing real-time functional assessment of neural pathways, it acts as the surgical team’s “eyes and ears” on the nervous system, beyond what the surgeon can directly see. The fundamental modalities – SSEPs, MEPs, EMG, BAEPs, EEG – each contribute unique information: from the status of the spinal cord’s tracts to the integrity of cranial nerves and the brain’s electrical activity. Interpreting these signals requires understanding baseline physiology, anesthesia interactions, and the significance of changes like amplitude loss or EMG bursts. When alarms occur, a coordinated, stepwise response involving both surgical maneuvers and anesthetic optimization is essential to protect the patient. IONM’s role spans nearly all realms of neurosurgery, enhancing safety in complex spine deformity corrections, guarding cranial nerve function in skull base tumors, mapping critical cortex in epilepsy surgery, and preserving peripheral nerve function in plexus repairs. For neurosurgery residents, mastery of IONM principles and collaboration with neurophysiology specialists is crucial – it enables them to perform daring surgeries with a safety net, ultimately improving patient outcomes and reducing neurologic complications  . By integrating IONM into intraoperative decision-making, neurosurgeons can operate with greater confidence that if something starts to go wrong neurologically, they will know early and can act to keep the patient safe.
