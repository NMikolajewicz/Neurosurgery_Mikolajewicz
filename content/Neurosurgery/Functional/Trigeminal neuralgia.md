
etiology of TN is believed to be related to abnormal conduction within the trigeminal nerve, possibly caused by changes in myelin induced by pulsatile mechanical trauma from an adjacent vessel. At the point just before it enters the brainstem, there is a short segment where nerve axons are still ensheathed in central myelin (produced by oligodendrocytes), but after a few millimeters, there is a transition to peripheral myelin (produced by Schwann cells). The region of this transition is called the _Obersteiner-Redlich zone_ .

nerve containing the central form of myelin is especially susceptible to pathologic changes from vascular contact that result in demyelination and altered conduction.

Separation of the nerve from the offending vessel appears to immediately reverse many of the physiologic changes

Radiographic and anatomic studies have demonstrated that vascular contact with the trigeminal nerve is common even in asymptomatic individuals but tends to be more severe and more proximal on the nerve ipsilateral to TN symptom

TN can occur in the absence of neurovascular compression; in one study of 184 patients with TN, 28.8% had no evidence of vascular compression.

For patients without neurovascular compression, trigeminal internal neurolysis (open rhizotomy) may be effective to control symptom

Cranial nerve (CN) V2 and V3 branches of the trigeminal nerve are more common sources of pain, 7especially radiating out from near the mouth

Patients with _TN type 1_ have predominantly shocklike pain, whereas patients with _TN type 2_ report that at least 50% is constant pain, although there still may be a component of lancinating pain. In the recent classification endorsed by the International Headache Society and the International Association for the Study of Pain, these two types of presentation are referred to as “paroxysmal only” and “with concomitant continuous pain” in relation to classical and idiopathic TN

patients with TN type 1 are more likely to do well than those with TN type 2

TN with a history of MS is called _symptomatic trigeminal neuralgia_ (STN). MS is present in 1% to 3% patients with TN, and 2% to 4% of patients with MS have TN, probably because of intrinsic demyelination within the nerve or increased sensitivity to vascular trauma


MVD
release of CSF from the trigeminal cistern relaxes the cerebellum enough to allow adequate retraction

The transverse sinus generally runs along a line connecting the inion to the external auditory meatus, parallel and posterior to the zygomatic arch. The sigmoid sinus runs along the digastric groove posterior to the mastoid eminence

The occipital artery is often encountered in the muscular tissue and sacrificed. A large mastoid emissary vein is usually seen at this point, which represents an important landmark because it overlies the junction of the transverse and sigmoid sinuses.

mastoid emissary vein is a valveless venous channel that passes through the mastoid emissary foramen behind the ear and connects extracranial scalp/posterior auricular or occipital veins with the sigmoid sinus or sometimes the transverse-sigmoid region intracranially. It is one of the skull’s emissary veins, which create venous communication between extracranial and intracranial systems.




The mastoid air cells are visible at the lateral margin of the bony opening and should be carefully waxed to prevent postoperative CSF rhinorrhea.

Superior petrosal vein (at junction of petrosal bone and tentorium) is key landmark for MVD
key is to orient yourself to the petrous ridge/tentorium superiorly, the superior petrosal vein complex nearby, and the root entry zone at the lateral pons, where the usual vascular conflict is found.

- After retrosigmoid exposure and CSF release, look anterior and superior to CN VII/VIII in the CPA.
- CN V sits more rostral/superior than the facial-vestibulocochlear bundle.
- It courses horizontally/slightly obliquely forward from the mid-pons toward Meckel’s cave.
- The superior petrosal vein (Dandy vein/SPV complex) often overlies or frames the operative corridor and is one of the most important landmarks around CN V exposure.

CNV course: lateral pons to meckels cave

  

differentiation from nearby cranial nerves
- CN V: larger, broader, flatter root; superior CPA
- CN VII/VIII: lower in the CPA, heading toward the internal auditory canal
- CN IX/X: still more caudal, toward the jugular foramen


  

Trigeminal MVD: 1-minute operative anatomy checklist

  

  

  

Setup and corridor

  

  

- Retrosigmoid exposure
- Relax cerebellum with CSF release
- Work in the superior CPA
- Preserve orientation to:  
    

- Petrous ridge / tentorium
- Superior petrosal vein complex
- Lateral pons

-   
    

  

  

  

Find CN V

  

  

- Look superior/anterior to CN VII/VIII
- Identify a broad, flat, white nerve root
- Trace it from the lateral mid-pons to the porus trigeminus/Meckel’s cave
- Confirm this is not:  
    

- VII/VIII running to IAC
- IX/X running caudally to jugular foramen

-   
    

  

  

  

Exposure

  

  

- Open arachnoid sharply over the superior CPA
- Skeletonize the trigeminal nerve enough to inspect:  
    

- Root entry zone
- Cisternal segment
- Porus trigeminus

-   
    
- Inspect superior, inferior, and ventral surfaces

  

  

  

Look for offenders

  

  

- Most common: SCA loop
- Also consider:  
    

- AICA
- Vertebrobasilar ectasia/loop
- Petrosal or trigeminal veins

-   
    

  

  

  

Decompression goal

  

  

- Offending vessel mobilized completely off the nerve
- No residual contact at the REZ
- No tethering arachnoid bands
- Reinspect entire visible course of CN V after padding/transposition

  

  

  

Pitfalls

  

  

- Incomplete view because of SPV/venous obstruction
- Missing a ventral compression
- Decompressing distal nerve but not true REZ
- Stopping before inspecting up to Meckel’s cave

  

  

  

Mental sequence

  

  

Tentorium/petrous ridge → SPV complex → lateral pons → CN V → REZ → full cisternal course → porus trigeminus
