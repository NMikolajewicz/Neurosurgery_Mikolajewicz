## **Adjacent Segment Disease (ASD)**

> [!summary] **Overview**  
> Adjacent Segment Disease refers to new degenerative changes at spinal levels above or below a prior fusion. It reflects the interplay between natural aging and altered biomechanics caused by eliminating motion at the fused level. ASD differs from _adjacent segment degeneration (ASDeg)_—radiographic changes alone—because ASD implies **clinical symptoms**.

---

### **Background**

- Occurs after spinal fusion designed to "lock" two or more vertebrae together.
- Eliminates natural motion at the fused level → altered stress distribution.
- ==Degenerative changes develop at the **motion segments above or below** the fusion construct==.
- Presents as new back/neck pain, radiculopathy, or myelopathy at adjacent levels.

---

### **Proposed Causes**

#### **1. Natural History / Aging**

- ==Degenerative cascade ==independent of surgery.
- Age-related disc desiccation → gradual disc height loss.
- Increased shear and axial load transmitted to adjacent levels.
- Difficult to distinguish from fusion-related degeneration, especially in older adults.
#### **2. Fusion-Related Biomechanics**

- ==Fusion alters normal motion== and load distribution:  
    – **Increased segmental motion and intradiscal pressure** at adjacent levels.  
    – Accelerates facet arthropathy, disc degeneration, and foraminal stenosis.
    
- More r==igid constructs (e.g., long fusions) may amplify== stresses, whereas motion-preserving techniques may mitigate them.

---

### **Incidence**

- **Lumbar spine:** 2–14% per year (Harrop 2008).
- **Cervical spine:** ~3% per year (Hilibrand 1999).  
    – 13.6% at 5 years; ==25.6% at 10 years== post-fusion.
    
- **Reoperation rate:** 22% at 10 years after cervical fusion (Lee 2015).
- Radiographic ASDeg is more common than clinically significant ASD.

---

### **Risk Factors**

> [!note] **Alignment Matters**  
> Restoring physiological **lordosis** during fusion is one of the strongest modifiable factors. Malalignment significantly increases adjacent level stress.

#### **Patient-Related**

- High BMI
- Preexisting adjacent disc degeneration
- Low preoperative adjacent disc height
- Older age (natural history contribution)

#### **Surgical / Biomechanical**

- Facet ==joint violation== (iatrogenic destabilization)
- Misalignment:  
    – Decreased pre and postoperative lumbar lordosis  
    – Increased postoperative **lumbopelvic mismatch (PI–LL mismatch)**  
    – Elevated postoperative pelvic incidence/pelvic tilt indicators of suboptimal spinopelvic balance  
    – Anterior shift of lumbosacral sagittal plumb line
    
- Improper restoration of cervical or lumbar lordosis
- Motion restriction from fusion alters adjacent segment loading.

#### **Operative Strategy Effects**

- **Minimally invasive techniques** → lower ASD incidence due to less soft-tissue disruption.
- ==**Multilevel fusion may carry _lower_ risk** than single-level fusion== (counterintuitive but supported by biomechanical studies—stress distributed across more levels).
- Instrumentation stiffness and graft choice may influence adjacent level stress.

---

### **Clinical Presentation (Common Patterns)**

- New radiculopathy at level adjacent to fusion
- Myelopathy (cervical)
- Axial pain with mechanical characteristics
- Radiographic findings:  
    – Disc height loss  
    – Foraminal stenosis  
    – Facet arthropathy  
    – Retrolisthesis/instability

---

### **Imaging**

- **X-ray:** alignment, instability, sagittal balance.
- **MRI:** disc degeneration, foraminal/Lateral recess stenosis.
- **CT:** facet arthropathy, bony stenosis.
- **Standing full-spine films** to assess global sagittal balance (critical in lumbar ASD).

---

### **Management** 

- **Conservative:** PT, weight reduction, NSAIDs, epidural/facet injections.
- **Surgical:** extension of fusion, decompression ± fusion, correction of malalignment.
- **Motion-preserving alternatives:** cervical disc arthroplasty or dynamic stabilization may reduce ASD risk in select patients.
