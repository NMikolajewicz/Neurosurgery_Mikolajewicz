## Risk

### Surgical risk

* 1% chance of clinically relevant ICH
* 1% chance of damaged abdominal viscera.
* 5-8% risk of shunt infection
* 50% of children require a shunt revision within 2 years

### Shunt failure predictors

==Failure of VP shunts occured in 8.4% of adult patients.==
Predictors of shunt failure

* A left-sided shunt
* a single operating surgeon
* shunts placed at night
* entry at Kocher's point were significantly associated with shunt failure.
* Although not associated with overall shunt failure, posthemorrhagic and postmeningitic hydrocephalus and CSF protein levels > 100 mg/dL were significantly associated with early shunt failure.

## Set up

* Pin head
* Neuro navigation
* Ventricle and peritoneal catheter
* Tunneler
* Shunt programmer
* Strata valve - initial setting 2.0
	* Consider fixed pressure valve for patients that require +++ MRI imaging
* +/- general surgery involvement

## Steps

* Landmarking
	* 7 cm superior and 5 cm lateral to inion
	* Candycane incision
* Cranial opening
	* Cut down but preserve galea and periosteom. Will use to anchor valve.
	* Hemostasis
	* Create a pocket between skin and galea by dissection with metz scissors
* Burrhole
	* Cauterize dura and open in cruciate manner
	* Cauterize arachnoid and using 11 blade to create opening in cortical surface
* Shunt preparation
	* Prime catheters in bacitracin water
	* Touch catheters only with bayonets
	* Assemble valve and peritoneal catheter on sterile surface. Tie with 2-0 silk tie.
* Tunneling
	* Abdo to neck (or neck to abdomen - kalia)
	* Can create intermediate opening just above clavicle if difficulty tunneling
	* Cover cranial surface with sterile green towels and place valve end of catheter once tunneled
* Ventricular catheter insertion
	* Use navigation
	* Insert into ventricle at frasers point
		* Hardpass 5 cm and confirm CSF flow
		* Softpass remaining 5 cm for total depth of 10 cm
	* Connect to valve and tienwith silk tie
	* Anchor valve to galea with silk sutures
	* Packman gel foam in burrhole
* Closure
	* 3-0 vicryl
	* +/- vancopowder (Kalia)

## Abdominal access

### Steps

* The abdominal incision is made on a point centered about 2 cm lateral and 1 cm superior to the umbilicus.
	* Staying above the umbilicus reduces the risk of bladder injury.
* The abdominal incision is widened to approximately 3 cm in length and is deepened through the fat and Scarpa's fascia to the anterior rectus sheath, which is opened transversely.
* The vertical fibers of the underlying rectus abdominis muscle are then visualized, and an artery forceps is used to split the fibers of the muscle to reach the posterior rectus sheath.
* Two small hemostat forceps are then used to lift the posterior rectus sheath, and using a to-and-fro motion alternating between the two forceps, the underlying bowel falls away from the rectus sheath, which is then incised transversely.
	* Sometimes, this simultaneously opens the underlying transversalis fascia and the peritoneum.
	* This is confirmed by the free passage of a blunt instrument into the peritoneal cavity

### Layers

1. Skin
2. Subcutaneous tissues
	1. Campers
	2. Scarpas
3. Anterior rectus sheath (fascia)
4. Rectus abdominalis (muscle)
5. Posterior rectus sheath (fascia)
6. Transverse fascia
7. Parietal peritoneum

![[Neurosurgery/Surgical/_resources/VP_shunt.resources/image.1.png]]
Subcutaneous fat is identified just below the skin. Scarpa’s fascia is a thin but definable layer, and more fat is seen below this fascia. The firm white anterior rectus sheath is opened transversely. The rectus muscle fibers are found just below the anterior rectus sheath, and these fibers are split vertically with a fine hemostat.

![[Neurosurgery/Surgical/_resources/VP_shunt.resources/image.2.png]]
The glistening white posterior rectus sheath is identified below the muscles, and the surgeon uses a fine hemostat to grab this sheath and pull it toward himself or herself. An area of approximately 1 cm is exposed by using a second hemostat to sweep muscle aside and then regrasp the sheath. By keeping tension toward the surgeon, any underlying bowel or viscera should fall away. The posterior rectus sheath is opened with scissors. The underlying transversalis fascia and peritoneum are often opened with the same cut. The transversalis fascia and the peritoneum will often be opened with this maneuver. A blunt instrument should pass deeply and not face any resistance.
