## Key Points (for quick reference)

* **Etiology:** Pars interarticularis defect (spondylolysis) allows vertebral slippage.
* **Prevalence:** ~5–8% of adults; more common in males; often at L5–S1.
* **Presentation:** Low back pain (extension-aggravated), possible radiculopathy; exam may show step-off and positive Stork test.
* **Imaging:** X-ray (slip grading), CT (pars defect), MRI (neural compression).
* **Classification:** Meyerding Grade I–V by % slip; Wiltse Type II = isthmic.
* **Natural History:** Low-grade slips usually stable; neurological decline rare unless high-grade.
* **Non-op Rx:** NSAIDs, PT (core/hamstring), brace/injections as needed.
* **Surgery:** Indicated for refractory pain or neuro deficits; typically **decompression + instrumented fusion** (PLF ± interbody). MIS-TLIF and pars repair are viable techniques in select patients.
* **Complications:** Includes infection, dural tear, pseudarthrosis, adjacent-segment disease.
* **Guidelines:** NASS (2014) advises fusion for symptomatic isthmic cases; AANS/CNS endorse multidisciplinary spine guidelines (LBP, radiculopathy).
* **Emerging data:** High-level evidence favors fusion over decompression alone; new studies explore motion-sparing repairs and MIS approaches.

## Pathophysiology and Etiology

* Isthmic (lytic) spondylolisthesis results from a ==defect or stress fracture in the== **==pars interarticularis==** of a lumbar vertebra (spondylolysis)
	* **Spondylolysis** is a defect or stress fracture in the _pars interarticularis_ of the vertebral arch
	* most ==commonly at L5==
	* “Scotty dog collar” on oblique X-ray. 
* Consequences of pars defect
	* disconnects the posterior vertebral elements
	* **destabilizes the segment**
	* allows the vertebral body to slip forward under axial and shear loads. 
* Repetitive hyperextension (e.g. gymnastics, football linemen) or acute trauma can induce fatigue fractures of the pars. 
* Congenital factors (a congenitally thin pars or dysplastic facet joints) and biomechanical factors (high pelvic incidence or sacral slope) increase shear forces at L5–S1 and predispose to slip. 
* In summary, ==a pars defect (acquired or congenital) + unfavorable load geometry → forward translation of the vertebra.==

## Epidemiology

* Isthmic spondylolisthesis is relatively common
* lifetime prevalence of pars defects: 5–10%
	* up to 11.5% by CT-based studies
	* 4–8% of adults have anterolisthesis due to isthmic slip. 
* In community cohorts, lumbar spondylolysis was seen in ~11.5% of adults (with male:female ~3:1). 
* Most isthmic slips occur at L5–S1. 
* Risk factors include
	* athletic activities that repeatedly hyperextend the low back (gymnastics, diving, football, weightlifting)
	* occupational heavy lifting
	* genetic predisposition (family history, spina bifida occulta)
* Isthmic slips tend to manifest clinically in early adulthood (20s–40s) i
* Male patients predominate in isthmic slip (by contrast, degenerative slips occur more in older women).

## Clinical Presentation

* may be **asymptomatic**
* **mechanical low back pain**.
	* chronic, intermittent
	* **worse with activity or lumbar extension**
	* relieved by rest (e.g. lying supine). 
* Radicular symptoms +/- claudication
	* often L5 distribution
* Exam findings
	* **step-off** at the lumbosacral junction
	* increased lumbar lordosis from truncal retroflexion (a “hanging-off” posture)
	* L5 root findings (e.g. ankle dorsiflexion weakness or diminished reflexes) if foraminal stenosis is present.
* Paraspinal muscle spasm and _hamstring tightness_ are common.
* ==The Stork (“one-leg hyperextension”)== test is often positive:
	* standing on one leg and extending the spine reproduces pain
	* indicates mechanical instability. 
* **Symptoms:** Axial low back pain (often worsened by extension), buttock pain, possible radicular leg pain; standing/walking may exacerbate symptoms if stenosis exists.
* **Physical Exam:** Lumbosacral step-off, hyperlordosis, tenderness over the pars region; pain on lumbar extension; neurologic deficits (motor/sensory/reflex changes) consistent with nerve root compression.

## Imaging and Diagnosis

* **Plain radiographs**
	* standing AP and lateral views (first-line)
	* **lateral film** quantifies vertebral slippage
		* anterior translation is graded by Meyerding criteria
			* Grade I = 1–25% slip
			* Grade II = 26–50%
			* Grade III = 51–75%
			* Grade IV = 76–100%
			* Grade V >100% or spondyloptosis
* **Dynamic (flexion-extension) X-rays**
	* assess for instability or slip progression. 
* **Oblique views** can demonstrate the pars defect as the “Scotty dog collar” sign
* **Computed tomography (CT)**
	* gold standard to confirm an isthmic defect
* **Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)**
	* essential for preoperative evaluation
	* Identifies disc degeneration, degree of central or foraminal stenosis, and nerve root compression.
	* isthmic spondylolisthesis usually _does not_ cause central canal stenosis the way degenerative slip can
		* the broken pars prevents laminar load shift. 
* **nuclear medicine (SPECT)**
	* occasionally used in young patients to detect active bone stress at the pars.
* Key diagnostic features on imaging are:
	* **Pars defect on CT** (nonunion or stress fracture) – diagnostic of spondylolysis.
	* **Anterior slip on lateral X-ray** (Meyerding Grade).
	* **MRI findings** of disc/foraminal narrowing correlating with clinical radiculopathy.

## Classification Systems

* **Meyerding Classification:**
	* Grades I–IV (and V) by percent slip on lateral X-ray
	* Low-grade = I–II (<50%)
	* High-grade = III–IV (>50%)
	* Grade V (spondyloptosis) is rare in adults.
* **Wiltse Classification (by etiology):**
	* Type I (dysplastic/congenital)
	* **==Type II (isthmic)==** ==– defects in pars interarticularis==
	* Type III (degenerative)
	* Type IV (traumatic fracture of posterior elements)
	* Type V (pathologic lesion).

## Natural History and Prognosis

* Low-grade isthmic slips
	* Most **low-grade (I-II) isthmic slips** in adults are **stable and benign**. 
	* Significant progression of slippage after skeletal maturity is uncommon. 
	* ~5% patients with pars defect develop slippage
	* slip progression tends to occur during adolescence when growth is ongoing. 
	* **adults with low-grade slip rarely worsen markedly over time**.
* High-grade isthmic slips
	* dysplastic or those in growing adolescents have worse prognosis.
	* complications
		* produce larger deformity
		* biomechanical instability
		* sagittal imbalance and spinal deformity (often necessitating surgical correction)
		* neurological deterioration (e.g. progressive radiculopathy or cauda equina)
* In summary
	* **==low-grade, adult isthmic slip – usually stable with good prognosis==**
	* **==high-grade slip – guarded prognosis, often requiring fusion to prevent progression and neurologic injury==**==.==

## Non-operative Management

* Initial treatment is **conservative** for most adult lytic spondylolisthesis without red-flag features. 
	* goals are pain control and functional improvement.
* Conservative measures:
	* **Physical therapy:** Core stabilization exercises, hamstring stretching, and controlled lumbar flexion/extension programs to improve muscular support and flexibility.
	* **Medications:** NSAIDs or analgesics for pain/inflammation.
	* **Activity modification:** Avoidance of repetitive lumbar extension or heavy lifting.
	* **Bracing:** Temporary use of lumbar-sacral orthosis (especially in younger patients or acute presentations) can offload the injured pars and reduce motion.
	* **Epidural injections:** Transforaminal or interlaminar steroid injections can help relieve radicular pain if nerve root irritation is present. Injections are used cautiously and selectively.
* A trial of 3–6 months of non-operative therapy is typically recommended unless there are progressive neurologic deficits. 
	* _Evidence:_  Randomized trials (e.g. Möller 2000) showed only slight improvement with exercise vs fusion; but exercise did not worsen outcomes. Conservative care remains first-line in stable, low-grade cases.

## Surgical Management

* **Indications:**
	* symptoms are **severe, disabling, or progressive despite adequate conservative treatment**. 
	* incapacitating low back or leg pain (especially if ≥6–12 months) not relieved by non-operative care
	* new or worsening neurological deficits (radiculopathy or claudication)
	* significant slip progression on follow-up films
	* development of cauda equina syndrome. 
	* Instability on dynamic imaging (a slip that enlarges on flexion) 
* In practice, 10-20% adult isthmic patients fail conservative management and require surgery.
* **Surgical goals**
	* Decompress neural elements (if compressed)
	* correct deformity/alignment if needed
	* achieve a solid fusion to stabilize the segment and prevent further slip.
* **Approaches:**
	* **posterior decompression and instrumented fusion**
		* midline laminectomy or “Gill” decompression (removing loose pars fragments and lamina)
		* **pedicle screw instrumentation** and bone graft fusion. 
		* Common fusion techniques include:
			* **==Posterolateral fusion (PLF):==**
				* Bone graft placed over decorticated transverse processes, with pedicle screws bilaterally.
			* **==Posterior/Transforaminal Interbody Fusion (PLIF/TLIF)==**
				* Cage + bone graft inserted between the vertebral bodies (via posterior approach) to restore disc height and aid fusion.
				* Interbody fusion used to improve fusion rates and lumbar lordosis.
			* **Direct pars repair:**
				* In selected young patients with minimal slip, direct repair of the pars (e.g. Buck’s screw or pedicle screw with hook) can be considered to preserve motion.
				* in adults with established slippage, direct repair is less common.
	* **Minimally invasive vs open**
		* Minimally invasive (MI) techniques
			* MI-TLIF, percutaneous screws, tubular laminectomy
			* MIS fusion yields similar fusion and clinical results to open surgery, with less muscle disruption.
* **Outcomes:**
	* Fusion rates approach 95–100% with modern techniques. 
	* Most report substantial pain relief and functional gain. 
	* Möller & Hedlund (2000)
		* fusion group had significantly better reduction of low back pain and disability than the conservative group.
	* Azizpour et al. (2023)
		* decompression+fusion provided superior 2-year outcomes (Oswestry scores, recovery rates) compared to decompression alone. 
		* Fusion (with instrumented stabilization) is therefore essential for optimal, lasting results in adults.
* **Complications:**
	* cerebrospinal fluid leak/dural tear
	* neural injury (nerve root deficit)
	* implant malposition
	* infection
	* blood loss
	* pseudoarthrosis (non-union).
		* Pseudarthrosis rates are lower with rigid fixation but can be 5–15%, especially in smokers or poor bone. 
	* adjacent-segment degeneration over the long term.

## Guidelines (NASS, AANS/CNS)

Evidence-based guidelines inform management: the North American Spine Society (NASS) developed guidelines for adult isthmic spondylolisthesis (published 2014) and for degenerative spondylolisthesis.  The key recommendations are:

* **Imaging:**
	* standing lateral X-rays (with flex/ext) to diagnose and grade slip (Meyerding)
	* MRI to evaluate neural compression
	* CT to confirm pars defects.
	* Imaging should correlate with symptoms.
* **Non-operative care first**
	* In absence of severe deficits or instability, begin with medical/interventional therapy (NSAIDs, PT, epidural, bracing).
* **Surgery indications**
	* Persistent, disabling pain despite ≥6 months of conservative care, or neurologic deficits, warrant surgery.
* **Fusion over decompression alone**
	* NASS specifically notes that **decompression with instrumented fusion** yields better outcomes than decompression alone in lumbar spondylolisthesis. 
	* The guidelines suggest decompression+fusion is the preferred surgical treatment for symptomatic isthmic patients (especially adults).
* **Alignment considerations**
	* Evaluation of spinopelvic parameters is advised (pelvic incidence, lumbar lordosis) for surgical planning.

The American Association of Neurological Surgeons (AANS) and Congress of Neurological Surgeons (CNS) have endorsed these guidelines (and broader lumbar spine guidelines on back pain/radiculopathy). In practice, spine surgeons follow similar principles: ==trial conservative therapy, obtain MRI to guide treatment, and fuse unstable isthmic segments.==

## Emerging Evidence

Recent studies have refined our understanding and technique:

* A **prospective Dutch cohort (2023)** compared decompression alone versus decompression + fusion in adult isthmic spondylolisthesis.  At 2-year follow-up, **fusion+decompression produced superior functional outcomes and recovery rates** (lower disability scores and higher patient-perceived recovery) than decompression alone.  All reoperations occurred in the decompression-only group.  Meta-analysis of this and a prior RCT confirmed a significant advantage for fusion, supporting guideline recommendations.
* **Pars repair vs fusion (2025):** Gao et al. retrospectively compared direct pars repair (pedicle screw-rod-hook fixation) vs TLIF in 140 adults with low-grade slip. Both approaches improved pain and function similarly, but pars repair preserved segmental motion and had comparable fusion rates (97%) to TLIF (100%). This suggests motion-preserving repair can be an effective alternative in select cases, although long-term data are pending.
* **Minimally invasive techniques:** Ali et al. (2022) found that MIS-TLIF in low-grade isthmic cases significantly improved back/leg pain and disability scores. All patients achieved or maintained sagittal balance, indicating MIS approaches can correct alignment safely. Other studies likewise report good outcomes with MIS fusion, including shorter hospital stays and quicker mobilization, without compromising fusion.
* **Interbody fusion trends:** There is a growing use of interbody fusion for isthmic slip. A 2021 review noted increased posterior interbody fusion (e.g. TLIF) usage for slip reduction and fusion stability. Early evidence suggests that adding anterior column support may improve fusion rates in high-grade slips.

No recent high-quality studies directly contradict current guidelines. Instead, the emerging literature supports the practice of instrumented fusion for adult isthmic spondylolisthesis and expands surgical options. Ongoing research is evaluating patient-specific factors (pelvic parameters, bone quality) and the role of newer implants or biologics. Spine societies continue to update guidelines as new evidence accrues.
