> [!summary] **Cauda Equina Syndrome Review**  
> • CES = **compression of L2–S5 roots**, most often from a **large extruded L4–5 or L5–S1 disc**; a true **surgical emergency**.  
> • Key symptoms: **urinary retention**, saddle anesthesia, bilateral sciatica, bowel/sexual dysfunction, LE weakness.  
> • **Urinary retention is most predictive**: ==PVR **>200 mL = sensitive**, **>500 mL = highly specific**==.  
> • **Normal PVR does NOT exclude CES**—MRI is mandatory whenever suspicion persists.  
> • MRI lumbar spine is the **gold standard**; red flags alone are unreliable.  
> • Best outcomes with ==**decompression within 24 hours**== (benefit up to 48 h).  
> • Etiologies beyond disc herniation include hematoma, abscess, tumor, trauma, post-op compression.

## **1. Definition & Etiology**

- Cluster of symptoms and exam findings due to ==**compression of the cauda equina** (L2–S5 roots)==.
- **Most common cause:** acutely **extruded lumbar disc** (often large central/ paracentral L4–5 or L5–S1 herniations).
- Other causes:
    - Severe lumbar stenosis
    - Epidural hematoma or abscess
    - Tumors (primary or metastatic)
    - Trauma / burst fractures
    - Postoperative compression (hematoma, retained material)

## **2. Classic Clinical Features**

> [!warning] **Important:** No single "red flag" reliably predicts CES—absence does _not_ exclude the diagnosis. MRI is required whenever suspicion is significant.

### **Common Findings**

- Saddle anesthesia or perineal sensory loss
- Urinary retention (most reliable)
- Overflow incontinence or decreased urinary stream
- Bowel dysfunction
- Sexual dysfunction
- Sciatica (often bilateral but may be unilateral early)
- Lower extremity weakness (especially S1)
- Diminished ankle reflexes

---

## **3. Workup**

### **A. Post-Void Residual (PVR)**

- **≤200 mL:**
    - Lowers likelihood _but does not exclude CES_.
    - Katzouraki et al.: ==PVR ≤200 mL **+ absence of clinical red flags → 98.7% likelihood of negative MRI**.==
- **>200 mL:**
    - **Sensitive** for detecting CES.
- **>500 mL:**
    - **Highly specific** for CES (strong predictor of significant compression).

> [!tip] **Pearl:** PVR is an adjunct—not a rule-out test. A normal PVR cannot safely exclude CES in a concerning clinical scenario.

---

### **B. MRI — Gold Standard**

- **Always indicated** when CES is clinically suspected.
- Urgent MRI of the lumbar spine (with contrast if infection or tumor is possible).
- Imaging must assess:
    - Degree of thecal sac compression
    - Etiology (disc, hematoma, abscess, tumor)
    - Level-specific nerve root compression

## **4. Management**

> [!important] **CES = Surgical Emergency**
> 
> - Emergent **lumbar decompression** (usually laminectomy + diskectomy).
> 
> - Best neurological outcomes occur when decompression is performed ==**within 24 hours** of symptom onset (some data extend benefit to ≤48 hours)==.
> 
> - Address underlying cause (infection → antibiotics + drainage, hematoma evacuation, tumor resection/stabilization).
> 
> - Foley catheter if urinary retention is significant.

---

## **5. Clinical Pearls**

> [!tip] **High-Yield Pearls**  
> • **Urinary retention (>500 mL PVR)** is the _strongest clinical predictor_ of CES.  
> • **Normal PVR does NOT rule out** — MRI required if suspicion persists.  
> • Bilateral sciatica, saddle anesthesia, and sexual dysfunction greatly raise concern.  
> • CES may present subtly; even isolated severe radicular pain + urinary hesitancy warrants imaging.  
> • MRI is the **only definitive** diagnostic test.
