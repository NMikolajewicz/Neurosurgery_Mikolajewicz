> [!summary] **Posterior Fossa Syndrome (Cerebellar Mutism Syndrome)**  
> • Classically occurs after resection of midline posterior fossa tumors in children, especially medulloblastoma.  
> • <u>Onset</u>: typically delayed ==24–72 hours post-op==
> • <u>Presentation</u>: ==mutism== or markedly diminished speech despite preserved comprehension.  
> • <u>Associated findings</u>: ==emotional lability==, ==ataxia==, ==hypotonia==, dysarthria, dysphagia, and impaired initiation.  
> • <u>Pathophysiology</u>: disruption of cerebellar outflow pathways (==dentate nuclei== and superior cerebellar peduncles) leading to cerebello-cerebral diaschisis. 
> • <u>==Dentatothalamocortical tract==:</u> dentate nucleus - superior cerebellar peduncle - midbrain tegmentum - contralateral ventrolateral nucleus of thalamus - cortical projections
> • <u>Risk</u>: ==midline vermian approaches==, extensive retraction, large tumors, and pre-existing brainstem compression.  
> • <u>Imaging</u>: MRI may show signal change in the dentate nuclei or cerebellar peduncles, but imaging can also be normal.  
> • <u>Duration</u>: Syndrome often persists for weeks to months; long-term deficits in speech, executive function, and behavior are common.  
> • <u>Management</u> is supportive: early rehabilitation (SLP/PT/OT), behavioral therapy, and minimizing additional cerebellar injury intraoperatively.  
> • <u>Prognosis</u> is variable—many recover functional speech, but subtle cognitive and affective deficits frequently remain.

## Definition and Overview

Posterior Fossa Syndrome (PFS) is a constellation of neuropsychiatric and behavioral symptoms that develops in approximately 25% of children following surgical resection of posterior fossa tumors. The syndrome primarily affects children who have undergone treatment for medulloblastoma and other tumors in the posterior fossa, which contains the brainstem and cerebellum.

## Historical Context and Terminology

* First described by Hirsch et al. in 1979 when examining outcomes in children with medulloblastoma
* First case series by Rekate et al. in 1985 (termed "cerebellar muteness")
* Has been classified under various names over 35+ years:
	* Pseudobulbar palsy
	* Mutism without behavioral impairment
	* Cerebellar speech syndrome
	* Cerebellar mutism
	* Cerebellar mutism and subsequent dysarthria
	* Posterior fossa syndrome (PFS)
	* Cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome (CCAS)

## Epidemiology

* Occurs in ==approximately 25% of children after posterior fossa tumor resection
	* Pollack's study: 8.5% of 142 children exhibited postoperative mutism
	* Robertson's prospective study: 24% of 450 patients developed PFS
	* Korah's retrospective review: 29% of 63 patients developed PFS
* Medulloblastoma (most common malignant pediatric brain tumor) accounts for 20% of CNS tumors in childhood
* Diagnosed in approximately 400 children in the US annually
## Risk Factors

* Preoperative language impairment
* Preoperative behavioral disorders
* Larger tumor size
* Left-handedness
* Midline location of tumor
* Aggressive surgery of the cerebellum
* Vermis involvement

## Clinical Presentation and Symptoms

### Core Features

1. **Mutism and Speech Changes**
	Complete loss of speech Decreased speech production Dysarthria Changes in speech quality, prosody, and initiation
	
2. **Ataxia**
	Unsteady gait/balance Hypotonia
	
3. **Behavioral Symptoms**
	Regressed personality Physical agitation (e.g., restlessness) Poverty of spontaneous movement Withdrawal
	
4. **Emotional Lability**
	Dysphoria Apathy Distress Inconsolability Tearfulness Giggling Distractibility Irritability

### Onset and Duration

* Symptoms typically emerge 1-4 days after surgery
	* Immediate: 6/18 patients (Korah et al.)
	* 1-2 days postop: 7/18 patients
	* 2-4 days postop: 5/18 patients
	* No patients developed PFS after 4 days
* Robertson severity scale:
	* Mild: symptoms present <1 week (8% of cases)
	* Moderate: 2 features lasting 1-4 weeks or 1 feature 1-4 weeks + 1 feature >4 weeks
	* Severe: 2 features lasting >4 weeks
* 92% of PFS cases have moderate or severe symptoms
### Speech Symptoms

* May include complete mutism or changes in speech quality
* Voice changes (high-pitched, hypernasal, monotone)
* Difficulty initiating conversations
* Slower speech rate, fewer words
* Robotic quality with loss of melody
* Dysarthria during recovery phase
* Some develop whispered voice or high-pitched "whiny" voice
* Some use expletive language during recovery

### Motor Symptoms

* Primarily ataxia affecting gait
* Some have truncal ataxia and axial hypotonia
* May include cranial nerve deficits leading to dysphagia
* Facial or abducens nerve weakness possible

### Mood/Behavioral Symptoms

* Range from withdrawn with flat affect to agitated and inconsolable
* Common: irritability and emotional lability
* Screaming, high-pitched crying
* Inattention, tantrums
* Apathy, dysphoria
* Delirium (reported in some cases)

## Proposed Etiologies

Multiple theories exist, including:

* Involvement of the vermis
* Brainstem involvement
* Bilateral edema within cerebellar peduncles after surgery
* Transient impairment of afferent/efferent pathways of dentate nuclei
* Disruption of dentatothalamocortical pathways
* Focal vasospasm
* Neurotransmitter dysfunction
* Hydrocephalus

## Neuroanatomical Basis and Etiologies

### Dentatothalamocortical Pathway

Dentatothalamocortical tract: dentate nucleus - superior cerebellar peduncle - midbrain tegmentum - contralateral ventrolateral nucleus of thalamus - cortical projections

The ==dentatothalamocortical tract== is a critical cerebellar efferent pathway:

* Originates in ==dentate nuclei ==within cerebellum
* Projects through ipsilateral ==superior cerebellar peduncle==
* Decussates in midbrain tegmentum
* Synapses with contralateral **ventrolateral nucleus of thalamus**
* Second-order axons project to primary motor cortex and association areas in frontal/parietal lobes
* Modulates both motor activity and cognitive/behavioral functions

### Evidence for Dentatothalamocortical Involvement in PFS

Research by Morris et al. (2009) demonstrated:

* Patients with PFS had tumors located higher in the 4th ventricle (p=0.035)
* Postoperative MRI showed more frequent signal abnormalities in superior cerebellar peduncles (p=0.030) and midbrain (p=0.003) in PFS patients
* Diffusion tensor imaging revealed lower fractional anisotropy (FA) (I.e. more disrupted fiber tract integrity):
	* Bilateral superior cerebellar peduncle
	* Bilateral fornices
	* White matter proximate to right angular gyrus
	* White matter proximate to left superior frontal gyrus
	* Multiple bilateral injuries to proximal dentatothalamocortical pathways appear necessary for PFS development

90% of PFS patients showed signal abnormalities in ≥3 structures (pons, dentate, superior cerebellar peduncles, midbrain)

==80% of PFS patients had bilateral injuries vs. only 15% of non-PFS patients==

## Relationship to CCAS

* CCAS (Cerebellar Cognitive Affective Syndrome) described by Schmahmann
* CCAS characterized by: Deficit in emotional regulation Personality changes Blunting of affect Disinhibited behaviors Impairment of executive functions Disruption of spatial cognition Language deficits
* Key differences between PFS and CCAS: PFS hallmark is postoperative mutism beginning 1-2 days after surgery Mutism not required for CCAS CCAS can manifest acutely or due to chronic cerebellar disruption
## Treatment Approaches

### Non-pharmacological

* Speech therapy
* Physical therapy
* Occupational therapy
* Behavioral therapy

### Pharmacological (off-label use)

* **Atypical antipsychotics**
	* Risperidone (most commonly used, starting at 0.125mg twice daily)
	* Olanzapine (used less as first-line due to sedating effects)
	* Aripiprazole (case report of improvement in delirium symptoms)
* **Benzodiazepines**
	* Lorazepam
	* Clonazepam
* **Other medications**
	* Carbamazepine (reported effective in Turkel's case series)
	* Zolpidem (case report of paradoxical improvement)

## Long-term Outcomes and Sequelae

### Speech Recovery

* Speech generally recovers but often with persistent impairment
* Complete recovery of speech and language is infrequent
* Often slowly improving dysarthria over many months

### Cognitive Outcomes

* Significantly lower performance in multiple domains after 1 year: Processing speed Attention Working memory Executive function Cognitive efficiency Reading, speaking, and math
* Below average intellectual ability that remains below average up to 5 years post-diagnosis
* Significant decline in reading ability
* High risk for poor academic outcomes

### Behavioral and Psychosocial Outcomes

More likely to exhibit:

* Obsessive-compulsive behaviors
* Withdrawal behaviors
* Social problems
* Internalizing problems
* Anxious-shy behaviors (60% vs 17% in comparison sample)
* Somatic complaints
* Attention problems

### Resolution of Symptoms

* At 1-year follow-up among those with severe PFS:
	* Persistent ataxia: 92%
	* Speech and language dysfunction: 66%
	* Global intellectual handicap: 59%
* At 5-year follow-up (Korah et al.):
	* All patients recovering
	* Only 22% fully recovered
	* Most common residual symptoms: ataxia and dysarthric speech

## Case Examples

### Case 1: 7-year-old boy with medulloblastoma

* Symptoms: Mutism, left-sided weakness, irritability
* Speech: Initially mute, then dysarthric with robotic patterns
* Behavior: Outbursts, crying, mood lability
* Treatment: Risperidone (0.125mg BID, titrated to 0.25mg BID)
* Outcome: After 2 years, speech fluent, ongoing balance issues, emotionally stable

### Case 2: 10-year-old boy with medulloblastoma

* Symptoms: Mutism, ataxia, disinhibition, impulsivity, agitation, crying
* Treatment: Lorazepam 0.5mg PRN with good benefit
* Outcome: Slowly regained speech, remained ataxic, tumor recurred, died 2 years after diagnosis

### Case 3: 13-year-old boy with medulloblastoma

* Symptoms: Paucity of speech (not mute), left-sided weakness, severe ataxia, sixth nerve palsy
* Behavior: Outbursts, screaming, screeching, later flat and withdrawn
* Treatment: No medications administered
* Outcome: At 5 years, college freshman doing well academically but struggling socially/emotionally

### Case 4: 3-year-old girl with ependymoma

* Symptoms: Dysarthria, right-sided weakness, inability to ambulate independently
* Behavior: Tantrums, aggression, self-injury and injury to others
* Treatment: Risperidone 0.125mg BID, discontinued after radiation
* Outcome: At 5 years, engaging and well-behaved with resolved speech and behavior issues, ongoing balance challenges
