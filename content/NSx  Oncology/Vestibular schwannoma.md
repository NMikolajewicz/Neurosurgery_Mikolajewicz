## Vestibular Schwannoma (Acoustic Neuroma)

see [[Koos classification of vestibular schwannomas]]  
see [[Serviceable hearing]]

---

> [!summary] Big picture
> 
> - Benign Schwann-cell tumor, usually from the **superior vestibular nerve** in the IAC/CPA.
> 
> - Key surgical priorities: **preserve facial nerve (CN VII)** and **preserve hearing if serviceable**.
> 
> - Management triad: **observe vs stereotactic radiosurgery vs microsurgical resection** (approach tailored to Koos grade, hearing status, age/comorbidities).

---

### Epidemiology

- Incidence: ~**1 / 100,000** per year
- ~**8%** of all intracranial tumors
- Peak age: **40–60 years**
- Sex: slight **female** predominance
- Genetics: associated with **NF2 gene** on chromosome **22q12**

#### Neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2)

- Clinical hallmark: **bilateral vestibular schwannomas are pathognomonic**
- Inheritance: **autosomal dominant** defect in **NF2** gene
- Clinical implications:
    - Presentation in **2nd–3rd decade**
    - **More aggressive** growth
    - **Multifocal disease**: meningiomas, ependymomas, other cranial and spinal schwannomas

---

### Natural History and Growth

- Growth behavior:
    - **Not all tumors grow**
    - ~**50% remain radiographically stable** over time
- Growth rate (when growing):
    - Average **1–2 mm/year**
- Cystic variants:
    - **10–20%** of vestibular schwannomas
    - **Grow faster**, more **aggressive**, and **unpredictable**
    - **More difficult** microsurgical dissection; higher risk to CN VII and cochlear nerve
- Clinical correlation:
    - **Growth rate does _not_ correlate** reliably with degree of hearing loss

### Key Anatomy

#### CPA (Cerebellopontine Angle) Anatomy

- **Superior**: tentorium
- **Inferior**: CN IX–XI
- **Medial**: pons and medulla
- **Lateral**: posterior surface of the petrous temporal bone

![[Pasted image 20251211205246.png|500]]

#### IAC Anatomy – "7-up, Coke-down"

> [!tip] 7-up, Coke-down rule
> 
> - **Supero-anterior**: CN VII (facial)
> 
> - **Supero-posterior**: superior vestibular nerve
> 
> - **Infero-anterior**: cochlear nerve
> 
> - **Infero-posterior**: inferior vestibular nerve

- **Bill's bar**:
    - Vertical bony crest separating **facial nerve (anterior)** from **superior vestibular nerve (posterior)**
    - **Critical surgical landmark** to identify and protect CN VII, especially with fundal tumors

![[Pasted image 20251205100652.png|300]]

##### Porus Acousticus vs Fundus

- **Porus acusticus internus**:
    - Medial opening of the IAC into posterior fossa
- **Fundus**:
    - Lateral **blind end** of the IAC, near labyrinth
- **Surgical relevance**:
    - **Tumor extension to the fundus** makes **hearing preservation** significantly more difficult
    - Often requires more extensive IAC drilling and risks devascularizing the cochlear nerve

#### Vascular Relationships

> [!warning] Vessels at risk in VS surgery  
> Injury can produce brainstem infarcts, cerebellar edema, and/or hearing loss.

- **AICA (Anterior Inferior Cerebellar Artery)**
    - Origin: **basilar artery**
    - Often has a loop that **courses deep into the CPA**, sometimes entering the IAC (**subarcuate loop**)
    - Frequently **adherent to tumor capsule**
    - Injury → **lateral pontine syndrome** and **hearing loss** (via **labyrinthine artery** branch)
- **PICA (Posterior Inferior Cerebellar Artery)**
    - Origin: **vertebral artery**
    - Located **inferior** in CPA; **large tumors displace it**
    - Injury → **Wallenberg (lateral medullary) syndrome**
- **Petrosal vein (Dandy's vein)**
    - Drains cerebellum and brainstem into **superior petrosal sinus**
    - Ideally preserved; sacrifice can → **venous infarct**, cerebellar swelling, catastrophic brain edema
- **Sigmoid sinus**
    - **Posterior limit** of translabyrinthine approach
    - Limits **lateral exposure** in retrosigmoid approach

#### Trautmann's Triangle

- Key **dural window** for combined or **retrolabyrinthine** approaches
    - **Superior**: superior petrosal sinus
    - **Posterior**: sigmoid sinus
    - **Anterior**: solid angle / posterior semicircular canal

#### Cranial Nerve Displacement (Typical Pattern)

- **CN V**: displaced **superiorly and anteriorly**
- **CN VII**: displaced **anteriorly**, stretched over **anterior dome** of tumor capsule
- **CN VIII**: **nerve of origin**, usually **engulfed** or displaced **posteriorly**
- **CN IX, X, XI**: displaced **inferiorly** in large tumors

> [!important] Nerve that must be preserved  
> **Major cranial nerve to spare during VS surgery: ==CN VII==**

![[IMG_0321.PNG|500]]

#### Obersteiner–Redlich Zone

- Transition zone between **central (oligodendrocyte)** and **peripheral (Schwann cell)** myelin
- Vestibular schwannomas originate near this zone at or just lateral to the **porus acusticus**
- Also a key concept in **trigeminal neuralgia** (site of vascular compression on CN V)

### Pathology

#### Gross Pathology

- Well-circumscribed, **encapsulated**, firm mass
- Can be **solid** or **cystic**; cystic component often correlates with more rapid growth

#### Microscopic Pathology

- **Antoni A** areas:
    - Dense, cellular, compact spindle cells
- **Antoni B** areas:
    - Looser, myxoid, hypocellular regions
- **Verocay bodies**:
    - Pathognomonic pattern for schwannomas
    - Stacked, parallel rows of palisading nuclei separated by fibrillary processes
- Immunoprofile:
    - **Positive**: S100, SOX10, vimentin
    - **Negative**: EMA (helps distinguish from meningioma), GFAP (distinguish from glioma)

#### Cystic Schwannomas

- ~**10–20%** of vestibular schwannomas
- Often:
    - **Faster-growing**
    - **More aggressive** clinically
    - **More difficult** to dissect off CN VII and brainstem
- Practical takeaway:
    - Lower thresholds for **early intervention** and **honest counseling** re: facial nerve outcomes

### Clinical Presentation

#### Auditory Symptoms

- **Sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL)** – most common, ~**95%**
    - Classically **progressive high-frequency loss**
    - Mechanisms:
        - Direct **compression** of cochlear nerve
        - **Interruption of cochlear blood supply** (AICA/labyrinthine artery)
- **Sudden SNHL**
    - Occurs in **10–25%**
    - Likely a **vascular event** (ischemia/infarct of cochlea or cochlear nerve)
- **Tinnitus**
    - Present in **60–70%** of patients
    - Typically unilateral, same side as tumor

#### Facial Nerve (CN VII)

- **Facial weakness is rare pre-op**: <**5%**
    - Motor nerves tolerate **slow compression** well
- Subtle sign:
    - **Hitselberger's sign**: decreased sensation in **posterior EAC** (CN VII sensory component)

#### Trigeminal Nerve (CN V) – Mid/Late Sign

- **Corneal reflex loss**
- **Hyperesthesia** in **V1–V3** distribution
- Can present with **tic douloureux** (mimicking classical trigeminal neuralgia)

#### Mass Effect (Large Tumors > 3–4 cm)

- Brainstem compression:
    - Long tract signs, gait disturbance
- Hydrocephalus:
    - Headache, gait, cognitive changes
- Lower cranial nerve palsies (rare):
    - Hoarseness, dysphagia from CN IX–X involvement

> [!note] Vestibular symptoms  
> Often **chronic imbalance** and unsteadiness rather than dramatic vertigo, due to **central compensation**. True acute vertigo is more typical of Ménière's or vestibular neuritis.

### Imaging

#### MRI

- **Gold standard** for diagnosis and follow-up
- Typical protocol:
    - **T1 non-contrast**
    - **T2**
    - **CISS/FIESTA** (steady-state sequences)
        - Excellent for IAC / fundal extension & nerve–tumor interface
    - **Post-contrast T1** (key sequence; homogeneous or heterogeneous enhancement)
    - **DWI**:
        - Mainly to exclude epidermoid or other lesions; VS typically **does not restrict diffusion**

#### CT (Role)

- Not primary diagnostic tool (MRI preferred)
- Used for **surgical planning**:
    - **Trumpeted IAC**:
        - Bony **widening of canal** – indirect sign of vestibular schwannoma
    - **High jugular bulb**:
        - Important for **approach selection** and **IAC drilling corridor**
    - **Pneumatization pattern** of mastoid and petrous bone:
        - Guides **extent and safety of drilling** for translabyrinthine / middle fossa approaches

### Audiovestibular Work-up

> [!info] Core preoperative tests  
> These determine **hearing status**, **nerve of origin**, and help tailor **approach vs SRS vs observation**.

- **Pure Tone Audiometry (PTA)**
    - Baseline thresholds across frequencies
    - Used along with SDS to define **serviceable hearing**
- **Speech Discrimination Score (SDS)**
    - **Critical** for decision-making
    - **Poor SDS (< 50%)** → hearing preservation surgery less likely to be beneficial
        - Often favors **translabyrinthine** or **radiosurgery** over middle fossa attempts
- **Auditory Brainstem Response (ABR)**
    - Historical screening tool (now largely replaced by MRI)
    - Classically shows **prolonged wave V latency** or loss of waves
- **VNG / ENG (Videonystagmography / Electronystagmography)**
    - Assesses **caloric response**
    - Reduced vestibular function on tumor side suggests **superior vestibular nerve origin**

### Koos Classification (Summary)

Full detail in [[Koos classification of vestibular schwannomas]].

Koos classification stratifies tumors by **extrameatal extension** and **brainstem compression**:

- **Grade I** – Intracanalicular:
    - Tumor confined to **IAC** only
- **Grade II** – Small CPA component:
    - Extends into CPA but **no brainstem contact**
- **Grade III** – CPA-filling:
    - **Fills CPA**, contacting brainstem but without major distortion
- **Grade IV** – Brainstem displacement:
    - **Displaces** brainstem and adjacent cranial nerves

![[Neurosurgery/NSx  Oncology/_resources/Acoustic_Neuroma.resources/image.png|300]]

Practical use: combined with **hearing status**, **age**, and **comorbidities** to guide **observation vs SRS vs surgery**.

### Hearing Function & "Serviceable Hearing"

see [[Serviceable hearing]]

> [!tip] "50/50 Rule"  
> A commonly used definition of **serviceable hearing**:
> 
> - **PTA ≤ 50 dB**
> 
> - **Speech discrimination ≥ 50%**

- Patients meeting "serviceable hearing" criteria:
    - Consider **hearing preservation approaches** (retrosigmoid or middle fossa) or **SRS** in selected cases
- Patients with **non-serviceable hearing**:
    - Often best served by **translabyrinthine** approach if surgery chosen

### Differential Diagnosis

Main CPA/IAC differentials:

- **Meningioma**
    - Often **dural tail**, more **hyperostosis** than IAC widening
    - Can encase vessels/cranial nerves differently; may be **more vascular**
- Others (less common, but worth keeping in mind):
    - **Epidermoid cyst**
    - **Arachnoid cyst (CPA)**
    - **Metastasis**
    - **Lipoma**, **vascular loops** causing CN VIII symptoms

### Surgical Approaches

> [!summary] Choice of approach  
> Primarily based on:  
> **(1)** Tumor size/Koos grade  
> **(2)** Hearing status  
> **(3)** Anatomy (high jugular bulb, petrous pneumatization)  
> **(4)** Surgeon & center expertise

#### 1. Translabyrinthine Approach

- **Hearing sacrificed** (labyrinth is drilled)
- Advantages:
    - No cerebellar retraction
    - Direct access to IAC and CPA in one corridor
    - Good for:
        - **Non-serviceable hearing**
        - Medium–large tumors where hearing preservation is unrealistic
- Posterior limit: **sigmoid sinus**

#### 2. Retrosigmoid (Retromastoid) Approach

- **Potential to preserve hearing**
- Advantages:
    - Versatile; suitable for small to large tumors
    - Good for **Koos II–IV** depending on expertise
- Limitations:
    - **Lateral IAC/fundus** less accessible without aggressive drilling
    - Sigmoid sinus limits lateral exposure

#### 3. Middle Fossa Approach

- Primarily for:
    - **Small intracanalicular / fundal tumors** (Koos I–early II)
    - Patients with **excellent serviceable hearing**
- Advantages:
    - Best access to **lateral IAC/fundus** for hearing preservation
- Requires:
    - Good temporal bone anatomy
    - Familiarity with floor of middle fossa anatomy and **geniculate ganglion**, **GSPN**, etc.

### Management Overview (Observation, SRS, Surgery)

> [!info] Typical high-level strategy (varies by center)
> 
> - **Small, asymptomatic, elderly** → observation ± SRS
> 
> - **Younger, growing, Koos I–II with serviceable hearing** → hearing-preservation microsurgery (middle fossa/retrosigmoid) or SRS
> 
> - **Large Koos III–IV, mass effect, hydrocephalus** → microsurgery (often staged or combined with CSF diversion)

- **Observation ("wait and scan")**
    - Appropriate for small tumors, older/frail patients, or minimal symptoms
    - Serial MRI: commonly **yearly** initially, then spacing out if stable
- **Stereotactic Radiosurgery (SRS)**
    - Good tumor control in **small–moderate** lesions without major mass effect
    - Hearing preservation possible, especially in small Koos I–II
- **Microsurgical Resection**
    - Indicated for:
        - Large tumors with **brainstem compression**
        - Symptomatic mass effect
        - Patient preference or failed SRS/observation
    - Aim: **maximal safe resection**, prioritize **CN VII** ± hearing
